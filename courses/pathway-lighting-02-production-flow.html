Lighting Production Flow

Course 1 taught you how to support a lighting hang as a ground hand. This course goes deeper: how a lighting system actually moves from creative intent through advancing, load-in, rigging coordination, power, networking, focus, programming, show operation, and load-out — across corporate, arena, stadium, and outdoor festival production.

Start Lesson 1 Back to Course 1

What this course helps you understand

This is awareness-level training for new stagehands, early-career live event workers, freelance labor, local crew, and workers trying to become reliable lighting crew members on real calls. It does not certify, authorize, or qualify you to perform restricted rigging, electrical, lift, forklift, pyrotechnic, automation, fall-protection, or weather-command work — those stay with your employer, the venue, and qualified, authorized personnel.

Glossary

Terms used throughout this course. Come back to this list any time.

AddressThe starting control slot assigned to a fixture, used to match it to the console patch.
Art-NetAn IP-based entertainment-control protocol that can carry many universes across Ethernet networks.
BuskingLive improvised operation using prepared groups, palettes, effects, and playbacks — common in concerts and festivals.
CircuitAn electrical path supplying loads through appropriate protection.
CueA recorded lighting state or action, executed by an operator or triggered by show control/timecode.
DMX UniverseA set of up to 512 DMX data slots — a control-data grouping, not a fixture count.
DistroTemporary electrical distribution equipment — restricted power equipment handled by qualified personnel.
FOHFront of house — usually an audience-area operating or lighting position.
LoomA grouped bundle of cables, often preassembled, that speeds repeated truss or floor-package installation.
NodeA device that converts or routes network control data to outputs such as DMX.
PatchThe mapping between console identities and physical or network outputs — must match addresses, modes, universes, and outputs.
PointAn approved suspension location for rigging — requires qualified approval, never assumed safe by beginners.
RDMBidirectional management over compatible DMX infrastructure — helps identify or monitor fixtures, but doesn't replace paperwork.
sACNA standardized IP-based transport for entertainment-control levels, requiring correct addressing and routing.
Secondary retentionA secondary means intended to retain equipment if the primary attachment fails — part of overhead fixture safety.
TrimThe final operating height of flown equipment — access gets harder once trusses reach show trim.
VLANLogical network separation through managed switching, used to separate show-control or lighting traffic when designed and tested.

Sources & Standards

This course was built from a structured research packet that names the professional literature, standards bodies, and organizations its content draws on. Standards, codes, and editions change — verify current applicability before relying on any of these for an actual job. None of this replaces engineered plans, manufacturer instructions, or direction from qualified, authorized personnel.
