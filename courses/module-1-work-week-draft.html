What a Work Week Can Actually Look Like

Stagehand work doesn't run on a 9-to-5, and it doesn't run on a fixed weekly total either. Calls come in whatever shape the show needs. The call lengths below are ones every local hand runs into sooner or later — how many of them get stacked together in a given week is a different question, and depends heavily on your availability, your reputation, and how much of it you're actively chasing.

4-hour minimumStandard local calls (concerts, general warehouse/show work)Most agencies guarantee at least a 4-hour minimum even if the actual work wraps sooner
5-hour minimumCorporate & convention workCorporate calls tend to run a slightly higher minimum — and usually a bit higher pay rate to match

Show call: in, show & outLocal crew booked for the full production — load-in, the show itself, and load-outOne continuous shift, often 18–20 hours — you stay clocked in the entire time, including through the show. More common once you're an established, trusted hand than on your first few calls

That's called a show call, and it's mostly a concert, arena, and festival thing. You're booked for the in, the show, and the out as one continuous shift — not three separate calls. You don't leave and come back: you stay clocked in from load-in through the show itself and all the way through load-out, until the show wraps and the production drives off. Corporate work usually doesn't run this way — it tends toward more predictable, single-shift days.

Corporate crews vs. concert & arena crews

Local crews often specialize, and it's not something you pick call by call — it's more about which crew or agency you connect with. Some local crews work almost entirely corporate: trade shows, conferences, general sessions. That work tends to run a higher starting rate with steadier, more predictable hours. Other local crews focus on arenas and stadiums — concerts and live music — which usually starts at a bit lower pay but is the more visible, more sought-after side of the work. Neither is the "wrong" path; they're just different tracks with different tradeoffs.

A "week" isn't five similar days

Local crew schedules don't look like a typical job. Calls cluster — tight during a multi-day festival run — and can go quiet with no calls at all on other days.

The two patterns below are the far end, not the starting point

Most local crew — especially new hands — won't see this pace right away. What's below is closer to what an established, sought-after hand with wide-open availability takes on: someone saying yes to as much work, and as much travel, as they can get. It's worth knowing about early, because it's what saying yes to more work can eventually turn into — not something to expect on your first few calls.

Pattern: Two separate bookings, same day
Booking 1 — Load-in8:00 AM – 1:00 PM
(gap — roughly 5 hours)—
Booking 2 — Load-out6:30 PM – 11:30 PM

These can be for the same production or two unrelated ones. Don't assume a gap in your day means you're done — confirm exactly what you're booked for before you make other plans.

Pattern: Back-to-back with a short turnaround
Load-out endsAround 3:30–4:00 AM
Next load-in startsAround 6:00–6:30 AM, same festival

Common on multi-day festival runs. Sleep, food, and travel all have to fit in that short window — plan for it before you accept back-to-back calls, not after.

Track it yourself

Nobody's going to hand you a predictable weekly schedule — that's normal for this work, not a red flag about a specific employer or agency. Whatever pace you're working at, confirm call times and whether a call is a single booking or a bundled one, keep your own calendar, and build in real recovery time around back-to-back or overnight calls instead of assuming you'll bounce back.
