Lighting Pathway · Course 1

Supporting a Lighting Hang

This is where the Lighting pathway begins — not by teaching you to be a lighting technician, but by teaching you to be genuinely useful to one. Real support work, and the exact line between helping and adjusting.

What You Can Help With What Stays Off-Limits Mistakes & Communication

What You Can Help With, Under Direction

A ground hand genuinely helps build a lighting rig — this isn't just standing nearby and passing things along.

Real support work includes assembling truss, un-casing and re-casing fixtures, running and dressing cable, passing tools to someone working overhead, and staying alert while truss or battens move to trim. When you carry a fixture, use its actual handle — not the lens, the yoke lock, or its cable. When you coil cable, use the method your crew actually runs; over-under is common on a lot of entertainment cable, but don't assume every cable or every crew handles it the same way.

Ground support during a trim — when truss or a batten moves from working height to its final position — is real work too. You're not operating the hoist or fly system. You're watching for obvious snags or cable conflicts, staying clear of the moving system, and listening for the designated caller.

What Stays Off-Limits Without Direction

This is where the line is strict, and where most beginner mistakes actually happen.

If you didn't set it, don't "fix" it

A fixture that looks crooked might be exactly where the lighting department wants it. Focus, tilt, shutters, barn doors, and gel or color that's already installed are the lighting department's call, not yours — even if straightening it seems harmless. Same goes for DMX addressing, patch, console settings, and feeder/electrical connections. If something looks wrong, ask the lighting lead instead of adjusting it yourself.

Electrical distribution and feeder connections are especially strict — that stays with the Head Electrician or whoever's qualified and assigned, full stop. You may be asked to move or stage cable and power equipment; you don't energize, reconfigure, or troubleshoot it.

Common Mistakes, and How Lighting Crews Actually Communicate

A few habits separate a hand who speeds up a hang from one who slows it down.

The most common ways a hand damages gear or slows a hang: grabbing a fixture by the lens or yoke lock instead of its handle, touching a lamp's glass with bare skin (contamination can damage some lamps), tangled or incorrect cable coiling, and "correcting" a fixture or cable that was already placed on purpose.

There's no single nationwide hand-signal system for entertainment rigging — crews and venues run their own. What is universal: STOP always means stop. Know who's calling the move before it starts, keep clear of the moving system, and if communication breaks down, stop and re-establish it before anything continues.

PPE for lighting support is site- and task-specific, but expect hard hats where overhead work requires them, work gloves, protective footwear, and hearing or eye protection where the task or site calls for it. Follow whatever the call's hazard assessment requires rather than assuming one kit covers every venue.

Quick Knowledge Check

Click a question to check your answer.

You're carrying a fixture. What should you hold it by?

The fixture's actual handle — never the lens, the yoke lock, or its cable.

A fixture looks crooked. Should you straighten it?

No — it may be exactly where the lighting department focused it. Ask the lighting lead instead of adjusting it yourself.

Is there one universal hand-signal system used for entertainment rigging everywhere?

No — crews and venues run their own. What's universal is that STOP always means stop, and you re-establish communication before continuing if it breaks down.

Who connects and energizes temporary electrical/feeder power for the lighting rig?

The Head Electrician or another qualified, assigned person — not a general ground hand, even if you're asked to move or stage the equipment.

Truss is moving to trim overhead. What's your job?

Listen for the designated caller, stay clear of the moving system, watch for obvious snags or cable conflicts and report them — not operate the hoist or fly system.

Key Takeaways

  • Real lighting support: truss assembly, cable running/dressing, fixture handling, staying alert during a trim — all under direction.
  • Focus, addressing, patch, console settings, and electrical connections stay with the lighting department. If it looks wrong, ask before you touch it.
  • There's no universal hand-signal system for entertainment rigging — only STOP is a safe assumption everywhere.
  • Common mistakes are handling errors, not knowledge gaps: wrong grip points, poor cable coiling, "fixing" something already set.
