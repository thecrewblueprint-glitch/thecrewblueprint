Module 8

Tools, Gear, and Handling Basics

Not every tool on a jobsite is meant for every person to use. This lesson is about recognizing gear, handling it safely, and understanding the hazards underneath it — not becoming an expert with any of it overnight.

Common Tools Cases & Cable Tape & Vocabulary Lifting & Hazards Staying Organized

Common Stagehand Tools

Recognition first, permission second — carrying a tool doesn't mean every task is yours to do.

You'll see (and likely carry) an adjustable wrench, a multitool, a flashlight or headlamp, work gloves, a tape measure, and a marker — a radio is more of a lead's tool, not something you'll be carrying early on. A c-wrench or adjustable wrench comes up around bolts, clamps, and hardware — but tightening, loosening, or removing hardware is still something you do only when assigned, not just because you're holding the right tool. A multitool is genuinely useful for small tasks, but it's not a substitute for the actual tool a department needs for their equipment.

For the full bring-list and where to find everything cheap, see Gear & Tools in the Resource Hub.

Road Case Parts and Cable Coiling

Two things you'll handle on nearly every call, and two things that deserve more care than they usually get.

Road cases have parts worth knowing: lids, latches, hinges, handles, casters, brakes, labels, skids, and corners. Cases look similar to each other, but they're not interchangeable — don't open, stack, or tip one without direction, and never assume a case is empty or harmless just because it's light.

Cable is everywhere — audio, power, feeder, data, video, speaker, intercom. It can look identical regardless of what it's carrying, and some of it may still be active. Only coil, pull, move, or disconnect cable under direction from the department it belongs to — it comes up on every single call, so it's worth keeping front of mind.

Tape Basics and Production Equipment Vocabulary

Not all tape is the same, and a lot of gear names get thrown around assuming you already know what they mean.

TapeCommon Use
Gaff tapeThe production standard — strong, cloth-based, removes cleanly
Electrical tapeCable ends, minor electrical work under direction
Spike tapeMarking floor positions for gear or performers
Console/labeling tapeMarking settings or positions on equipment

You'll also hear equipment named constantly without explanation: pipe and drape, decks, risers, truss, chain motors, carts, cable ramps, soft goods. Some of these are simple to move under direction. Others are part of a structural, overhead, or electrical system — the name alone doesn't tell you which. When in doubt, ask before you move it.

Lifting, Team Carry, and Hazard Points

Strength alone isn't enough. Coordination and hazard awareness are what actually keep a lift safe.

Safe handling means knowing when to ask for help, checking the path before you move, and communicating before you lift — not just picking something up because it looks manageable. Lift only what you can control, avoid twisting while carrying, and watch fingers, feet, corners, and edges the whole way. A team carry should be coordinated by one person calling the movement, moving at the group's pace, and setting the load down together.

Three hazard words worth knowing

Pinch point — where a body part can get caught between two objects. Crush point — where a person can be trapped or crushed by moving, falling, or shifting equipment. Stored energy — energy held inside an object that can release suddenly: tension, compression, gravity, springs, a suspended load, or a rolling case picking up speed.

Keeping Gear Organized

Organization isn't neatness for its own sake — it's a safety and speed habit.

Disorganized gear blocks exits, creates trip hazards, slows departments down, sends cases to the wrong place, and turns load-out into chaos. You help by following staging directions, reading labels, keeping paths clear, and checking back in instead of leaving gear wherever you finished with it.

Quick Knowledge Check

Click a question to check your answer.

You have a multitool. Does that mean you can loosen hardware you find on a case?

No — tightening, loosening, or removing hardware is something you do when assigned, not just because you're holding the right tool.

Can you assume a cable lying loose is disconnected and safe to move?

No — cable can look identical regardless of what it carries or whether it's still active. Only move cable under direction from the department it belongs to.

What's the difference between a pinch point and stored energy?

A pinch point is where a body part can get caught between two objects. Stored energy is energy held inside an object or system that can release suddenly, like a rolling case gaining speed or a suspended load.

What should happen before you lift something with a partner?

Check the path, communicate before lifting, and have one person call the movement so everyone moves together.

Why does gear organization matter beyond just looking tidy?

Disorganized gear blocks exits, creates trip hazards, slows departments, and sends cases to the wrong place — it's a safety and speed issue, not just appearance.

Key Takeaways

  • Carrying a tool doesn't authorize you to use it on anything you'd like — permission still comes from the task assignment, not the tool in your hand.
  • Cable and road cases look interchangeable but aren't — move either only under direction from the department they belong to.
  • Gear vocabulary (pipe and drape, decks, truss, motors) doesn't tell you what's safe to move on its own — ask when unsure.
  • Pinch points, crush points, and stored energy are the three hazard concepts underneath most handling injuries — know what each one looks like.
  • Keeping gear organized is a safety habit, not tidiness — it prevents blocked paths, trip hazards, and load-out chaos.
