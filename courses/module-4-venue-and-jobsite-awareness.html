Module 4

Venue and Jobsite Awareness

This module isn't about any one skill — it's about not becoming a hazard yourself once you're actually on-site. Where to stand, who to find, what's off-limits, and what to leave alone.

First 15 Minutes Finding Your Lead Restricted Areas Overhead & Cable Clear Paths

The First 15 Minutes on Site

How you spend your first few minutes on a call sets the tone for the rest of the day.

Don't walk onto a jobsite and immediately start grabbing gear, opening cases, or joining whichever group looks busiest. Your first priority is checking in, figuring out who's in charge, understanding where you're supposed to report, and watching how people and equipment are already moving before you jump into it.

A hand who slows down for those first few minutes to get oriented isn't being slow — they're doing the one thing that actually sets up a safe, smooth day.

Where "checking in" actually happens

It could be a security entrance, loading dock, production office, crew check-in table, backstage door, or a specific meeting spot — it varies by call. Confirm which one applies before you leave the house; don't guess when you arrive.

Finding Your Lead, and Reading the Venue

A jobsite has real structure, even when it doesn't look like it from the outside.

Not everyone on-site can hand you work. Your lead or department head is the one responsible for direction, answering questions, and keeping the crew aligned — know who that is before you start. If you're not sure, ask at check-in or ask the nearest clearly identified supervisor. Don't just start following whoever looks busy or confident.

You'll also hear location shorthand constantly. It's worth knowing the basics before someone tells you to head to one and you have no idea where that is:

TermWhat It Means
StageThe performance area itself
DockWhere trucks load and unload
Truck / RampThe trailer gear travels in, and the incline used to move it in or out
BackstageThe area behind or around the stage, out of audience view
BarricadeThe physical barrier separating the crowd from the stage or work areas

These are standard industry terms — they don't change from venue to venue. What varies is the physical layout: where the dock actually sits, how far backstage is from the stage itself. Confirm the layout with your lead; don't assume the terms mean something different.

Stage directions and house directions sound almost identical, so it's easy to hear one and assume it's the other. Listen carefully — your physical position relative to the stage is what actually decides which direction is being called.

Stage directions come from the performer facing the audience. House directions come from the audience facing the stage. Because they're facing each other, the two mirror: stage left is the same physical side as house right.

TermWhat It Means
Stage LeftThe performer's left, facing the audience
Stage RightThe performer's right, facing the audience
House LeftThe audience's left, facing the stage — the same physical side as stage right
House RightThe audience's right, facing the stage — the same physical side as stage left
DownstageToward the audience, the front of the stage
UpstageAway from the audience, the back of the stage
Center StageThe middle of the stage

Get one of these backwards in front of the wrong person and it's an easy way to look like you don't know what you're doing — worth getting straight before your first call, not during it.

FOH isn't a direction at all — it stands for Front of House, a physical zone out in the crowd where the mix and production control positions sit. Being told to "head to FOH" means leaving the stage area entirely and going out into the audience space. Stage and house directions tell you which side you're on; FOH tells you which zone of the venue you're in — a different kind of term entirely, and easy to conflate when you're new.

Restricted Areas and Moving Equipment

Some areas are off-limits, and some equipment isn't yours to touch. Neither is optional.

Restricted areas exist to protect people, performers, gear, and the production — not to make your day harder. They might be marked with signs, tape, cones, a closed door, security, or just a verbal instruction, and some aren't obviously marked at all. Never assume you're allowed somewhere just because a door happens to be open or you saw someone else walk through — ask if you're unsure.

The same caution applies to forklifts, carts, pallet jacks, pushers, dollies, and rolling racks. You don't need to operate any of it unless you're trained, authorized, and assigned to. Your job is staying alert: keep your distance, stay out of blind spots, follow marked paths, and never ride equipment unless it's built and authorized for that. Every one of these, however small it looks, creates real struck-by, crush, pinch, and foot hazards.

Overhead Hazards and Cable Paths

A lot happens above your head and under your feet on a live event site. Both deserve real attention.

Rigging, truss, motors, fixtures, speakers, video walls, scenic pieces, and chain hoists all move overhead on a working call. This is awareness only — it isn't teaching you to rig, fly equipment, or inspect anything overhead. The rule for beginners is simple: stay clear of overhead work, and never stand under a suspended load.

Cable paths carry audio, lighting, video, data, power, and control lines. Don't unplug, pull, coil, tape, or move a cable unless the department running it tells you to. Trip hazards aren't just cable, either — tape balls, trash, loose case lids, tools, straps, uneven ground, ramps, dock plates, and bad lighting all count. Keeping your area clean isn't tidiness for its own sake; it's what keeps the whole crew moving safely.

Emergency Exits and Keeping Paths Clear

Jobsites need clear paths — for workers, for emergency response, and sometimes for an audience.

Never stage gear in front of an emergency exit, fire extinguisher, fire lane, electrical panel, stairway, ramp, dock door, or main travel path unless a lead specifically tells you to and it still complies with site rules. Exact requirements vary by venue and local authority, so follow whatever's marked on-site — and if you spot a blocked path, report it to your lead instead of just working around it.

Quick Knowledge Check

Click a question to check your answer.

What should you do in your first few minutes on a new call?

Check in, figure out who's in charge, find where you're supposed to report, and observe how people and equipment are moving before jumping in.

You don't know who your lead is. What do you do?

Ask at check-in or ask the nearest clearly identified supervisor — don't just start following whoever looks busy.

A lead tells you to head to stage left. Whose left is that — yours, or the performer's?

The performer's left, while facing the audience — that's the same physical side as house right. Stage directions are always defined from the performer's point of view, not yours.

How do stage directions and house directions relate to each other?

They mirror, because they come from opposite points of view — the performer faces the audience, the audience faces the performer. Stage left is the same physical side as house right, and stage right is the same physical side as house left.

What's the difference between a stage/house direction and FOH?

Stage and house directions (stage left/right, house left/right, upstage/downstage) describe which side you're on. FOH (Front of House) is a physical zone out in the audience where mix and production positions sit — a completely different kind of term.

A door to a restricted-looking area is open. Are you allowed in?

Not automatically — an open door or another worker walking through isn't permission. Ask if you're unsure.

You're not trained on the forklift moving nearby. What's your job?

Stay alert, keep distance, stay out of blind spots, follow marked paths, and never ride equipment unless it's authorized for that.

A cable is in your way. Can you move it?

No — don't unplug, pull, coil, or move a cable unless the department running it tells you to.

Key Takeaways

  • Your first few minutes on-site are for getting oriented, not grabbing gear — check in, find who's in charge, and watch before you act.
  • Know who your lead is before you start working. Learn basic venue terms, and get stage and house directions straight — they mirror each other, and FOH is a different kind of term entirely: a zone out in the audience, not a direction.
  • Restricted areas and unfamiliar equipment aren't yours to enter or operate without direction — ask instead of assuming.
  • Stay clear of overhead work and never stand under a suspended load. Don't touch a cable path unless the department running it tells you to.
  • Never block an emergency exit, fire lane, or main travel path — and report a blocked one instead of working around it.
