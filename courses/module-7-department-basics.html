Module 7

Department Basics

You don't need to become an expert in every department right away. You need to know what each one does, recognize when specialized work is happening, and help without getting in the way.

Audio, Lighting & Video Staging & Rigging Backline & Props Why You Don't Touch It How to Help

Audio, Lighting, and Video Basics

Three departments, three sets of expensive, sensitive gear, one rule that applies to all of them: support, don't touch.

Audio handles everything that makes sound reach the audience and crew clearly — speakers, microphones, consoles, monitor systems, signal cable, comms. Gear can be heavy, fragile, and already tuned or positioned for a reason. You can push cases, move speaker carts when directed, and help with cable paths under supervision — you don't plug in, unplug, coil, patch, or power anything on your own.

Lighting covers fixtures, truss, cable, power, and control systems that shape what the audience sees. Video covers screens, cameras, projectors, LED walls, and playback systems — often the most fragile and expensive gear on the call. Both follow the same rule as audio: recognize it, help move it when told to, and leave the connecting and adjusting to the department running it.

Staging, Carpentry, and Rigging Awareness

One of these you can help build under direction. The other, you stay clear of entirely.

Staging and carpentry crews work with decks, risers, platforms, scenic pieces, stairs, railings, and set pieces — often heavy, physical work. You can help move and place these items under direction, but structures only get assembled, adjusted, or modified by the crew running that build.

Rigging is different in kind, not just degree. It involves lifting, suspending, or securing equipment above the stage or audience — truss, motors, points, steel, spansets, shackles, flown systems. This is specialized work requiring real training and calculation. Your relationship to it is the same as Module 1 already established: stay clear of overhead work, never stand under a suspended load, and respect exclusion zones without needing to be told twice.

Backline, Props, Wardrobe, and Production Support

Not all important gear is big or technical. Some of it is small, personal, and just as easy to damage.

Backline is musician equipment — drum kits, amps, keyboards, pedals, stands, instrument cases. It's often personal to a specific performer, not just expensive. Props are objects used in the performance itself; wardrobe covers clothing, costumes, and performer apparel; production support covers the smaller stuff — tables, chairs, signage. None of it looks like a safety hazard the way a suspended truss does, but it still requires permission before you touch it — it's often scheduled, assigned, or irreplaceable in a way that isn't obvious just by looking at it.

Why You Don't Touch Department Gear Without Direction

This is the rule underneath every department above. Worth saying directly, once.

Gear may be placed intentionally, connected to an active system, waiting for inspection, or staged for a specific sequence you can't see from the outside. Moving it without direction can create confusion, delay the crew, damage equipment, or create a safety problem — even when your intention was to help. Don't move, connect, disconnect, adjust, open, or operate department gear unless you're trained, authorized, and specifically told to.

How to Help Without Getting in the Way

You can be genuinely useful around a department without knowing a single technical detail about what they do.

Check in, listen, stay nearby but out of the work zone, ask before touching anything, keep paths clear, and complete whatever you were actually assigned. Getting in the way usually happens the opposite way: wandering, standing in a work zone, blocking a path, or touching gear because it looked like it needed moving. Being available and out of the way is its own kind of useful — it doesn't require technical knowledge, just attention.

Quick Knowledge Check

Click a question to check your answer.

You're asked to help audio move some cases. Can you plug in a cable while you're there?

No — you can push cases and help with cable paths under direction, but plugging in, unplugging, or adjusting audio gear requires direction from the audio crew.

What's the difference between how you treat staging/carpentry work vs. rigging?

You can help move and place staging items under direction. Rigging is specialized overhead work — stay clear entirely, never stand under a suspended load.

Why can't you assume backline or props gear is safe to move just because it looks unattended?

It may be personal to a performer, scheduled, or show-critical in a way that isn't obvious — ask before touching it, same as any other department's gear.

What's the underlying reason you don't touch department gear without direction?

It may be placed intentionally, connected to an active system, or staged for a sequence you can't see — moving it can create safety problems or delays even with good intentions.

Name one way to be useful around a department without technical knowledge.

Any of: check in, listen, stay nearby but out of the work zone, ask before touching anything, keep paths clear, complete your assigned task.

Key Takeaways

  • Audio, lighting, and video all follow the same rule: help move gear under direction, never connect/adjust/operate it yourself.
  • Staging and carpentry allow beginner support under direction; rigging is specialized overhead work you stay clear of entirely.
  • Backline, props, and wardrobe aren't safety hazards the way rigging is, but still require permission — they're often personal or scheduled in ways that aren't visible.
  • Department gear may be intentionally placed or connected to an active system — never move, connect, disconnect, adjust, or operate it without direction.
  • You can be genuinely useful without technical knowledge: check in, stay out of the work zone, ask before touching anything, keep paths clear.
