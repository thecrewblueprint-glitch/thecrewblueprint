# SFS-U02 — Cart / Dolly / Hand-Truck Movement

**Prepared:** 2026-08-31  
**Status:** Source packet complete enough for bounded course drafting; practitioner/equipment review remains required  
**Domain:** `D-FLD` Stagehand Field Skills  
**Recommended tier:** `T2` Field Skill  
**Authority:** assigned general material movement only; no powered industrial-truck, MEWP, motorized tug, or specialist-equipment operation authority

## 1. Research question

What can The Crew Blueprint responsibly teach a new stagehand about moving production equipment on non-powered carts, dollies, hand trucks, and flatbed carts without inventing universal load limits or turning general material movement into equipment authorization?

## 2. Executive finding

This belongs in the universal Stagehand Core Field Skills catalog.

The transferable learner outcome is not “know every cart.” It is:

> **Recognize the assigned non-powered handling aid, verify that the load and route remain controllable, preserve sightlines and clearances, move at a controlled pace, stop before changed conditions, and park/handoff the load without creating a new hazard.**

The course must not teach one universal maximum load, one universal crew count, or one universal push/pull rule detached from the actual equipment, route, load, manufacturer limit, employer procedure, and responsible lead.

## 3. Evidence base

### OSHA — Pushing, Pulling and Carrying

OSHA recommends using transport devices such as hand trucks and pallet jacks to reduce manual handling demand. Its materials-handling guidance also emphasizes usable handles, clear sightlines, and avoiding loads stacked so high that the worker must bend or lean around them to see.

Source: OSHA eTool — Materials Handling: Pushing, Pulling and Carrying  
https://www.osha.gov/etools/electrical-contractors/materials-handling/pushing

### OSHA — Carts, Hand Trucks, Pallet Jacks and Lift Tables

OSHA's manual-materials-handling material identifies wheeled devices as useful transport aids and emphasizes balanced loads, maintaining visibility, respecting manufacturer weight restrictions, and using equipment suited to the floor/work environment.

Source: OSHA — Manual Materials Handling: Carts, Hand Trucks, Pallet Jacks and Lift Tables  
https://www.osha.gov/Publications/OSHA3213.pdf

### NIOSH — Ergonomic risk

NIOSH identifies forceful pushing/pulling, awkward posture, carrying, twisting, and poor equipment condition as material-handling risk factors. Current NIOSH guidance specifically notes that excessive force while pushing a cart can contribute to musculoskeletal risk and recommends correcting broken or poorly functioning cart equipment.

Sources:

- NIOSH — Identify Risk Factors  
  https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/ergonomics/ergo-programs/risk-factors.html
- NIOSH — About Ergonomics and Work-Related Musculoskeletal Disorders  
  https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/ergonomics/about/index.html
- NIOSH — Ergonomic Guidelines for Manual Material Handling, DHHS 2007-131  
  https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/docs/2007-131/pdfs/2007-131.pdf

### Existing Crew Blueprint stagehand evidence

Package 15 and Package 18 already establish that moving road cases and production equipment, navigating docks/ramps, protecting clear paths, and recognizing powered-equipment boundaries are normal general-stagehand concerns. Package 52 separately establishes a bounded road-case movement model.

This packet extends that evidence to **general non-powered wheeled handling aids** rather than treating every wheeled object as a road case.

## 4. Equipment families the learner should recognize

At awareness level, distinguish:

- four-wheel flatbed/platform cart;
- two-wheel hand truck;
- furniture/equipment dolly;
- department-specific rolling rack/cart;
- cable caddy/cart;
- road case acting as its own wheeled transport platform;
- pallet jack or powered/motorized device as a separate equipment class controlled by employer/site training and authorization.

The course should not claim that terminology is identical at every employer or union local.

## 5. Transferable pre-move checks

Before moving an assigned non-powered cart/dolly, the course may teach the learner to confirm:

1. **Assignment** — what is moving, where it is going, and who owns the move.
2. **Equipment condition** — obvious wheel/caster, handle, platform, brake/lock, strap/restraint, or frame damage that should be reported.
3. **Load condition** — the load is visibly stable enough for the assigned move and does not obstruct the required sightline.
4. **Route** — doors, thresholds, ramps/slopes, turns, floor changes, public/crew traffic, cables, dock edges, and other changed conditions are identified before movement.
5. **People** — the responsible lead decides whether the move needs additional crew, a spotter/guide, another handling aid, or a different method.

The learner does not invent a load plan or override manufacturer/employer restrictions.

## 6. Transferable movement principles

The course can safely teach the following high-level principles:

- Keep the equipment under controlled movement rather than allowing momentum to determine the path.
- Preserve a usable sightline. If the load blocks the path view, stop and get a different plan rather than moving blind.
- Keep hands, feet, ankles, and other people out of wheel, pinch, crush, wall, doorway, and load-shift zones.
- Slow or stop before a turn, threshold, ramp/slope, doorway, surface change, congested area, dock/trailer interface, or other changed condition.
- Follow the designated caller/lead when multiple people are involved.
- Stop if a wheel/caster binds, the load shifts, the route becomes blocked, communication is lost, or the effort needed changes unexpectedly.
- Park only in the assigned area without blocking egress, fire/life-safety equipment, doors, aisles, department access, or active production routes.

These are curriculum principles, not a substitute for the exact cart manufacturer's instructions or site procedure.

## 7. Push versus pull — qualification required

OSHA ergonomics guidance generally prefers pushing to pulling when conditions permit because pushing can reduce some ergonomic demands and avoids a cart striking the worker's shins/ankles while being pulled.

However, Crew Blueprint should **not** reduce this to “always push.” Real production routes can involve tight corners, doorways, ramps, unusual cart geometry, spotter arrangements, controlled repositioning, or manufacturer/site procedures that change the method.

Recommended course wording:

> **Use the direction and method that keeps the assigned load controlled and visible under the responsible lead's plan. Pushing is often ergonomically preferable when the cart, route, and sightline support it; it is not permission to ignore equipment- or site-specific handling rules.**

## 8. Visibility and stack height

OSHA materials-handling guidance supports a strong learner-facing rule:

> **Do not continue a move when the load prevents you from seeing the route you are responsible for watching.**

The course should avoid inventing a universal numeric stack-height limit. The actual limit depends on the equipment, materials, route, employer, and manufacturer constraints.

## 9. Wheels, casters, brakes, and resistance

A learner may be taught to recognize and report:

- visibly damaged or missing wheels/casters;
- wheels that bind or fail to rotate/track normally;
- damaged handles/platforms/frames;
- brake/lock hardware that is present but visibly damaged or fails to hold during a controlled pre-move check;
- unstable, leaning, shifting, or poorly contained loads;
- unexpected resistance or effort;
- floor/route conditions incompatible with safe control.

The learner should **not** repair caster/brake hardware or improvise a restraint as part of this general Field Skill.

## 10. Route-specific stop conditions

Stop and ask for a different plan when:

- the load blocks the required view;
- the equipment or load is visibly damaged/unstable;
- the route includes a slope/ramp/threshold/doorway the crew cannot control confidently;
- the cart requires excessive or unexpected force;
- the route intersects powered equipment, vehicles, active forklifts, or moving machinery without a clear traffic plan;
- the load begins to shift;
- people enter the intended travel path;
- the assigned destination is blocked or unsafe;
- communication with the lead/partner/spotter breaks down;
- the equipment turns out to be powered or otherwise outside the learner's authorization.

## 11. What good observed practice looks like

A controlled practical observation can record whether the learner:

- identifies the assigned equipment and destination;
- performs a simple visible condition check;
- identifies route constraints before movement;
- preserves useful sightlines;
- moves without uncontrolled speed or abrupt impacts;
- maintains hand/foot clearance from obvious pinch/crush zones;
- stops before changed conditions and re-establishes the plan;
- parks/handoffs the load in the designated location;
- reports rather than improvises when equipment, route, or load conditions change.

## 12. Practice record

Record:

- `course_id`: `C-FLD-CART-DOLLY-MOVEMENT`
- handling-aid type/model if identifiable;
- load description;
- route/environment;
- major route features;
- observer and role;
- result: pass / rework / stop;
- comments/variation;
- date.

Observed practice is evidence of that observed task under those conditions. It is not standing authorization for powered equipment, every cart/load, or every route.

## 13. Visual requirements

A user-friendly course should include reviewed visuals for:

1. common non-powered cart/dolly families;
2. clear-versus-blocked sightline examples;
3. wheel/pinch/crush zones;
4. route planning around doors, thresholds and turns;
5. stable versus visibly unstable load examples;
6. correct designated parking/handoff versus blocked egress/work path;
7. a stop-condition decision diagram.

Avoid depicting an exact load limit, crew count, or product-specific method unless sourced to that exact equipment/procedure.

## 14. Matrix handoff

Suggested high-priority claims:

- `CL-FLD-CART-DOLLY-0001` — transport aids can reduce manual-handling demands.
- `CL-FLD-CART-DOLLY-0002` — forceful pushing/pulling and poor posture are ergonomic risk factors.
- `CL-FLD-CART-DOLLY-0003` — route visibility should not be obstructed by the load.
- `CL-FLD-CART-DOLLY-0004` — manufacturer load/equipment limits and employer/site procedure control the actual move.
- `CL-FLD-CART-DOLLY-0005` — damaged or poorly functioning wheels/carts should be reported rather than simply forced through the move.
- `CL-FLD-CART-DOLLY-0006` — course observation does not authorize powered-equipment operation.

## 15. Curriculum recommendation

**Build as a Core Field Skill.**

Place it immediately after road-case/team-movement instruction and before more specialized cable-route or staging tasks.

The course should be short, visual, scenario-based, and designed around **control + sightline + route + stop conditions**, not cart trivia.
