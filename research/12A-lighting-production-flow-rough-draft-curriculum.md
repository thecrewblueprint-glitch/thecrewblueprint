## 1. Module Overview

**Module Topic:** Lighting production flow for corporate, arena, stadium, and outdoor festival production.

**Intended Learner Level:** Awareness-level training for new stagehands, early-career live event workers, freelance labor, local crew, and workers trying to become reliable lighting crew members on real calls.

**Purpose:** Help learners understand how lighting systems move from creative intent and advance work into engineering, load-in, rigging coordination, power, network/control, focus, programming, show operation, load-out, and documentation.

**Core Framing:** Large-scale lighting production is a controlled chain of translations: creative intent becomes a technical design; the design becomes a documented system; the system is installed, tested, programmed, operated, and safely removed.

| Area | Draft Writer Identification |
|---|---|
| Key concepts | Production lifecycle; controlled documentation; department coordination; power and data layers; rigging interface; focus and checkout; programming/rehearsal; show operation; load-out planning; environment-specific differences. |
| Safety-sensitive claims | Temporary power, rigging, work at height, lift operation, weather decisions, fall protection, feeder connections, and overhead work require qualified personnel, site policy, and supervision. This course is awareness-level only. |
| Operational workflows | Advance -> engineering/CAD/shop prep -> site establishment -> rig assembly -> power/control commissioning -> focus/checkout -> programming/rehearsal -> show operation -> load-out/as-built documentation. |
| Terminology | DMX, RDM, sACN, Art-Net, universe, node, patch, address, distro, feeder, truss, point, trim, FOH, loom, palette, cue, tracking, mark cue, VLAN. |
| Real-world scenarios | Corporate ballroom client changes; arena roof schedule; stadium distance and redundancy; outdoor festival weather and changeovers; patch/address mismatch; load-out fatigue. |
| Weak or missing research areas | No site-specific code editions, engineered weather thresholds, manufacturer procedures, jurisdictional labor rules, feeder connection sequence, or formal certification pathway. These should remain out of scope unless later sourced and reviewed. |
| Contradictions or ambiguity | Packet distinguishes established practices from site-specific variables. It does not prescribe universal electrical/rigging procedures because safe requirements vary by system, jurisdiction, employer, venue, and qualified authority. |

**Important Scope Statement:** This module does not certify, authorize, or qualify a learner to perform restricted rigging, electrical, lift, forklift, pyrotechnic, automation, fall-protection, or weather-command decisions. Learners must follow site policy, employer training, venue rules, manufacturer instructions, and directions from qualified leads.

## 2. Lesson Drafts

### Lesson 1: The Lighting Production Lifecycle

**In This Lesson You Will Learn**
- Recognize the full path from creative intent to load-out.
- Explain why the console is only one part of the lighting system.
- Identify the main phases of planning, installation, testing, operation, and removal.
- Understand why standardized process matters even when every venue is different.

**Short Answer:** Lighting production flow is the organized process that turns a visual idea into a working, documented, safe, and repeatable system. The basic chain is creative intent, technical design, documentation, installation, testing, programming, live operation, and load-out. New crew are most useful when they understand where their task fits in that chain.

**What This Looks Like on a Real Call:** On a corporate ballroom load-in, the lighting crew may start with cases, truss, fixtures, cable, and a plot. By show time, that equipment must become front light, scenic color, camera-friendly looks, cue lists, and a clean room for presenters. In an arena, the same concept scales into touring trusses, roof points, local labor, console files, and daily repeatability. On a festival, the workflow must also support short artist changeovers and guest lighting packages.

**Beginner Mistakes:**
- Thinking the lighting console is the whole system. The console depends on power, data, patching, addressing, networking, fixtures, paperwork, and crew execution.
- Ignoring the production critical path. Overhead work, power, stage release, and rehearsal access usually control the schedule.
- Treating paperwork as office work only. Documentation is how the crew knows what was intended, what changed, and what must be tested.
- Assuming every venue works like the last one. The process may be consistent, but roof points, access, power, labor, and rules change.

**Professional Habit:** Before starting a task, ask: What phase are we in, who owns this decision, and what does this task affect next? Useful crew members protect the flow of work instead of only completing isolated tasks.

**Safety Notes:** Do not perform work you have not been trained or authorized to do. Rigging, temporary power, lift operation, fall-protection work, and weather decisions must be performed or supervised by competent, appropriately qualified personnel. Production pressure never justifies bypassing inspection, exclusion zones, protective devices, engineered limits, or de-energization procedures.

**Reflection Questions:**
1. Where does your assigned task fit in the larger lighting production chain?
2. What could happen if the crew skips documentation during a fast load-in?
3. Why does a standardized process still need site-specific adaptation?

**Key Takeaway:** The lighting rig is not built by the console; it is built by a coordinated workflow.

### Lesson 2: Advancing, Documentation, and Shop Preparation

**In This Lesson You Will Learn**
- Identify what gets confirmed during advance work.
- Recognize the core documents used by a lighting department.
- Explain why pre-addressing, labeling, testing, and console preparation reduce jobsite risk.
- Understand why one controlled record of changes matters.

**Short Answer:** Advancing is the pre-production process of turning assumptions into confirmed information. Documentation turns the plan into instructions the crew can build, test, and revise. Shop preparation makes the load-in faster and safer by checking equipment before the venue clock starts.

**What This Looks Like on a Real Call:** Before a stadium build, the team may confirm truck routes, dock access, floor loading, roof capacity, approved point loads, generator needs, FOH location, cable paths, lift access, fire lanes, curfews, and inspection requirements. In the shop, fixtures may be addressed, labeled, mode-checked, firmware-checked, tested, cased by zone, and matched to paperwork.

**Beginner Mistakes:**
- Waiting until site to discover missing information. Unconfirmed roof, power, door size, or cable route details can stop the build.
- Using old paperwork after field changes. A stale patch sheet can waste hours during troubleshooting.
- Labeling only one end of a cable. Both ends need to be traceable when the rig is built, flown, or packed.
- Assuming RDM or network discovery replaces physical labels and inspection. Digital tools help, but they do not replace controlled paperwork.

**Professional Habit:** Update paperwork during the build, not after memory fades. When a fixture, address, universe, cable route, or substitution changes, make sure the department lead knows and the controlled record is updated.

**Safety Notes:** Documentation must not be treated as proof that a system is safe by itself. Rigging plans, power schedules, and point plans may require engineering, qualified inspection, venue approval, licensed personnel, or local authority review. Follow the site process before acting on technical documents.

**Reflection Questions:**
1. What information would you want before loading a truck for a lighting rig?
2. Why can one wrong fixture mode cause confusion during checkout?
3. Who should be told when a field substitution changes the plot?

**Key Takeaway:** Good paperwork is a safety tool, a troubleshooting tool, and a speed tool.

### Lesson 3: Load-In, Rig Assembly, and Department Coordination

**In This Lesson You Will Learn**
- Describe the early sequence of a lighting load-in.
- Identify how lighting work interfaces with rigging, audio, video, scenic, automation, and venue operations.
- Recognize why overhead work and exclusion zones matter.
- Understand the load path concept at an awareness level.

**Short Answer:** Load-in establishes the jobsite, sorts equipment, builds the lighting system, and protects the production critical path. Lighting crew do not work in isolation. The department must coordinate with rigging, scenic, audio, video, automation, venue staff, and production management.

**What This Looks Like on a Real Call:** On an arena call, local hands may unload trucks, sort cases by truss or zone, help assemble truss, hang fixtures under direction, install looms, and prepare systems for inspection before the truss flies. Once the rig is overhead, access is harder, so orientation, clamps, secondary retention, cabling, and inspection must be handled before lift or show trim.

**Beginner Mistakes:**
- Standing under moving or suspended loads. Overhead work requires controlled zones and direction from qualified leads.
- Moving gear into another department's path. Audio, video, scenic, rigging, and lighting often compete for space, lifts, and timing.
- Hanging fixtures without verifying orientation or plot position. A fixture in the wrong place may require unsafe or time-consuming correction later.
- Dressing cable where it can pinch, snag, touch moving parts, block exits, or create trip hazards. Cable routing affects both function and safety.

**Professional Habit:** Stay aware of zones, overhead activity, and the lead's sequencing. Ask before moving equipment that may be staged for another zone, department, or truck-pack order.

**Safety Notes:** The complete overhead load path includes fixture, clamp, truss, suspension hardware, hoist, rigging point, and building or temporary structure. New stagehands should understand the concept but must not make rigging judgments unless trained and authorized. Do not climb, alter rigging hardware, remove retention, or enter exclusion zones without approval.

**Reflection Questions:**
1. What makes overhead work a critical-path item?
2. How can lighting crew create problems for audio, video, scenic, or rigging departments?
3. What should you do if you notice missing secondary retention or damaged hardware?

**Key Takeaway:** A fast load-in depends on sequencing, communication, zones, labels, and respect for overhead risk.

### Lesson 4: Power, Control Data, Networks, and Layered Troubleshooting

**In This Lesson You Will Learn**
- Separate power problems from data, patch, configuration, and mechanical problems.
- Recognize the basic path from console to fixtures.
- Understand DMX, RDM, sACN, Art-Net, universes, nodes, and switches at an introductory level.
- Explain why temporary power work is restricted to qualified personnel.

**Short Answer:** A fixture needs suitable power and correct control data. Similar symptoms can come from different layers: source power, distro, circuit, cable, address, mode, patch, node output, universe, IP configuration, fixture mechanics, or console programming. Good troubleshooting isolates one layer at a time.

**What This Looks Like on a Real Call:** A common control path is LD/operator to console, console to Ethernet switch or fiber, switch to gateway or DMX node, node to DMX line, then fixture. On a festival, the house backbone may support visiting consoles and guest universes. On a corporate show, the network may also interface with show control or video systems.

**Beginner Mistakes:**
- Changing several variables at once. This can hide the actual fault and create new problems.
- Assuming a lit fixture is fully functional. Pan, tilt, zoom, shutters, color, fans, encoders, and network settings still need checkout.
- Confusing DMX slots with fixture count. One fixture may use many slots.
- Assuming Ethernet automatically means the network is correct. IP addressing, universe routing, multicast/unicast behavior, priority, merging, VLANs, and switch configuration matter.

**Professional Habit:** Troubleshoot in layers: confirm what changed, identify the symptom, isolate the simplest layer first, communicate before changing shared settings, and record the fix if it changes the build.

**Safety Notes:** Temporary entertainment power must be designed, connected, and inspected by properly qualified personnel under applicable codes, venue rules, employer procedures, and authority requirements. Single-pole feeder connectors, grounding, bonding, protective devices, lockout/tagout, and verified de-energization are not beginner tasks. Treat grounding and bonding as life-safety systems.

**Reflection Questions:**
1. If a fixture does not respond, what layers could cause the symptom?
2. Why can duplicate IP addresses or uncontrolled DHCP damage the production flow?
3. Why should a beginner not attempt feeder or distro changes without authorization?

**Key Takeaway:** Do not guess across the whole system; isolate the layer and escalate restricted work.

### Lesson 5: Focus, Programming, Show Operation, and Load-Out

**In This Lesson You Will Learn**
- Explain the difference between function check, focus, programming, and operation.
- Recognize how scripted shows and busked shows differ.
- Understand why some faults are not repaired during a live performance.
- Identify load-out risks and documentation needs.

**Short Answer:** After installation, the lighting system must be checked, focused, programmed, rehearsed, operated, and removed. A fixture making light is not enough. The crew must verify full response, aim and calibrate fixtures, build repeatable console logic, operate safely during the show, and load out without losing information or damaging equipment.

**What This Looks Like on a Real Call:** In a theater or corporate cue-to-cue, the programmer may build precise cue stacks timed to a script. In concerts and festivals, the operator may combine stored cues, palettes, effects, and live busking. During show operation, the lighting team monitors power, data, fixtures, weather, backups, cueing, visibility, and communication with the show caller or stage management.

**Beginner Mistakes:**
- Treating focus as just pointing lights generally. Focus includes purpose, position, shuttering, color quality, camera needs, scenic interaction, and documentation.
- Ignoring backup files. Console files and show data should be saved and backed up throughout programming and before load-out.
- Trying to repair every show fault immediately. Access risk and the possibility of causing a bigger failure matter.
- Rushing load-out because the show is over. Fatigue, darkness, simultaneous departments, and schedule pressure make load-out high-risk.

**Professional Habit:** During show and load-out, stay disciplined: listen to the chain of command, confirm releases before moving or de-energizing systems, protect cable paths, document damage, and pack by the planned truck order.

**Safety Notes:** Do not access overhead equipment, energized equipment, lifts, roofs, or restricted work areas during a show or load-out unless directed by authorized leads and trained for the task. Public clearance, department release, de-energization procedures, and controlled lowering of flown systems must be respected.

**Reflection Questions:**
1. When might it be safer to leave a failed fixture alone during a show?
2. What information should be saved or documented before load-out?
3. Why should load-out be planned during pre-production instead of after the final cue?

**Key Takeaway:** The show is not finished until the system is safely removed, documented, packed, and accounted for.

## 4. Glossary

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Jobsite Context |
|---|---|---|
| Address | The starting control slot assigned to a fixture. | Used when matching physical fixtures to the console patch. |
| Art-Net | An IP-based entertainment-control protocol. | May carry many universes across Ethernet networks. |
| Busking | Live improvised operation using prepared groups, palettes, effects, and playbacks. | Common in concerts and festival operation. |
| Circuit | An electrical path supplying loads through appropriate protection. | Used when planning branch power to fixtures or dimmers. |
| Cue | A recorded lighting state or action. | Executed by an operator or triggered by show control/timecode. |
| DMX Universe | A set of up to 512 DMX data slots. | A fixture may use one slot or many slots depending on mode. |
| Distro | Temporary electrical distribution equipment. | Restricted power equipment handled by qualified personnel. |
| FOH | Front of house, usually an audience-area operating or lighting position. | May hold consoles, followspots, front light, or camera-related positions. |
| Loom | A grouped bundle of cables, often preassembled. | Speeds repeated truss or floor-package installation. |
| Node | A device that converts or routes network control data to outputs such as DMX. | Connects Ethernet protocols to fixtures or splitters. |
| Patch | The mapping between console identities and physical or network outputs. | Must match addresses, fixture modes, universes, and outputs. |
| Point | An approved suspension location for rigging. | Requires qualified approval and should not be assumed safe by beginners. |
| RDM | Bidirectional management over compatible DMX infrastructure. | Can help identify, address, or monitor fixtures but does not replace paperwork. |
| sACN | A standardized IP-based transport for entertainment-control levels. | Requires correct addressing, routing, priorities, merging, and switch behavior. |
| Secondary retention | A secondary means intended to retain suitable equipment if the primary attachment fails. | Part of overhead fixture safety; follow plot and site policy. |
| Trim | Final operating height of flown equipment. | Access becomes harder after trusses reach show trim. |
| VLAN | Logical network separation through managed switching. | Can separate show-control or lighting traffic when designed and tested. |

## 6. Instructor Notes

Emphasize that this is awareness-level training. Do not let learners interpret it as authorization for rigging, power, lift, or weather-command decisions. Learners may think lighting equals console operation. Repeatedly connect console work to power, data, patching, fixtures, rigging, documentation, and chain of command. Use simple diagrams to show the control path from console to switch/fiber/node/DMX/fixture and the separate power path from source to distribution to circuits to loads. Clarify that DMX slots are not fixture counts. One fixture may consume many slots depending on mode. Spend extra time on load-out risk. New crew often treat load-out as cleanup instead of a hazardous work phase. Avoid giving procedural feeder connection sequences, rigging calculations, or weather thresholds. Keep those as Guardian-sensitive and site-specific. Examples should be varied across corporate, arena, stadium, festival, theater, convention center, and touring environments.

## 7. Student Reinforcement Material

**Field Reminders:** Know the phase: advance, build, test, focus, program, operate, or strike. Ask who owns the decision before changing patch, address, network, power, rigging, or show files. Label both ends of important cables. Test before access disappears. Keep exits, fire lanes, doors, public paths, and mechanical hazards clear. Troubleshoot by layer: power, data, patch, configuration, mechanics, console logic. Do not enter exclusion zones or stand under moving loads. Do not perform electrical, rigging, lift, or fall-protection work without training and authorization. Document substitutions, damage, missing gear, and field changes.

**Call-Prep Notes for New Lighting Crew:** Bring PPE required by employer/site policy. Arrive ready to follow the department lead and venue rules. Learn the plot naming, truss names, fixture numbers, and zone labels as early as possible. Listen for release language before moving, lowering, de-energizing, or packing systems. During load-out, slow down enough to protect people, gear, cable paths, and truck order.

**Remember This On Site:** Professional speed comes from preparation, labels, sequencing, communication, and repeatable systems. Fast crews do not skip safety-critical steps; they remove confusion before it becomes delay.

## 9. Handoff Notes for Persona Rewrite Layer

Preserve the core lifecycle framing: lighting production is a chain from creative intent to technical design, documentation, installation, testing, programming, show operation, and load-out. Do not remove safety limitations around rigging, temporary power, work at height, lift operation, fall protection, weather decisions, feeder connectors, lockout/tagout, grounding, bonding, and qualified supervision. Keep the distinction between LD visual authority and technical/safety implementation led by the master electrician, crew chief, rigging lead, venue, or qualified personnel. Preserve layered troubleshooting language. Do not let the rewrite imply that a console fix solves all fixture issues. Tone can become more conversational and jobsite-grounded, but do not add unsupported war stories, certification promises, OSHA/ANSI/ESTA claims, or manufacturer-specific procedures. Good places for stronger voice: beginner mistakes, professional habits, real-call examples, and student reminders. Keep uncertainty markers where the packet says procedures vary by jurisdiction, employer, venue, system, engineering, and local authority.
