# 24 — Lead / Crew Chief Competency Framework: Cross-Department Model

**Research Queue ID:** CB-001  
**Status:** Complete research packet  
**Research date:** 2026-08-17  
**Purpose:** Establish the common competency transition from technician/hand to lead or crew chief before department-specific lead research.

---

## Decision question

**What distinguishes a lead from a technician across all departments?**

## Bottom-line finding

The evidence supports a clear answer: **a lead is accountable for the performance of a defined system, work package, or crew—not only for their own technical task.**

The transition is best modeled as:

**individual competence → independent technical judgment → work planning → delegation → verification → safety leadership → communication/escalation → accountability for team/system outcome**

Titles vary. “Lead,” “Crew Chief,” “Head,” “Lead Worker,” and “Site Supervisor” are not nationally interchangeable grades. The common feature is expanded **scope of responsibility**.

AEG’s current Stagehand Crew Chief role explicitly assigns tasks, oversees load-in/load-out, enforces safety/timeline requirements, trains team members, and owns orderly completion. PRG’s discipline-specific Crew Chief models add responsibility for planning, executing, and monitoring the technical requirements of the department while leading technicians and stagehands. OSHA’s Recommended Practices separately identify managers and supervisors as needing specific training in hazard recognition, control, incident response, and safety leadership.

---

# 1. Technician versus lead

## Technician

A competent technician is primarily accountable for:

- their assigned technical work;
- correct use of equipment and procedures;
- recognizing problems;
- communicating status;
- operating within authorization and safety boundaries.

An experienced technician may teach or assist others without automatically becoming the lead.

## Lead / Crew Chief

A lead adds responsibility for:

- interpreting the work objective;
- breaking work into tasks;
- assigning people appropriately;
- sequencing work;
- coordinating interfaces with other departments;
- monitoring progress and quality;
- identifying hazards and stopping/redirecting work;
- verifying completion/readiness;
- escalating constraints;
- communicating upward to production/client/department leadership;
- communicating downward to the crew;
- being accountable for the crew/system outcome within the assigned scope.

The crucial difference is therefore **span of accountability**, not simply years of experience.

---

# 2. Evidence from general stagehand leadership

AEG’s Stagehand Crew Chief description is unusually explicit. The role:

- oversees the stagehand crew;
- assigns load-in/load-out tasks;
- ensures safety and timeline requirements are met;
- assigns assembly/disassembly work across props, audio, lighting, and other show equipment;
- maintains safe/orderly work areas;
- completes safety training and trains team members in safe equipment operation.

This supports a cross-department principle:

> **A lead converts production intent into organized crew action and verifies that the work is completed safely and on time.**

Existing Crew Blueprint Package 01 also found that general-hand advancement is driven by reliability, competence, independence, communication, ability to teach/direct others, and trust—not a universal national title ladder.

---

# 3. Evidence from department crew chiefs

PRG’s current Crew Portal provides particularly strong comparative evidence.

### Lighting Crew Chief

PRG requires experience leading technicians and stagehands, ability to plan/execute/monitor lighting-specific requirements, strong electrical/lighting technical understanding, and responsibility for lighting systems.

### Audio Crew Chief

PRG requires experience leading technicians and stagehands, ability to plan/execute/monitor audio technical requirements, deep audio/product knowledge, and responsibility for audio systems.

### Video Crew Chief

PRG’s progression ties increasing video experience to responsibility for video systems and progressively larger crews.

This pattern is consistent across departments:

**technical mastery + people leadership + system responsibility**.

The department changes; the leadership architecture does not.

# 4. Eight cross-department lead competencies

## 4.1 Scope comprehension

The lead must understand the assigned outcome well enough to translate plans, plots, schedules, instructions, or production requirements into executable work.

This does not mean the lead owns the entire production. Their authority is bounded by department, crew, system, or task scope.

## 4.2 Work decomposition and sequencing

A lead must decide:

- what must happen;
- in what order;
- what can happen in parallel;
- what depends on another department;
- what must be verified before the next step.

This is a major step beyond being able to perform one task correctly.

## 4.3 Delegation and crew matching

Leads assign work according to demonstrated capability, authorization, task risk, and production need.

Crew Blueprint should explicitly reject the idea that leadership means “tell everyone what to do.” Effective delegation includes knowing **who should not be assigned a task without additional training or supervision**.

## 4.4 Technical verification

A lead must verify that work meets the relevant plan, system requirement, manufacturer procedure, or department standard before declaring it complete.

The verification method differs by department:

- lighting: plot/patch/data/power/system readiness;
- audio: signal/routing/system readiness;
- video: mechanical/data/display integrity;
- staging: configuration/connection/inspection handoff;
- rigging/electrics: safety-critical verification within qualified scope.

## 4.5 Safety leadership

OSHA’s Recommended Practices state that managers and supervisors are responsible for worker safety and should be trained in hazard recognition, controls, incident response, and their safety-program roles.

For Crew Blueprint, a lead should be trained to:

- identify hazards before assigning work;
- ensure workers understand relevant controls;
- respond constructively to hazard/incident reports;
- stop or redirect unsafe work;
- escalate conditions outside the lead’s authority;
- avoid normalizing schedule pressure as a reason to bypass controls.

## 4.6 Communication and interface management

A lead is an information bridge.

They must communicate:

**upward:** status, blockers, resource needs, safety issues, completion;  
**downward:** assignments, priorities, changes, constraints, safety expectations;  
**laterally:** dependencies and handoffs with other departments.

## 4.7 Change control and escalation

Live production changes constantly. The lead must distinguish:

- changes they are authorized to make;
- changes requiring department-head/designer/production approval;
- changes requiring a qualified specialist;
- conditions requiring work to stop.

The strongest lead behavior is not improvisation at any cost. It is **controlled adaptation within authority**.

## 4.8 Outcome accountability

The lead owns the answer to:

> “Is our assigned scope actually ready?”

That requires more than personal completion. It requires checking the crew’s work, resolving discrepancies, and communicating an accurate status.

# 5. Authority is multidimensional

Crew Blueprint should not teach “lead” as unlimited authority.

A person may have:

- **crew authority** — assign and direct workers;
- **technical authority** — make defined system decisions;
- **safety authority** — stop work/report hazards;
- **design authority** — change creative/engineering intent;
- **employer authority** — discipline/hire/fire;
- **union authority** — steward/referral/contract functions.

These can belong to different people.

For example, a lighting crew chief may direct the lighting crew but not have authority to redesign the lighting designer’s intent, alter venue electrical infrastructure outside qualified scope, or override production management.

This distinction is essential for preventing “leadership training” from becoming a false license to make decisions outside role boundaries.

---

# 6. Recommended readiness gates

Do not promote learners into a lead curriculum solely because they completed a technician course.

Recommended evidence of readiness:

1. consistent safe technical execution;
2. demonstrated independent work in the department;
3. reliable communication and status reporting;
4. ability to recognize and escalate faults;
5. ability to explain/teach routine work accurately;
6. ability to read the department’s basic plans/paperwork;
7. experience working through real load-in/show/load-out cycles;
8. demonstrated judgment under time pressure;
9. supervised experience directing a small task/team.

These are Crew Blueprint design recommendations, not national certification prerequisites.

---

# 7. Recommended Lead Tier curriculum architecture

Every department-specific lead course should reuse a common spine:

### Module A — Authority & Scope

- role boundaries;
- responsibility map;
- interfaces;
- escalation authority.

### Module B — Plan the Work

- interpret plans;
- sequence tasks;
- labor/equipment awareness;
- dependencies;
- pre-work hazard review.

### Module C — Lead the Crew

- assignments;
- communication;
- capability matching;
- pacing;
- coaching;
- conflict/error correction.

### Module D — Verify the System

- department-specific inspection/test/readiness;
- discrepancy handling;
- documentation;
- handoff.

### Module E — Adapt & Escalate

- change control;
- fault triage;
- schedule pressure;
- cross-department conflicts;
- stop-work/escalation scenarios.

---

# 8. Assessment model

Lead assessment should not be dominated by recall questions.

Use scenario decisions that require the learner to:

- assign a mixed-experience crew;
- identify a dependency conflict;
- respond to a worker reporting a hazard;
- choose whether to continue, stop, or escalate;
- detect incomplete work before handoff;
- communicate a delay accurately;
- respond to a requested change outside authority;
- prioritize several simultaneous problems.

A strong answer must demonstrate **technical judgment + people judgment + scope awareness**.

---

# 9. What a lead course must not imply

Completion does not establish:

- employer appointment as crew chief;
- union status or seniority;
- ETCP certification;
- electrical qualification;
- rigging qualification;
- engineering authority;
- competent-person status under every OSHA rule;
- authority to supervise work for which the learner lacks technical competence.

Leadership training cannot substitute for the underlying craft competence required by the department.

## Curriculum decision

**Define lead readiness as a change in accountability, not a title or time-served threshold.**

Across departments, the lead is the person who can translate intent into safe organized work, direct people within scope, verify the technical result, manage interfaces and changes, and accurately own the readiness status of the assigned crew/system.
