# 28 — Staging / Carpentry Lead: Build Authority & Safety Inspection

**Research Queue ID:** CB-005  
**Status:** Complete research packet  
**Research date:** 2026-08-17  
**Depends on:** CB-001 / Package 24; CA-002 / Package 22  
**Purpose:** Define the authority, competencies, verification duties, and boundaries of a staging/carpentry lead without confusing build leadership with structural engineering or code authority.

---

## Decision question

**What authority should a staging/carpentry lead have over a build, and what should remain with engineering, manufacturer, venue, or other qualified authority?**

## Bottom-line finding

The defensible model is:

> **The staging/carpentry lead owns execution of the approved build within scope; they do not automatically own structural design, load-rating, engineering approval, code interpretation, or authorization to improvise structural changes.**

The lead transition is therefore:

**read approved plan → organize labor/materials → control sequence → verify component/system condition → enforce manufacturer/build requirements → identify discrepancies → stop/escalate unsafe or unauthorized changes → communicate readiness accurately**.

This follows the cross-department lead framework in Package 24: the lead’s defining feature is expanded accountability for a crew/system outcome. It also preserves Package 22’s core boundary between supervised assembly participation and structural authority.

---

# 1. Evidence base

## ESTA / ANSI temporary-structure standards

ESTA’s published standards list includes:

- **ANSI ES1.19-2020, Event Safety — Safety Requirements for Special Event Structures**, covering temporary structures used for presentation, performance, structural support, seating, and viewing at special events.
- **ANSI E1.21-2024, Entertainment Technology — Temporary Structures Used for Technical Production of Outdoor Entertainment Events**, establishing minimum design/performance parameters for the design, manufacture, use, and maintenance of temporary ground-supported structures.

ESTA notes that E1.21-2024 is referenced by the 2024 International Fire Code and correlated with the 2024 International Building Code temporary-structure requirements.

Source: ESTA Technical Standards Program — Published Documents  
https://tsp.esta.org/tsp/documents/published_docs.php

These standards support an important curriculum distinction: structural systems have formal design, use, maintenance, and operational requirements that exceed ordinary crew-level assembly skill.

## Existing Crew Blueprint evidence

Package 22 found that modular staging work is legitimately performed by general/staging crews under direction, but that product families vary in locking hardware, bracing, legs, rail systems, permissible heights, and load limits. Package 22 therefore established that a worker may participate in assembly after instruction without becoming the person authorized to redesign or approve the system.

Package 24 then established the lead-level shift from personal execution to planning, delegation, verification, safety leadership, escalation, and outcome accountability.

CB-005 should combine those two findings rather than create a separate technical doctrine.

# 2. What the staging/carpentry lead should own

## 2.1 Scope and plan comprehension

The lead must understand the approved build package well enough to organize execution. Depending on context, that may include:

- stage/deck layout;
- riser layout;
- stair and guardrail locations;
- scenic placement drawings;
- manufacturer instructions;
- hardware/component lists;
- sequencing constraints;
- interfaces with rigging, lighting, audio, video, backline, FOH, and venue operations.

The lead should be able to distinguish a **crew-execution decision** from a **design/engineering decision**.

## 2.2 Labor and material organization

The lead should be able to:

- divide the build into logical work zones;
- assign workers according to capability;
- stage deck/scenic components to minimize unnecessary handling;
- preserve access and egress;
- coordinate carts/cases/hardware so the next build step is not obstructed;
- prevent workers from improvising hardware or configuration changes.

## 2.3 Sequence control

The staging lead should know which steps must be complete and verified before downstream work begins.

Examples of interfaces include:

- platform surface before sensitive equipment placement;
- stairs/access/rails before occupancy where required;
- final scenic position before dependent department work;
- completion of staging scope before production declares the area ready.

This is workflow authority, not engineering authority.

## 2.4 Condition and assembly verification

The lead should verify, at the level supported by training and assigned role:

- correct component family/product system;
- obvious damage or deformation;
- required manufacturer components present;
- connections visibly complete according to the approved system procedure;
- assigned bracing/support components present;
- access/rail components installed according to the plan;
- work area free of obvious incomplete-build hazards;
- discrepancies documented/escalated before handoff.

The course should teach **verification against an approved reference**, not free-form structural judgment.

## 2.5 Safety leadership

OSHA’s Recommended Practices say supervisors should be trained in hazard recognition, controls, incident response, and safety-program responsibilities. For CB-005, that translates into:

- pre-task hazard review;
- route/handling planning for large sections;
- pinch/crush/fall-edge awareness;
- controlling incomplete-build access;
- responding to worker hazard reports;
- stopping work when the build no longer matches the approved method or safe conditions;
- escalating structural, weather, ground-condition, or code concerns.

Source: OSHA — Education and Training  
https://www.osha.gov/safety-management/education-training

# 3. What the staging/carpentry lead should NOT automatically own

A Crew Blueprint lead course must not imply automatic authority to:

- design a stage or temporary structure;
- calculate or approve structural loading;
- alter engineered layouts;
- remove required bracing;
- substitute structural hardware because something “looks equivalent”;
- drill/cut/weld/modify structural members without approved procedure;
- approve ground-bearing or anchoring solutions outside assigned authority;
- determine legal occupant capacity;
- sign engineering documents;
- declare compliance with building/fire codes where that responsibility belongs to another party;
- override manufacturer limitations;
- approve a temporary structure after severe weather or suspected damage unless specifically qualified/authorized to do so.

A lead may be the person who identifies the problem and stops the build. That does not make them the engineer of record, authority having jurisdiction, manufacturer representative, or competent/qualified person for every applicable standard.

---

# 4. Outdoor structures and weather

ANSI E1.21 is particularly relevant to outdoor production structures because it addresses design, manufacturing, use, maintenance, and operational reliability of temporary ground-supported systems.

Crew Blueprint should therefore teach staging leads to understand that outdoor temporary structures may have:

- weather-monitoring requirements;
- operational management plans;
- wind or environmental action thresholds;
- configuration-specific restrictions;
- inspection requirements after unusual events or changes.

The course should **not** teach learners to calculate wind loading or invent weather thresholds. The lead’s job is to know the approved operating plan, monitor assigned triggers, and escalate/act according to that plan.

# 5. Recommended CB-005 curriculum architecture

## Module A — Build authority and responsibility map

- what the lead owns;
- what the lead verifies;
- what requires engineering/manufacturer/venue approval;
- how to stop unauthorized field changes.

## Module B — Advance, drawings, and material plan

- interpret layout/build documents;
- identify component families;
- organize hardware/materials;
- identify missing information before work starts.

## Module C — Crew deployment and build sequence

- work zones;
- capability matching;
- task sequencing;
- cross-department interfaces;
- handling and housekeeping controls.

## Module D — Verification and inspection handoff

- inspect against approved plan/manufacturer requirements;
- detect missing/incomplete/damaged components;
- document discrepancies;
- communicate “ready,” “not ready,” and “ready pending X” accurately.

## Module E — Change, weather, and escalation

- field change requests;
- uneven/changed site conditions;
- weather-plan activation;
- damaged components;
- conflicting production pressure;
- when to stop and escalate.

# 6. Assessment model

Use scenarios rather than build instructions.

Examples:

1. A required brace appears to be missing and another worker suggests continuing because the deck feels stable.
2. Production asks to move a stair location from the approved drawing.
3. A deck component is visibly bent but the schedule is behind.
4. A different hardware item fits physically but is not listed for the system.
5. Outdoor conditions change and the event’s operations plan reaches an escalation trigger.
6. Another department wants to load equipment onto a section whose build has not been verified.

A passing learner should identify **scope, hazard, required verification, and escalation path**—not improvise a structural solution.

# 7. Evidence-strength notes

**Strongly supported:**

- temporary staging/structures require defined design/use/maintenance frameworks;
- product/system requirements matter;
- supervision must include hazard recognition and control;
- lead responsibility includes sequencing, delegation, verification, and escalation.

**Crew Blueprint design recommendation:**

- the exact module sequence above;
- a formal “ready / not ready / ready pending” handoff vocabulary;
- requiring scenario-based assessment before lead-tier completion.

These are instructional design choices, not national job-title standards.

## Curriculum decision

**Teach the staging/carpentry lead as the accountable executor and verifier of an approved build—not as an automatic structural designer.**

Their core professional skill is converting approved intent into organized, safe, verified crew execution while recognizing exactly when the decision has crossed into engineering, manufacturer, venue, code, or other qualified authority.
