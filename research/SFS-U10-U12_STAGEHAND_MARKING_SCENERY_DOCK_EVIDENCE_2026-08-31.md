# SFS-U10 — Jobsite Tape / Label / Marking Conventions

## Evidence decision

Tape, labels, marks, and temporary identifiers are ordinary stagecraft workflow tools. They can support cable handling, case routing, scenery presets, equipment destinations, scene changes, temporary identification, and work-area organization.

The transferable beginner lesson is **not** a universal color code. The evidence supports teaching learners to:

- recognize that productions use tape/labels/marks to communicate temporary information;
- read the local key, label, destination, department instruction, or lead direction before acting;
- preserve existing marks unless assigned to change/remove them;
- make legible marks only when assigned;
- distinguish a temporary production mark from a safety/regulatory marking or manufacturer label;
- avoid inventing a color meaning that was never established for that call.

## Primary/industry evidence

### IATSE Local 193 — Carpentry Basics

Local 193 describes scenery being **spiked with tape** so a set-piece location can be found later and notes that colored tape can distinguish scenes or pieces. This is strong evidence that tape marks are routine stagecraft communication, while the exact color meaning remains production-specific.

Source:  
https://www.iatselocal193.com/carpentry-basics

### IATSE Local 122 — Primer 20: Stagehand Skills

Current Local 122 training lists cable wrapping and cable taping/tabbing among stagehand skills. This supports tape handling as a legitimate early-career stagehand subject.

Source:  
https://www.iatse122.org/event/primer-20-stagehand-skills-3/

### OSHA — 29 CFR 1910.176

OSHA requires permanent aisles and passageways to be appropriately marked where mechanical handling equipment is used and requires aisles/passageways to remain clear. This is useful for distinguishing **regulatory/workplace path marking** from ordinary production labels or spike tape.

Source:  
https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.176

## What Crew Blueprint can safely teach

1. **Read before removing or changing.** A tape mark may encode a preset, location, destination, department ownership, sequence, or temporary condition.
2. **Confirm the local convention.** Color meanings vary by production, venue, employer, local, and department.
3. **Use plain language on labels where possible.** Destination, case number, department, side/stage location, or owner should be readable when the production uses those fields.
4. **Keep marks controlled.** Loose tape, abandoned labels, and excess material can become clutter or trip/housekeeping problems.
5. **Preserve manufacturer, regulatory, inspection, serial, load-rating, electrical, fire-safety, or other controlled labels.** A beginner production-marking lesson must never imply authority to replace or obscure them.
6. **Remove marks only when the controlling crew says they are dead/released.** A mark that appears obsolete may still be needed for reset, rehearsal, strike, documentation, or another performance.

## Do not teach as universal

- a national spike-tape color code;
- one universal department color system;
- that any color automatically identifies electrical safety, emergency egress, or another regulated meaning;
- permission to cover manufacturer labels or safety markings;
- permission to mark historic/sensitive surfaces or client property without approval;
- adhesive compatibility with every floor, scenic surface, cable, textile, or finish.

## Visual evidence requirements

Future visuals should compare:

- readable vs ambiguous temporary labels;
- production spike mark vs manufacturer/safety label;
- one example of a production-specific color key clearly labeled **example only**;
- clean removal/reset vs abandoned tape debris;
- case destination labels and scenery preset marks without implying one universal format.

**Curriculum state:** `draftable with production-specific qualifier`.

# SFS-U11 — Scenery / Flat / Wagon Movement Under Direction

## Evidence decision

Moving scenery, flats, wagons, props, and scenic pieces is legitimate stagehand/carpentry support work. The transferable beginner subject is **controlled physical movement and handoff under the assigned scenic/carpentry/stage authority**.

It is not appropriate to turn this into a general fly-system, automation, structural-bracing, or show-cue authority course.

## Primary/industry evidence

### IATSE Local 193 — Carpentry Basics

Local 193 defines flats, jacks, stage braces, box sets, periaktoi, wagons, turntables, and common scenic movement terminology. It explicitly notes that wagons can move on casters and that set pieces may be moved by hand, while other systems can involve ropes, tracks, automation, motors, or winches. It also identifies locks/brakes/pins and stage spike marks.

Source:  
https://www.iatselocal193.com/carpentry-basics

This is strong evidence for teaching recognition and hand-moved scenic support while preserving a clear boundary around powered/overhead mechanisms.

### Existing Crew Blueprint Stagehand research

Package 15 and Package 18 already document current municipal/employer stagehand classifications that include moving scenery, flats, props, road cases, staging, microphones, instruments, and other production equipment.

Relevant source family already in corpus includes:

- City of Bloomington, Illinois — Stagehand  
  https://www.governmentjobs.com/careers/cityblm/jobs/5346682
- City of Eugene — IATSE Grip/Stagehand classification  
  https://www.governmentjobs.com/careers/Eugene/classspecs/19820

### OSHA/manual-material-handling evidence already used in SFS-U01/U02

Manual handling risk changes with shape, stability, route, visibility, force, awkward posture, team coordination, and available mechanical aids. There is no universal federal OSHA maximum stagehand lift weight.

Useful sources:

- OSHA Technical Manual — Ergonomics  
  https://www.osha.gov/otm/section-7-ergonomics/chapter-1
- OSHA — Ergonomics: Solutions to Control Hazards  
  https://www.osha.gov/ergonomics/control-hazards
- OSHA — Materials Handling and Storage  
  https://obis.osha.gov/Publications/OSHA2236/osha2236.html

## Transferable beginner learning boundary

A learner can be prepared to:

- identify common ground-supported scenery categories;
- determine who owns the scenic move;
- confirm destination and orientation before moving;
- inspect for obvious loose/damaged conditions and report rather than improvise repair;
- identify whether the piece is intended to roll, carry, pivot, or remain fixed;
- confirm locks/brakes/pins are released or engaged only according to the assigned scenic system and responsible person;
- preserve sightlines and communication during a team move;
- stop when the route, weight, stability, actor/public traffic, scenic mechanism, cue state, or piece behavior is not understood;
- return control to the scenic/carpentry/stage authority after placement.

## Explicit specialist boundaries

General Stagehand Core should **not** provide practical operating instruction for:

- counterweight or powered fly systems;
- automated scenery/machinery;
- motorized turntables;
- winch/track drive systems;
- structural bracing acceptance;
- overhead scenic attachment;
- load calculations;
- powered lifting devices;
- show-critical cue execution without assigned supervision/authorization.

Those systems require their own research, manufacturer/employer/venue procedures, and often specialist qualification or appointment.

## Important movement distinction

A piece that is physically movable is not automatically **released to move**. Show-state, rehearsal-state, performer interaction, locks, cables, automation, rigging, scenic connections, props, electrical/data connections, and other dependencies can make an apparently simple move unsafe or show-critical.

The course should teach:

> **Confirm that the scenic owner has released the piece before moving it.**

## Visual evidence requirements

Future visuals should show:

- flat vs wagon vs loose scenic item;
- obvious caster/lock/brake recognition without model-specific operating claims;
- route/sightline examples;
- two-person controlled movement;
- correct handoff after placement;
- clear boundary diagram separating hand-moved ground scenery from fly/automation/overhead systems.

**Curriculum state:** `draftable for ground-supported assigned movement; specialist mechanisms excluded`.

# SFS-U12 — Loading Dock / Ramp / Trailer Handoff Awareness

## Evidence decision

Loading docks, truck approaches, ramps, trailers, dockboards/bridge plates, road-case traffic, and powered-equipment traffic are ordinary live-event work environments. A general stagehand should understand the pedestrian and hand-moved-equipment hazards and the **handoff structure** around truck loading.

This packet is intentionally **not** a forklift, powered pallet-jack, commercial-driving, trailer-securement, or truck-loader qualification course.

## Regulatory evidence

### OSHA — 29 CFR 1910.176

Where mechanical handling equipment is used, OSHA requires safe clearances at aisles, loading docks, doorways, turns, and passageways; aisles/passageways must remain clear and in good repair.

Source:  
https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.176

### OSHA — Loading Docks

OSHA identifies loading-dock hazards including falls from dock edges and slippery conditions, and emphasizes keeping surfaces clear and watching for equipment movement.

Source:  
https://www.osha.gov/etools/powered-industrial-trucks/workplace/loading-docks

### OSHA — Pedestrian Traffic

OSHA warns pedestrians about forklift blind spots, rear swing, stopping distance, elevated loads, and the need to stay clear/use designated paths where available.

Source:  
https://www.osha.gov/etools/powered-industrial-trucks/workplace/pedestrian-traffic

### OSHA — Powered Industrial Trucks / dockboards

Forklift operator training is governed separately. Dockboards/bridge plates used by powered equipment have rated-capacity, secure-position, and operating requirements. A general-stagehand course should recognize these as controlled systems rather than instructing an unqualified learner to operate them.

Sources:

https://www.osha.gov/powered-industrial-trucks  
https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.178  
https://www.osha.gov/etools/powered-industrial-trucks/operating-forklift/load-handling

## Industry evidence

Existing Stagehand research packages document road-case pushing, truck loading/unloading, loaders/pushers, department handoffs, and loading-dock work as real stagehand duties.

Local 193 has a dedicated Trucks and Pushers training section, and current stagehand expectations describe workers converting to pusher work after department release during load-out unless instructed otherwise.

Sources:

https://www.iatselocal193.com/expectations  
https://www.iatselocal193.com/trucks-and-pushers

## Transferable beginner learning boundary

A learner can be prepared to:

- identify who is controlling truck/dock flow;
- understand that dock edge, trailer, ramp/plate, road-case traffic, and powered equipment create changing hazards;
- remain in the assigned pedestrian/work path;
- confirm whether a case/cart is ready to move and where it is going;
- keep clear of powered-equipment operating zones and elevated loads;
- recognize blind spots and rear-swing risk;
- stop when the dock/ramp/bridge condition, truck movement, surface, route, case control, visibility, or equipment interaction is unclear;
- hand a load/case/cart to the designated loader/pusher/department flow rather than inventing a truck pack;
- report damaged cases, unstable stacks, obstructed aisles, unsafe surfaces, or missing destinations.

## Do not teach as general-stagehand authority

- forklift operation;
- powered pallet-jack operation;
- MEWP operation;
- commercial vehicle operation;
- trailer positioning;
- dockboard engineering/acceptance;
- regulated cargo acceptance;
- load securement responsibility;
- truck-pack design or final load acceptance where that belongs to a loader/driver/lead;
- vehicle chocking/braking procedures as a learner-operated task unless the controlling employer specifically trains and assigns it.

## Visual evidence requirements

Future visuals should emphasize **pedestrian awareness**, not powered-equipment operation:

- dock-edge/no-go awareness;
- clear vs obstructed aisle;
- blind-spot/rear-swing zones;
- trailer/ramp/bridge transition as a changed condition;
- handoff point from pusher to truck loader;
- case destination recognition;
- diagram showing `pedestrian/general hand` vs `powered-equipment operator` vs `truck loader/driver/lead` authority.

**Curriculum state:** `draftable as dock/trailer awareness and handoff; powered operation excluded`.

# Cross-packet synthesis

These three evidence units reinforce a broader Stagehand Core pattern:

1. **Production work uses temporary information systems.** Read labels/marks before acting; do not assume universal meanings.
2. **Movable equipment may still be controlled/restricted.** Release/ownership matters as much as physical capability.
3. **Material flow is a chain of handoffs.** The learner should know where their responsibility starts and ends.
4. **Ordinary early-career exposure does not erase specialist boundaries.** A beginner may work around scenery mechanisms, forklifts, docks, trucks, rigging, or power without being authorized to operate or accept those systems.
5. **Visual instruction should make authority boundaries visible.** Diagrams should distinguish recognition/support from operation/acceptance.
