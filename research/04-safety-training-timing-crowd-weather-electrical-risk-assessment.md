# Research Package 04 — When Safety Topics Enter the Training Progression in U.S. Live Events

**Research date:** 2026-08-15

## Research question

For U.S. live events and public-assembly venues, when are the following topics typically introduced in a worker's development: day-one/entry-level, or later after floor experience and/or additional responsibility?

1. Crowd safety / crowd-management awareness for general stagehands.
2. Severe-weather response protocols for outdoor/festival events.
3. General electrical-hazard awareness for non-electrical workers.
4. Predictive/proactive hazard and risk assessment across a show's timeline.

This package also examines the formal U.S. Crowd Manager training/certification landscape and whether a general stagehand would normally be expected to hold that credential.

---

# Executive conclusion

The four topics do **not** enter a worker's development at the same level.

The strongest evidence supports this training sequence:

- **Day one / entry level:** electrical-hazard awareness, emergency-plan awareness, basic crowd-safety awareness, reporting/escalation, and basic hazard recognition.
- **Early field experience:** site-specific severe-weather procedures, evacuation/shelter expectations, crowd-flow awareness, and more deliberate jobsite scanning.
- **Experienced hand / lead level:** structured pre-task hazard assessment, anticipating changing risks across load-in/show/load-out, helping brief less-experienced workers, and understanding how crowd, weather, egress, temporary structures, and technical systems interact.
- **Supervisor / event-operations / safety-management level:** formal crowd-management duties, event-wide weather planning, formal risk assessment, contingency planning, decision thresholds, crowd-manager assignments, emergency-plan ownership, and coordination with public safety authorities.

The key distinction is between **awareness** and **assigned responsibility**. OSHA's guidance strongly supports teaching ordinary workers hazard recognition and emergency procedures early. By contrast, formal Crowd Manager duties and event-wide weather/risk planning are assigned operational responsibilities and should not be presented as ordinary stagehand duties unless the worker has specifically been assigned and trained for them.

# 1. Crowd safety and crowd-management awareness

## Recommended training point: entry-level awareness; formal crowd-management responsibility later

A general stagehand should receive **basic crowd-safety awareness early**, particularly when working in occupied public-assembly environments. That does not mean the stagehand is functioning as a formal Crowd Manager.

Useful awareness-level content includes:

- keeping exits and egress paths clear;
- recognizing crowd congestion near work zones;
- not creating trip hazards or blocked routes with cases, cable, carts, barricade, or temporary equipment;
- understanding that audience movement changes the risk profile of the site;
- knowing who has authority during an evacuation or crowd emergency;
- following venue/event emergency instructions rather than independently directing the public unless assigned to do so;
- immediately escalating unsafe crowd conditions to the appropriate lead, venue, security, or event-management personnel.

OSHA's general emergency-preparedness guidance says workers should understand the emergency action plan, potential emergencies, alarms, evacuation procedures, individual roles, protective actions, and who is in charge. That supports basic awareness at worker orientation rather than waiting until someone becomes a lead.

**Source:** OSHA, Emergency Preparedness and Response — Getting Started  
https://www.osha.gov/emergency-preparedness/getting-started

The Event Safety Alliance's Event Safety Guide likewise treats crowd management as one of the common safety concerns across live events rather than as an obscure specialist topic.

**Source:** Event Safety Alliance, Standards and Guidance  
https://eventsafetyalliance.org/standards-guidance-2

## Formal Crowd Manager is a distinct assigned role

The model fire-code landscape is important here.

The **2024 International Fire Code (IFC) §403.11.3** requires trained Crowd Managers where facilities or events involve gatherings of more than 500 people. It requires at least two trained Crowd Managers and at least one trained Crowd Manager per 250 persons or portion thereof, subject to listed exceptions and fire-code-official discretion.

The duties include:

- inspecting the assigned area for egress barriers;
- identifying and mitigating fire hazards;
- verifying permit-condition compliance;
- directing and assisting attendees during evacuation;
- assisting emergency responders when requested;
- performing other duties required by the fire-safety plan or fire-code official.

This is clearly more than ordinary stagehand awareness.

**Source:** International Code Council, 2024 International Fire Code, Chapter 4, §403.11.3  
https://codes.iccsafe.org/content/IFC2024P1/chapter-4-emergency-planning-and-preparedness

## Jurisdictional adoption is local/state-specific

There is no single federal U.S. Crowd Manager license. The requirement generally comes through **state or local adoption of model fire codes** such as the IFC, NFPA 101 Life Safety Code, and NFPA 1, plus local amendments and fire-marshal approval.

Concrete examples:

### Ohio

The **2025 Ohio Fire Code** uses the same general structure: Crowd Managers for gatherings above 500, at least two trained managers, and one per 250 persons or portion thereof, with exceptions.

**Source:** 2025 Ohio Fire Code, §403.11.3  
https://codes.iccsafe.org/content/OHFC2025V1.0/ohio-administrative-code-1301-7-7-04-emergency-planning-and-preparedness

### California

The **2022 California Fire Code** also contains the trained Crowd Manager requirement for gatherings above 500 and the one-per-250 framework.

**Source:** 2022 California Fire Code, §403.11.3  
https://codes.iccsafe.org/content/CAFC2022P3/chapter-4-emergency-planning-and-preparedness

### Portland, Oregon

The **2023 Portland Fire Code** likewise includes the same general Crowd Manager framework.

**Source:** 2023 Portland Fire Code, §403.11.3  
https://codes.iccsafe.org/content/ORPDXFC2023P1/chapter-4-emergency-planning-and-preparedness

### Rhode Island

Rhode Island is a particularly clear example of a jurisdiction that operates an actual state Crowd Manager training/certification process. The Rhode Island Office of the State Fire Marshal offers Crowd Management training and requires proof of completion plus registration to activate certification. Rhode Island states that it does not accept reciprocal certification and requires completion of its own session.

**Source:** Rhode Island Office of the State Fire Marshal, Crowd Management  
https://fire-marshal.ri.gov/safety-information-public-programs/crowd-management

### Ocean City, Maryland

Ocean City's Fire Marshal Office provides its own Crowd Manager training/testing material focused on public-assembly safety, emergency plans, exits, occupant load, inspection, fire protection, and evacuation duties.

**Source:** Town of Ocean City Fire Marshal Office, Crowd Manager Training Course  
https://oceancitymd.gov/oc/departments/fire/fire-marshal-office/crowd-manager-training-course/

## Who issues or defines the training?

There is no single national licensing body.

The structure is better understood as:

1. **Codes define the requirement** — often IFC, NFPA 101, NFPA 1, or a jurisdiction's adopted derivative.
2. **The Authority Having Jurisdiction (AHJ)** — typically a state/local fire marshal or fire-code official — determines what training is approved and how the requirement is enforced locally.
3. **Training providers** offer courses designed to satisfy those requirements.

The National Association of State Fire Marshals states that Crowd Manager training is required by national fire-code standards for many public assemblies and endorses a two-hour online Crowd Manager course written to align with NFPA 101, NFPA 1, and the IFC.

**Source:** National Association of State Fire Marshals, Crowd Manager Training  
https://www.firemarshals.org/Crowd-Manager-Training

The International Code Council has also publicly described Crowd Manager training designed around IFC/NFPA requirements for sporting events, concerts, fairs, nightclubs, and similar assemblies.

**Source:** International Code Council, Crowd Manager Training Program Updated  
https://www.iccsafe.org/about/periodicals-and-newsroom/icc-crowd-manager-training-program-updated/

## Would a general stagehand normally hold Crowd Manager certification?

**Usually no.**

The fire codes require the **event/facility to provide trained Crowd Managers**, not every worker in the building to hold the credential. The duties are specifically tied to assigned areas, egress/fire inspection, emergency evacuation, permit compliance, and coordination with emergency responders.

In practice, those responsibilities fit more naturally with:

- venue operations;
- ushers or front-of-house staff;
- event staff;
- security supervisors;
- facility managers;
- event managers;
- fire/life-safety staff;
- specifically assigned production supervisors where the event plan places that responsibility on them.

A stagehand **could** be designated and trained as a Crowd Manager by an employer or venue, but Crowd Manager certification should not be presented as a normal prerequisite or career credential for an ordinary general hand.

## Curriculum implication

Teach a beginner stagehand **crowd-safety awareness**, not Crowd Manager certification content.

A later Crew Blueprint leadership or venue-operations course could introduce formal Crowd Manager requirements as an example of role-specific public-assembly responsibility.

# 2. Severe-weather response protocols

## Recommended training point: basic site-specific response early; planning/decision authority later

Workers at outdoor events need basic severe-weather orientation **before exposure**, not after years of experience. OSHA's emergency-preparedness guidance supports educating workers about foreseeable emergencies, protective actions, warning systems, evacuation, and who is in charge.

**Source:** OSHA, Emergency Preparedness and Response — Getting Started  
https://www.osha.gov/emergency-preparedness/getting-started

For a beginner stagehand, severe-weather training should therefore cover:

- how weather warnings will be communicated;
- where workers are expected to shelter or evacuate;
- who has authority to stop work;
- what to do when instructed to clear a stage, roof, field, scaffold, tower, or temporary structure;
- why workers should not improvise structural, rigging, or electrical responses outside their training;
- how to report developing conditions such as lightning, high wind, flooding, extreme heat, hail, or poor air quality;
- re-entry only when authorized.

## Event-wide weather planning is advanced operational work

Event Safety Alliance's **ANSI ES1.7-2021 Event Safety — Weather Preparedness** covers development and use of event-planning strategies to mitigate weather-related risk at live events and temporary event structures.

**Source:** Event Safety Alliance, Standards and Guidance  
https://eventsafetyalliance.org/standards-guidance-2

ESA's dedicated Weather Safety Training is aimed at event colleagues who work events and addresses weather phenomena, forecasting, infrastructure impact, communications, public-safety coordination, and the elements of an actionable severe-weather plan.

**Source:** Event Safety Alliance, Event Weather Safety Training  
https://eventsafetyalliance.org/inperson-learning/weather-planning-workshop

ESA's 2026 weather-preparedness series also emphasizes pre-event forecasting, on-site monitoring, decision points, operational planning, and communications when conditions deteriorate.

**Source:** Event Safety Alliance, 2026 Safer Events Series — Weather Preparedness  
https://event-safety-alliance.squarespace.com/safer-events-series

Those are **planning and command competencies**, not ordinary entry-level stagehand responsibilities.

## Curriculum implication

A useful progression is:

**Entry stagehand:** recognize warnings, understand site procedure, know shelter/evacuation expectations, stop and report.

**Experienced worker / lead:** understand weather-related hazards affecting the work area, reinforce stop-work decisions, communicate changes to crew, help execute an established plan.

**Supervisor / production / event-safety level:** develop or help develop the severe-weather plan, monitor conditions, establish triggers, coordinate evacuation/shelter, communicate with public safety, and make or implement operational decisions.

# 3. General electrical-hazard awareness

## Recommended training point: day one whenever exposure is reasonably foreseeable

This is the clearest of the four topics.

OSHA **29 CFR 1910.332** says workers who face electrical risk not reduced to a safe level by installation safeguards must receive electrical safety-related training. It specifically distinguishes between **qualified** and **unqualified** persons.

Unqualified workers who are exposed to electrical risk must be trained in electrical safety practices necessary for their safety. The degree of training is determined by the risk.

**Source:** OSHA, 29 CFR 1910.332 — Training  
https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.332

OSHA also states in its own field safety program that an unqualified person should be trained to identify electrical hazards and maintain appropriate safe distances.

**Source:** OSHA Field Safety and Health Management System Manual, Chapter 22  
https://www.osha.gov/shms/chapter-22

For live-event general hands, awareness-level material can appropriately include:

- recognizing damaged cable, connectors, enclosures, distribution equipment, standing water near electrical systems, exposed conductors, overheating, arcing, or unusual smells/sounds;
- maintaining distance from electrical work areas;
- not opening, modifying, testing, energizing, de-energizing, or repairing systems without qualification/authorization;
- understanding that temporary event power can still present serious hazards;
- reporting hazards to the appropriate electrician/electrics lead or supervisor;
- never assuming that because a cable or device appears disconnected it is safe to handle outside assigned work procedures.

This content should be sharply distinguished from training someone as a **qualified electrical worker**.

OSHA defines a qualified person as someone trained and demonstrated to have skills and knowledge in electrical equipment/installation and its hazards.

**Source:** OSHA, 29 CFR 1910.399 — Definitions  
https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.399

## Curriculum implication

Electrical **hazard recognition and boundaries** belong in beginner safety training.

Electrical operation, testing, troubleshooting, feeder/power-distribution work, energized work, and qualification belong in specialized departmental training and employer authorization.

# 4. Predictive/proactive hazard and risk assessment

## Recommended training point: begin hazard recognition early; teach formal predictive assessment progressively with experience

This topic needs the most nuance.

OSHA's Recommended Practices for Safety and Health Programs explicitly says workers should be trained to identify hazards and controls. OSHA recommends training workers in hazard-identification techniques such as job hazard analysis and involving workers in eliminating hazards before incidents occur.

**Source:** OSHA, Safety Management — Education and Training  
https://www.osha.gov/safety-management/education-training

That means **proactive thinking should not be withheld from beginners**. Entry-level workers should learn habits such as:

- look before moving equipment;
- identify pinch/crush/trip/fall/overhead/traffic hazards;
- notice changing conditions;
- ask what else is happening in the work area;
- report hazards before they become incidents;
- stop and clarify when a task changes or an unexpected hazard appears.

However, there is an important difference between basic hazard recognition and **show-wide risk assessment**.

Formal risk-management programs require people to identify, analyze, evaluate, mitigate, communicate, and monitor risk across multiple operations and phases. IAEE's CEM Security, Risk and Crisis Management course, for example, describes risk management as a continuing process of identifying, analyzing, evaluating, and addressing exposures and monitoring risk. The broader CEM program requires three years of full-time exhibitions/events experience before candidates enter the certification program.

**Sources:**

IAEE Learning — Security, Risk and Crisis Management  
https://learn.iaee.com/certification

IAEE CEM Requirements  
https://www.iaee.com/cem/how-to-apply/

Event Safety Alliance standards similarly frame crowd, weather, communication, temporary structures, fire safety, and electrical safety as interacting event-level planning disciplines.

**Source:** Event Safety Alliance, Standards and Guidance  
https://eventsafetyalliance.org/standards-guidance-2

## Practical competency progression

### Level 1 — Beginner awareness

Worker can:

- identify obvious hazards in their immediate work area;
- follow a safety briefing;
- report unsafe conditions;
- avoid creating hazards;
- stop and ask when conditions change;
- understand basic hierarchy/controls concept at an awareness level.

### Level 2 — Proven hand / technician

Worker can:

- scan a task before starting;
- recognize interactions between people, equipment, vehicles, cable paths, overhead work, and timing;
- anticipate hazards created by the next phase of the task;
- adjust sequencing within assigned authority;
- communicate emerging hazards clearly.

### Level 3 — Lead / crew chief

Worker can:

- conduct or participate in pre-task hazard reviews;
- brief others on foreseeable hazards;
- recognize conflicts between departments and work zones;
- re-evaluate risk when conditions change;
- coordinate stop-work/escalation through the chain of command;
- think across load-in, show, and load-out rather than only the immediate task.

### Level 4 — Supervisor / event safety / production management

Worker can:

- conduct structured event risk assessment;
- assign controls and responsible parties;
- establish monitoring/decision thresholds;
- integrate crowd, weather, fire, electrical, structural, medical, and operational risks;
- coordinate with venue management and authorities having jurisdiction;
- maintain and revise emergency, weather, and public-safety plans.

## Curriculum implication

Crew Blueprint should teach **proactive hazard awareness from the beginning**, but avoid presenting a new stagehand as responsible for formal event-wide risk analysis.

A useful progression is:

> **See it → report it → anticipate it → brief/control it → formally assess/manage it.**

# Recommended placement in The Crew Blueprint

| Topic | Entry-level course | Intermediate/proven hand | Lead/crew chief | Supervisor/event operations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Crowd safety awareness | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Formal Crowd Manager duties/certification | No | Usually no | Sometimes, if assigned | Often relevant depending on venue/event role |
| Severe-weather response awareness | Yes for outdoor work | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Severe-weather plan development | No | Limited | Partial/supporting | Yes |
| Electrical hazard awareness | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Qualified electrical work | No | Only through specialization | Specialized | Specialized/authorized |
| Basic proactive hazard recognition | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Formal predictive/event-wide risk assessment | No | Introductory | Yes at crew/task level | Yes at event/program level |

# Bottom line

For a beginner stagehand curriculum, the defensible boundary is:

## Teach on day one

- emergency-plan awareness;
- crowd/egress awareness;
- electrical hazard recognition and boundaries;
- weather warning and response expectations;
- immediate hazard recognition;
- stop-work/escalation behavior;
- reporting and communication.

## Teach later as responsibility increases

- formal Crowd Manager duties;
- public evacuation leadership;
- event-wide severe-weather planning;
- weather monitoring and decision thresholds;
- formal job hazard analysis leadership;
- multi-department predictive risk assessment;
- safety-plan ownership;
- coordination with fire officials, emergency services, and other authorities.

The core principle is that **everyone should be trained to recognize hazards and follow emergency procedures, but only specifically trained and assigned personnel should own public-safety, weather, or formal risk-management functions.**
