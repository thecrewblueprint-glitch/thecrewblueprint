# Research Package 48 — Stagehand Field Skills: Flatbed Cargo-Securement Support

**RQ item:** RQ-SFS-03  
**Research status:** Source packet complete; driver/qualified load-lead review required  
**Prepared:** 2026-08-30  
**Use:** Evidence package for teaching a stagehand to assist a responsible driver or qualified load lead. It is not driver training, a cargo-securement certification, or authority to approve a load.

## Decision question

What should a stagehand know when assigned to help strap production equipment on a flatbed, and where must Crew Blueprint stop so the driver, motor carrier, or qualified load lead retains the securement plan and final acceptance?

## Bottom-line finding

Flatbed securement is a system-level task, not merely “using ratchet straps.” It can involve vehicle structures and anchor points, winches and winch straps, ratchet straps, chains and binders, blocking, bracing, dunnage, chocks, edge protection, cargo geometry, working load limits, tiedown count, and commodity-specific rules.

Federal rules require cargo on covered commercial motor vehicles operating on public roads to be prevented from leaking, spilling, blowing, falling, or shifting enough to affect vehicle stability or maneuverability. The driver does not have to personally perform every loading and tiedown action, but FMCSA states the driver must be familiar with the methods and may need to adjust the cargo or devices. The driver also has express pre-trip and en-route inspection responsibilities.

Crew Blueprint should teach the helper to recognize components, inspect conservatively, follow the approved plan, stay clear of stored-energy and load-shift zones, communicate, and stop when the plan changes. It should not teach a general hand to certify the load.

## Regulatory scope

The cited federal provisions apply to the commercial-motor-vehicle circumstances defined by the rules. State law, intrastate rules, vehicle type, cargo, motor-carrier procedures, contracts, and site requirements may add or change obligations. A training example must not imply that federal CMV rules govern every event cart, trailer, warehouse load, or private-property movement.

### General securement requirement

49 CFR 393.100 applies the cargo-securement subpart to trucks, truck tractors, semitrailers, full trailers, and pole trailers. Covered cargo must be prevented from leaking, spilling, blowing, or falling, and from shifting enough to affect stability or maneuverability.

Sources:

- https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-393/subpart-I/section-393.100
- https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/regulations/cargo-securement/cargo-securement-rules

### Driver responsibility is real even when others load

FMCSA's interpretation says a driver is not required to personally load, block, brace, and tie down the cargo, but the driver must be familiar with securement methods and procedures and may have to adjust cargo or devices under 49 CFR 392.9(b).

Source: https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/regulations/question-4-there-requirement-driver-must-personally-load-block-brace-and-tie-down-cargo

49 CFR 392.9 requires the driver to assure cargo is properly distributed and secured before driving. For covered trucks/tractors, it also requires inspection within the first 50 miles and reexamination when duty status changes, after three hours, or after 150 miles, whichever comes first, subject to stated exceptions.

Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-392/subpart-A/section-392.9

Curriculum implication: a stagehand may physically help, but the course must visibly return the final inspection and acceptance to the driver/qualified load lead.

## System components the learner should recognize

| Component | Beginner recognition | Decision owner |
|---|---|---|
| Vehicle deck, rail, pockets, track, anchor points | Identify the exact points named in the load plan and report damage | Driver/qualified load lead determines suitability |
| Ratchet straps | Inspect and operate under Package 47 | Lead selects capacity, route, and tension |
| Winch straps and winches | Recognize as a separate system; operate only after model-specific instruction | Driver/load lead and manufacturer procedure |
| Chains and binders | Recognize; remain awareness-only unless separately trained and authorized | Qualified load lead |

| Tarp and tarp hardware | Separate assignment with fall, wind, and stored-force hazards | Driver/load lead |

## Source findings

### 1. Securement devices and vehicle structures must be serviceable

49 CFR 393.104 requires securement devices, systems, vehicle structures, anchor points, blocking, bracing, and related components to be strong enough and free of damage that compromises performance. It prohibits knots in tiedowns, requires devices to be secured against loosening or release, and requires edge protection where abrasion or cutting could occur.

Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-393/subpart-I/section-393.104

The beginner's role is conservative: identify and report damage or uncertainty. The learner does not downgrade a defect as “probably fine.”

### 2. Cargo restraint is more than a strap count

49 CFR 393.106 requires cargo to be immobilized or secured by adequate structures, dunnage, shoring bars, tiedowns, or a combination. Cargo likely to roll requires chocks, wedges, a cradle, or equivalent restraint. The section also defines an aggregate working-load-limit requirement.

Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-393/subpart-I/section-393.106

49 CFR 393.110 contains additional minimum-tiedown rules, while sections 393.116–393.136 contain commodity-specific requirements.

Sources:

- https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-393/subpart-I/section-393.110
- https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-393/subpart-I

Curriculum implication: calculations and tiedown counts can be shown as **awareness of why the lead's plan matters**, not as a beginner authorization exercise. A simplified quiz must not imply that one length/weight rule is the entire law.

### 3. Manufacturer instruction remains necessary

Kinedyne's cargo-securement education distinguishes webbing threading, winch operation, chain binders, cam buckles, ratchet straps, and strap inspection as separate skills.

Source: https://kinedyne.com/201-cargo-securement-education/

The course should mirror that distinction. A learner who can operate one ratchet strap is not automatically trained on a trailer winch, chain binder, or different end fitting.

## Appropriate learner outcome

After instruction and supervised practice, the learner should be able to:

- identify the driver or qualified load lead;
- repeat the assigned load position, orientation, anchor, strap, edge protection, and sequence;
- inspect the assigned equipment and visible anchor/vehicle area for obvious damage;
- stage straps and protection without creating trip, fall, or vehicle-movement hazards;
- pass, place, thread, or tension equipment only when directed;
- stay out of fall zones, pinch zones, snap/recoil paths, and areas where cargo could shift;
- stop immediately if cargo moves, packaging fails, an anchor deforms, a strap is damaged, edge protection moves, or the plan no longer matches the load;
- leave final calculation, plan, inspection, and acceptance to the responsible authority.

## Recommended helper sequence

### 1. Establish control

Before approaching the load, identify:

- who is directing the securement;
- whether the vehicle is immobilized and safe to work around;
- the planned cargo position and orientation;
- the assigned hardware and anchor points;
- the communication word or signal for stop;
- who will release tension later.

No helper changes a case position, strap route, anchor, tiedown type, or sequence without the load lead.

### 2. Inspect assigned components

Check labels and visible condition on straps, winches, chains/binders if assigned, edge protection, dunnage, and anchor areas. Report:

- cuts, abrasion, knots, heat/chemical damage, stitching or label problems;
- bent, cracked, corroded, jammed, or distorted hardware;
- damaged rails, pockets, deck, anchor structure, or packaging;
- wet/slippery surfaces, unstable footing, or unsecured adjacent cargo.

### 3. Stage without entering the line of fire

Place equipment where the lead directs. Do not stand under thrown hardware, on the far side of an unstable stack, between cargo and a fixed object, or in the path a tensioned strap/hardware could travel. Do not climb on cargo or trailer edges unless the employer has provided an authorized access/fall-control method.

### 4. Follow the route exactly

Install edge protection and tiedown hardware at the directed points. Keep tiedowns free of knots and unintended twists. Confirm end fittings are seated and that webbing is not routed across unprotected sharp edges.

### 5. Tension in the lead's sequence

Tensioning one part of a load can shift another. The helper operates only on the call of the lead, watches the cargo/anchor/edge protection, and stops at any unexpected movement, noise, deformation, or loss of footing.

### 6. Clear and inspect

Remove tools, tails, loose dunnage, and trip hazards as directed. The driver/qualified load lead performs and records whatever final inspection is required. The learner's “looks good” is not final acceptance.

### 7. Controlled release at destination

Before releasing any device, identify what could move when force is removed. Confirm unload sequence, stable support, personnel clearances, and who controls the release. Never assume the original tension or cargo position remained unchanged in transit.

## Stop-and-ask conditions

- No identified driver/load lead.
- Vehicle can move or is being repositioned.
- Cargo is unstable, leaning, rolling, or differently packaged than the plan assumed.
- Required anchor, strap, edge protection, dunnage, or label is missing or damaged.
- A tie-down route crosses a sharp edge without approved protection.
- A device requires a tool, bar, extension, technique, or force the learner was not trained to use.
- The learner is asked to calculate or approve the securement without qualification.
- Tarp work, climbing, chain binders, heavy machinery, or commodity-specific cargo appears outside the assignment.
- Any person enters the line of fire.

## Visual and assessment plan

Original reviewed visuals should show:

1. complete system: cargo, dunnage/blocking, anchors, tiedowns, and edge protection;
2. helper safe zones and common line-of-fire areas;
3. approved versus damaged anchor/strap examples;
4. edge protection staying under the tiedown;
5. tension sequence directed by one load lead;
6. the driver performing final acceptance;
7. controlled release with the unload path clear.

Knowledge scenarios should test authority, damaged components, changed cargo geometry, missing edge protection, driver inspection, and load movement.

Practical observation should be an **assist** on a controlled training load. The driver/qualified load lead must sign the checklist. Do not turn a classroom tiedown calculation into an authorization claim.

## Authority boundary

The motor carrier, driver, or qualified load lead owns the securement plan and final acceptance as applicable. The stagehand follows the plan and reports conditions. Course completion is not a CDL, driver qualification, cargo-securement certification, motor-carrier authorization, or permission to approve public-road transport.

## Review required before curriculum handoff

- Current commercial driver or qualified cargo-securement lead.
- Employer/motor-carrier procedure review for any real vehicle filmed.
- Manufacturer-instruction match for every device demonstrated.
- Owner review for production-load authenticity.
- Legal/compliance review before presenting jurisdiction-specific requirements as mandatory outside their cited scope.
