## How to use this file

This document is an **interpretive synthesis**, not an additional independent research source. The numbered research packages contain the detailed evidence and citations. When a claim here matters to curriculum design, trace it back to the corresponding numbered package before treating it as authoritative.

NotebookLM and downstream models should use this file as a map of the major conclusions, relationships, terminology cautions, and curriculum implications found across the research set.

Do not treat proposed Crew Blueprint ladders below as existing national industry standards. They are curriculum models inferred from multiple sources and are explicitly labeled as such.

# 1. The industry does not have one universal career ladder

The most consistent finding across the research is that U.S. live-events production does **not** operate under a nationally standardized beginner → intermediate → advanced title hierarchy.

Titles, responsibility levels, union classifications, pay categories, and advancement structures vary by employer, venue, IATSE local, market, production type, and department.

The curriculum should therefore avoid inventing titles such as "Stagehand Level I / II / III" and presenting them as industry-standard positions unless Crew Blueprint clearly labels them as its own learning levels.

A better architecture is to model **competencies and responsibility**, then map real-world titles onto those competencies where evidence supports the mapping.

Related research: Packages 01, 02, 05, 06.

# 2. General stagehand work has a real advancement path, but much of it is informal

Package 01 supports a recognizable progression from ordinary stagehand/general hand toward leadership roles such as Lead Hand/Crew Lead, Crew Chief, Head Stagehand, and certain house-specific supervisory positions.

However, workers can become significantly more trusted and capable without receiving a different payroll title. Reliability, safety judgment, independence, communication, equipment familiarity, call history, and ability to guide less-experienced workers can advance before formal title changes occur.

Terms such as **Key, Swing, Utility, and Point Person** should not automatically be taught as standardized national hand-track ranks. Their meaning can be employer-, production-, or region-specific.

### Curriculum interpretation

A defensible Crew Blueprint general-hand progression is:

**New Stagehand → Proven Stagehand → Lead Hand/Crew Lead → Crew Chief / equivalent local supervisory role → broader site/production operations leadership**

This is a curriculum abstraction from industry evidence, not a universal national job-title ladder.

Staying a generalist is not necessarily a dead end. Past crew-chief-level work, however, advancement tends to shift toward **leadership, logistics, supervision, safety, coordination, and operations** rather than ever-higher grades of generic manual labor.

Related research: Package 01.

# 3. Department careers are better represented as branching competency graphs than straight ladders

Package 02 shows that audio, lighting, video, rigging, electrics, carpentry, and staging each have real progression patterns, but familiar titles often represent **specializations or branches rather than sequential promotions**.

Examples:

- FOH engineer and monitor engineer are different audio responsibility domains; one is not automatically a higher rank than the other.
- Systems engineer, RF technician, A1, and other audio roles may branch from shared foundations.
- Lighting programmer/operator and lighting designer can represent different technical/creative paths.
- Video similarly branches into switching, projection, LED, playback, engineering, camera, media-server, and other roles.
- Rigging has one of the clearest progression structures, but even there arena and theatre certification scopes are distinct rather than simply lower/higher levels.
- Carpentry has recognizable Carpenter → Assistant/Lead/Head Carpenter structures in some professional environments, but terminology depends heavily on context.
- Staging terminology is particularly inconsistent between companies.

### Cross-department competency pattern

The strongest general pattern is:

**Can assist safely → can independently own a defined task/system → can troubleshoot and adapt → can lead/train others and accept departmental accountability → can branch into advanced systems/design/management**

Crew Blueprint should therefore build departmental pathways around **competencies and responsibility thresholds**, not title collection alone.

Related research: Package 02.

# 4. Warehouse, field/touring, and fixed-venue work are real operating lanes, not sealed professions

Package 03 supports three useful operating-environment lanes:

1. **Warehouse/shop** — equipment prep, QC, inventory, maintenance, package staging, shipping/receiving, logistics.
2. **Field/touring** — repeated load-in/show/load-out execution, changing venues, road logistics, live operation, local-crew interaction, field troubleshooting.
3. **Fixed-site/venue** — repeated operation within a known venue or venue group, house systems, event setup/operation, recurring clients, venue procedures.

Employers genuinely separate these job families, but workers can move between them.

### Transfer is asymmetric

- Warehouse → field: equipment familiarity and QC transfer strongly; live-show pressure, venue adaptation, crew direction, and road logistics must be developed.
- Venue → touring: technical operation transfers strongly; touring adds changing venues, truck packs, travel, local crews, and sustained road execution.
- Touring → venue: technical depth transfers; recurring clients, hospitality expectations, and deep house knowledge become more important.
- Touring → warehouse: troubleshooting and equipment knowledge transfer well into prep, repair, QC, and shop leadership.

### Curriculum implication

Represent these as **career lanes with crossover bridges**, not separate universes and not one linear ladder.

Related research: Package 03.

# 5. Stage management is a distinct discipline

Package 05 supports treating stage management as a professional career track separate from:

- general stagehand labor;
- audio;
- lighting;
- video;
- rigging;
- other technical crafts.

Its core function centers on coordination, communication, rehearsal/performance organization, cueing/calling, production records, and cross-department execution of the show.

A common theatrical progression is approximately:

**Assistant/early-career stage-management work → Assistant Stage Manager → Stage Manager → Production Stage Manager**

This should not be confused with the sometimes different meaning of **stage manager** in concert, festival, corporate, or venue production. Those contexts overlap in coordination responsibilities but can represent materially different jobs.

There is no ETCP-style national technical certification ladder for stage management. Actors' Equity membership and stage-management professional organizations are not equivalents to an ETCP competency certification.

Related research: Package 05.

# 6. Leadership has both vertical and cross-cutting forms

One of the most important architecture findings is that the answer is not simply "everyone rises vertically inside the track where they started."

### Craft-specific leadership is usually vertical

Examples include:

- rigger → lead/head rigger;
- carpenter → head carpenter;
- audio technician/engineer → audio lead/A1 or equivalent;
- general hand → crew chief;
- stage-management worker → PSM.

These roles generally require real competence in the underlying craft.

### Broader production leadership is cross-cutting

Production Coordinator, Production Manager, Director of Production, operations, and similar roles can recruit people from multiple backgrounds. Evidence in Package 05 includes postings that accept combinations of production management, stage management, technical direction, stagehand work, design, touring production, and venue operations.

### Curriculum implication

The architecture should resemble:

**Multiple craft/generalist/stage-management pathways → craft-specific leadership → optional transition into cross-cutting production/operations leadership**

Production management should eventually be treated as a career track in its own right rather than merely "the highest stagehand level."

Related research: Package 05.

# 7. Safety training should expand in scope as responsibility expands

Package 04 supports a progressive model rather than putting all safety material either at beginner level or advanced level.

A useful sequence is:

**See it → report it → anticipate it → brief/control it → formally assess/manage it**

### Beginner

Teach immediate hazard recognition, emergency-plan awareness, electrical boundaries, stop/report/escalation behavior, and simple proactive scanning.

### Proven worker

Teach task-level anticipation: how hazards change as equipment, people, vehicles, overhead work, cable paths, weather, and work phases interact.

### Lead/Crew Chief

Teach structured pre-task thinking, crew briefing, cross-department conflicts, changing-condition reassessment, and thinking across load-in/show/load-out.

### Supervisor/Event Operations

Teach formal risk assessment, assigned controls, emergency planning, severe-weather decision frameworks, public-safety coordination, and other event-wide responsibilities.

Related research: Package 04.

# 8. Crowd management is not the correct primary framing for hand-level public safety

Companion Note 04A is important and should be treated as a curriculum correction/clarification to Package 04.

Formal Crowd Manager responsibilities are typically assigned public-assembly/fire-life-safety functions and commonly sit with venue operations, security, front-of-house/event staff, designated Crowd Managers, and other assigned personnel. A general stagehand would not normally be expected to hold a Crowd Manager credential simply because they work an event.

For hands, the more relevant concept is **Public-Area and Worksite Interaction Awareness**.

The worker should learn to recognize how:

- production materials;
- equipment;
- temporary infrastructure;
- terrain;
- weather;
- workers;
- public circulation;
- egress;
- changing site conditions

interact with one another.

The goal is not to teach the hand to control crowds. The goal is to teach the hand to **avoid creating or overlooking hazards that affect workers or members of the public**.

### Representative example

Barricade placed on uneven ground should not be taught merely as a material-handling task. The worker should notice stability, ground condition, alignment, surface transitions, nearby traffic, foreseeable contact, and whether the completed installation itself creates risk. Conditions beyond the worker's competence or authority should be escalated.

The same reasoning applies to cable ramps, decks, fencing, pipe-and-drape, carts, cases, temporary signage, work lights, floor protection, and other temporary production materials.

### Recommended hand progression

**Hazard recognition → environment/material interaction → public-interface awareness → proactive jobsite scanning → lead-level risk anticipation**

Keep this distinct from a possible future security/event-operations pathway:

**Crowd behavior → crowd flow → ingress/egress operations → designated Crowd Manager responsibility → emergency/public-safety planning**

Related research: Package 04 and Note 04A.

# 9. Credentials, job competence, and course completion must remain separate concepts

Package 06 makes this especially important.

### ETCP

ETCP is primarily an **experienced-practitioner certification system**, not an introductory training ladder. Its certifications should not be interpreted as Course 1, Course 2, Course 3 progression.

Arena Rigger and Theatre Rigger are different certification scopes rather than beginner/advanced levels of the same certificate.

### IATSE apprenticeships

Local apprenticeship systems are much closer to true developmental pathways. They can combine:

- work hours;
- classroom instruction;
- supervised practical experience;
- testing;
- wage progression;
- apprentice/junior/journeyman status.

But these systems are local-specific rather than nationally uniform.

### Tech Crew HQ

The public material reviewed presents Tech Crew HQ primarily as a broad introductory/foundational training system covering backstage basics and introductory technical disciplines. Public evidence did not establish a mandatory locked intermediate/advanced sequence.

### PLASA

PLASA's rigging pathway provides a particularly useful model: foundational safety requirements, trainee/logbook experience, supervised development, then professional assessment. Experienced workers are not necessarily forced through every beginner step if they can demonstrate readiness through the applicable route.

### Curriculum implication

Crew Blueprint should track at least four separate dimensions:

1. **Knowledge completed** — what learning content the person has studied.
2. **Practical competency** — what the person has demonstrated they can safely perform.
3. **Career responsibility** — what level of ownership/leadership their job actually gives them.
4. **External credentials** — OSHA cards, ETCP certifications, manufacturer credentials, union qualifications, or other third-party recognition.

A course completion badge must never silently imply employer authorization, qualified-person status, journeyman status, or professional certification.

Related research: Package 06.

# 10. Emerging Crew Blueprint architecture

Taken together, the research supports a system that looks less like a traditional single-track LMS and more like a **career and competency map**.

A defensible high-level structure is:

## Layer 1 — Orientation

Industry context, work environments, chain of command, basic safety behavior, terminology, communication, professional expectations.

## Layer 2 — General Stagecraft Foundation

Material handling, cases/carts, worksite movement, basic tools, cable awareness, public-area/material interaction, load-in/load-out fundamentals, hazard recognition, basic department awareness.

## Layer 3 — Career-lane exposure

Introduce:

- general hand track;
- warehouse/shop;
- touring/field;
- fixed venue/in-house;
- stage management;
- technical departments.

Learners should understand what each lane actually does before choosing specialization.

## Layer 4 — Department introduction

Audio, lighting, video, rigging, electrics, carpentry/scenic, staging, and future departments as justified by research.

Introductory courses teach vocabulary, system purpose, safe assistance, workflow, boundaries, and how the department interacts with the rest of production.

## Layer 5 — Supervised practical competency

Skills that require real equipment, supervision, logged experience, employer training, or practical demonstration should be identified as such. Online education can prepare the learner but should not claim to substitute for hands-on qualification.

## Layer 6 — Independent technician / experienced hand

The learner understands what competent independent execution looks like, troubleshooting boundaries, communication, workflow ownership, and role-specific responsibility.

## Layer 7 — Lead / advanced specialist

Crew leadership, training others, planning, cross-department coordination, advanced troubleshooting, pre-task risk assessment, and accountability.

## Layer 8 — Cross-cutting production leadership

Production coordination, production management, technical direction where appropriate, venue/event operations, site supervision, scheduling, logistics, budgeting, communication systems, and broader safety/risk responsibility.

## Layer 9 — External professional credentials

Map relevant third-party certifications and qualifications onto the pathway without pretending Crew Blueprint issues them.

# 11. Important terminology and modeling cautions for Claude/NotebookLM

When synthesizing these sources:

- Do **not** assume the same title means the same responsibility everywhere.
- Do **not** convert regional/employer terminology into a national standard without evidence.
- Do **not** treat years of experience alone as competency. Research repeatedly shows combinations of experience, demonstrated skill, trust, reliability, safety, and responsibility.
- Do **not** treat every technical title as a promotion from the title before it. Many are branches.
- Do **not** treat course completion as job qualification.
- Do **not** teach Crowd Manager as a normal stagehand credential.
- Do **not** merge theatrical stage management with concert/festival stage management without explaining context differences.
- Do **not** assume warehouse, touring, and venue work are mutually exclusive careers.
- Do **not** force production management to be the final rung of the general-hand ladder. It is a cross-cutting management discipline that can be entered from several backgrounds.
- Preserve uncertainty where the evidence says terminology varies by employer, local, market, or production context.

# Final synthesis

The research supports building The Crew Blueprint around **competency progression, career branching, operating environments, and responsibility boundaries**, not around a fictional universal hierarchy of job titles.

The strongest emerging model is:

**Foundation → safe assistance → supervised competency → independent execution → craft/generalist leadership → optional cross-cutting production leadership → external professional credentials where applicable.**

Workers should be able to branch into departments, remain capable generalists, move between warehouse/venue/field environments, enter stage management as its own discipline, and eventually transition into broader production leadership where their experience supports it.

The curriculum should tell learners not only **what comes next**, but also **what evidence or real-world experience makes someone ready for what comes next**.
