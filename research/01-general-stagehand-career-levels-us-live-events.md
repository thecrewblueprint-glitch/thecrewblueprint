# Research 01 — General Stagehand / Ground-Crew Career Levels in U.S. Live Events

**Research date:** 2026-08-15  
**Scope:** U.S. live-events stagehand/general labor work, specifically the non-specialist "hand" track rather than progression into audio, lighting, video, rigging, etc. Canadian IATSE examples are included only where they help clarify terminology used across the same North American stage-labor ecosystem; they are marked as such.

## Executive conclusion

There is **no nationally standardized U.S. career ladder for a general stagehand** equivalent to a formal trade progression such as Apprentice → Journeyperson → Master. The industry instead uses a mix of:

- base classifications such as **stagehand**, **production stagehand**, **overhire stagehand**, **general laborer**, or simply **hand**;
- informal or employer-specific senior-hand language such as **lead hand**, **crew lead**, or "go-to" hand;
- formal supervisory titles such as **Crew Chief**, **Stagehand Crew Chief**, **Head Stagehand**, and, in many union/venue environments, **Head Carpenter**;
- broader operational titles such as **Stage Supervisor**, **Site Supervisor**, **Site Coordinator**, or **Project Manager**, which may sit above general stagehands but often become management/operations roles rather than a pure craft classification.

The strongest recurring top-of-track title for a worker who remains fundamentally on the general stage-labor side is **Crew Chief / Stagehand Crew Chief**. In theatre and union-house language, **Head Stagehand** and **Head Carpenter** can occupy similar command positions, though "Head Carpenter" is historically rooted in the carpentry/stage department and should not automatically be treated as a generic universal stagehand rank.

The honest answer is therefore: **staying general is not inherently a dead end, but the advancement ceiling changes from "more skilled hand" to "trusted crew leader/supervisor."** Beyond that point, further advancement usually moves into operations, production management, venue supervision, dispatch/stewardship, or a technical specialty. There is little evidence of a broadly recognized nationwide ladder of progressively higher-paid general-hand skill grades beyond leadership responsibility.

## 1. What titles are actually real?

### Stagehand / Production Stagehand / General Stagehand

**Status: strongly documented; national/common usage.**

This is the baseline classification. Duties typically include load-in/load-out, case movement, basic assembly/disassembly, staging support, cable handling under direction, barricade/floor work, and other general production labor.

Crew One, a large U.S. live-event labor provider, publicly recruits for **Stagehand** as a distinct area of interest alongside rigger and heavy-equipment operator. Its public services page similarly lists stagehands separately from specialized technical workers.

Sources:
- Crew One — Join the Crew: https://www.crew1.com/careers/join-the-crew
- Crew One — Services & Solutions: https://www.crew1.com/about/services-solutions

IATSE Local 13 in Minneapolis currently lists separate 2026 postings for **Stagehand**, **Head Stagehand**, and **Crew Chief**, showing that "stagehand" can clearly function as the base level beneath supervisory roles within the same labor ecosystem.

Source:
- IATSE Local 13 job-posting index: https://www.iatse13.org/

### Overhire Stagehand / Overhire

**Status: real and common in theatre/venue labor; it describes employment/call status more than seniority.**

"Overhire" generally refers to workers brought in beyond a venue's core staff for a production or call. It should not be presented as a higher level. An experienced technician may be overhire; a beginner may also be overhire.

IATSE Local 13's historical/current posting index includes multiple "overhire" craft positions, demonstrating the term as an employment/call classification rather than a rank.

### Lead Hand / On-Site Lead Hand / Crew Lead

**Status: real, but employer-specific and not nationally standardized.**

Frontline Event Labor in Southern California advertises **On-Site Lead Hands**, describing them as experienced crew leads who interface with a client's production manager, direct the crew, and keep the day moving. Its booking language also says clients may request a **lead hand** with their stagehand crew.

This is strong evidence that "lead hand" is genuine U.S. live-event labor language, particularly among staffing providers. However, it is not a universal IATSE classification and may mean different things in different labor companies.

Source:
- Frontline Event Labor: https://frontline-event-labor.com/

The practical distinction is supervisory trust: a lead hand is expected to direct other hands, relay the production manager's or crew chief's instructions, maintain pace, and serve as a working point of contact.

### Crew Chief / Stagehand Crew Chief / Stage Crew Chief

**Status: strongly documented; one of the clearest top-of-general-hand titles.**

AEG currently uses the formal title **Stagehand Crew Chief**. Its 2026 Mountain Winery posting is unusually useful because it explicitly defines both experience and authority:

- oversees the stagehand crew;
- assigns tasks;
- is responsible for efficient load-in/load-out;
- maintains safety requirements and timelines;
- manages crew time, attendance, breaks, and meals;
- trains team members in safe equipment operation;
- requires **2–4 years of related experience** plus leadership/team-building ability.

Source:
- AEG Worldwide — Stagehand Crew Chief, Mountain Winery: https://aegworldwide.com/careers/jobs/AEGLV9161/stagehand-crew-chief---mountain-winery

AEG also uses **Stage Crew Chief** in other 2026 venue/event operations postings, showing this is not a one-off title.

IATSE Local 13's 2026 job-posting index lists **MN State Fair Baldwin Free Stage Crew Chief**, while the same index separately lists Stagehand and Head Stagehand positions. That confirms Crew Chief as a live-event title inside a union-represented employment environment.

Source:
- IATSE Local 13: https://www.iatse13.org/

A labor-relations certification archived by IATSE Canada concerning Power Promotional Events also describes a bargaining unit containing separate classifications for **Crew Chief, Stagehands, Forklift Operators, Riggers and Climbers**. Although Canadian, it is useful terminology evidence because it cleanly distinguishes Crew Chief from Stagehand rather than treating Crew Chief as a department specialty.

Source:
- IATSE Canada certification archive: https://canada.iatse.net/category/certifications-decertifications/page/5/?post_types=legal_decisions

### Head Stagehand

**Status: real, especially in resident theatres/venues; not universally defined.**

IATSE Local 13 lists a **Guthrie Theater — Head Stagehand** posting dated March 2, 2026, while also listing ordinary stagehand roles. This makes Head Stagehand a particularly useful title for The Crew Blueprint because it demonstrates explicit advancement that does not require changing the noun to audio, lighting, or rigging.

Source:
- IATSE Local 13: https://www.iatse13.org/

"Head Stagehand" generally indicates a senior resident/venue worker with responsibility for coordinating stage labor, maintaining stage operations, or leading assigned crews. Exact authority is house-specific.

### Head Carpenter / Head Carp

**Status: extremely real in IATSE/live-event language, but structurally ambiguous for this research question.**

In many arena, theatre, and touring environments, **Head Carpenter** is the local/house person who coordinates local stage labor and serves as the main stage-side liaison for the touring production. This can function like the top general local-crew authority even where the individual is not spending the day doing conventional scenic carpentry.

IATSE Local 357's public permit-worker guidance states that once on a call, a worker takes direction from the assigned **Head of Department, the Head Carpenter or Crew Chief**. The same document says permit-worker performance is monitored using feedback from **Department Heads, Head Carpenters or Crew Chiefs**. This is direct evidence that Head Carpenter and Crew Chief are recognized crew-supervision roles rather than simply more experienced hands.

Source (Canada; terminology cross-check):
- IATSE Local 357 permit-worker guidance: https://www.iatse357.ca/index.cfm?formID=105817&zone=%2Funionactive%2Fform_page.cfm

IATSE Local 13 currently lists Head Carpenter, Stagehand, Head Stagehand, and Crew Chief postings within the same local's job ecosystem.

Important caveat: because **Head Carpenter** may also denote an actual carpentry/scenic department head, The Crew Blueprint should not present it as a universal generic stagehand promotion. It is best taught as a common union/house command title whose exact scope depends on venue and contract.

### Stage Supervisor / Site Supervisor / Site Coordinator

**Status: real, but usually broader than the pure "hand" track.**

IATSE Local 13's posting archive includes **Walker Stage Supervisor**. Event labor firms such as Rise Event Labor also advertise "Site Operations" and "Site Coordination" alongside Stagehand, Crew Chief, and Project Management.

Source:
- Rise Event Labor job description: https://www.riseeventlabor.com/job-description
- IATSE Local 13: https://www.iatse13.org/

These roles can be realistic next steps for a veteran general crew leader, especially on festivals and site builds. However, once a worker is controlling site logistics, multiple work zones, equipment movement, schedules, or client communication, the role is better understood as operations/supervision than simply a higher grade of stagehand.

### Project Manager / Production Manager

**Status: real, but not a stagehand rank.**

These roles sit above or adjacent to the hand hierarchy. A lead hand or crew chief may report to a production manager, but PM is a management/production function rather than "Stagehand Level 4."

Rise Event Labor explicitly lists **Stagehand • Crew Chief • Project Management** as separate service roles. Frontline says its lead hands can interface directly with a client's production manager. That relationship supports a typical chain such as:

**Stagehands → Lead Hand/Crew Lead → Crew Chief → Production/Site Management**

The exact layers vary by employer and show size.

### Point Person

**Status: real phrase, but not a stable stagehand classification.**

"Point person" is widely used in production as descriptive language for the person serving as the communication contact. It is not well-supported as a nationally recognized general-stagehand rank. A crew chief, lead hand, house head, technical director, or production manager may be called "the point person" without that phrase being their payroll title.

Recommendation: teach **point person** as a function, not a formal career level.

### Key

**Status: weak evidence as a general-stagehand rank; highly contextual.**

"Key" is formal terminology in some film/TV crafts (for example, key grip), and production crews may informally refer to a key person or key hand. Research did not find strong current evidence that **Key** is a standardized U.S. live-event general-stagehand payroll classification.

Recommendation: do **not** build a general-hand ladder around "Key" unless a specific employer/local uses it.

### Swing

**Status: real entertainment-industry word, but not supported as a general-stagehand seniority level.**

"Swing" has established meanings in performance and touring contexts—typically someone who can cover multiple tracks/positions. Search results did not support it as a normal U.S. stagehand progression title comparable to Stagehand → Lead → Crew Chief.

Recommendation: exclude it from the core hand hierarchy or label it explicitly as context-specific.

### Utility

**Status: real production term, but not a reliable stagehand seniority level.**

"Utility" is commonly used to describe a flexible support assignment across many production industries. It usually describes breadth/function rather than seniority. A utility worker may be highly experienced, but "Utility" alone does not mean "senior stagehand."

Recommendation: teach Utility as an assignment type rather than a career grade.

### Steward / Call Steward / Payroll Steward

**Status: real union/jobsite role, but distinct from craft-skill progression.**

Union stewards administer contract/jobsite matters, worker representation, payroll/sign-in functions, dispatch/call processes, or related duties depending on the local and venue. IATSE Local 13 lists **Payroll Steward**, and Local 26 explicitly reminds "Stewards/Head Carpenters" about administrative paperwork.

This is a route of responsibility available to experienced workers, but it is not simply the next technical level above crew chief.

## 2. What actually distinguishes the levels?

There is no national years-of-experience matrix. Across sources, advancement is better explained by **trust + competence + scope of responsibility** than tenure alone.

### Base hand

Typical indicators:

- follows direction accurately;
- dependable attendance and call-time discipline;
- safe case/material handling;
- understands basic venue/dock/stage geography;
- recognizes chain of command;
- knows when to stop and ask;
- can perform common load-in/load-out tasks without constant correction.

Crew One's application asks applicants to describe stagehand/live-event experience and certifications, but does not publish a Stagehand I/II/III ladder. This is representative of the wider market: the employer tracks skills and experience without necessarily translating them into public ranks.

### Experienced / senior hand

This layer unquestionably exists in practice, but often **does not have a separate payroll title**. The distinguishing characteristics are:

- can be trusted with less supervision;
- understands load sequencing and department sorting;
- anticipates what the crew needs without freelancing outside instructions;
- can guide newer workers informally;
- has a work-history/reliability record known to dispatchers, heads, or labor coordinators;
- may hold useful certifications or equipment qualifications;
- may be selected for better calls or more sensitive assignments.

The important point for curriculum design is that **seniority often exists before a title does**.

IATSE Local 357's permit guidance illustrates this trust system explicitly: the union tells workers to keep the Call Steward and Business Agent updated on skills/certifications and says performance feedback comes from Department Heads, Head Carpenters, and Crew Chiefs. That is a concrete example of reputation and demonstrated skill affecting career progression.

### Lead Hand / Crew Lead

Typical distinguishing factors:

- directs a small group of hands;
- translates a larger instruction into assignments;
- acts as a communication link to crew chief, production manager, department head, or client;
- watches pace and basic safety;
- is expected to know who is assigned where;
- is still a working member of the crew rather than purely management.

Frontline's description is especially clear: its On-Site Lead Hands "interface with your production manager" and "direct the crew."

This is the strongest evidence for treating Lead Hand as a real intermediate leadership role where the employer uses that title.

### Crew Chief / Stagehand Crew Chief

Typical distinguishing factors:

- responsible for the performance of the crew rather than only personal task execution;
- assigns tasks and labor;
- controls or helps control call flow, meal/break timing, attendance, and manpower allocation;
- communicates directly with production/venue/client leadership;
- enforces safety expectations;
- may train or coach crew;
- accountable for load-in/load-out completion and timeline.

AEG's current posting provides a concrete experience benchmark of **2–4 years related experience**, but this should not be universalized. Other employers may promote faster or require much more experience depending on scale and market.

### Head Stagehand / Head Carpenter / Stage Supervisor

Typical distinguishing factors:

- venue/show-level authority;
- may lead multiple crews or departments depending on house structure;
- interfaces with touring personnel, production management, venue management, or technical direction;
- may hold continuity responsibility across an entire event, season, or facility rather than a single labor pod;
- may be responsible for paperwork, staffing coordination, safety, equipment readiness, or building procedures.

These positions are less portable as a single national rank because each venue defines its hierarchy differently.

## 3. Do unions and labor providers publish tiered pay structures?

### The most important finding: usually not as a universal hand-skill ladder

Union locals frequently publish or maintain **contract-specific wage classifications**, but the structure is normally driven by:

- employer/venue agreement;
- job classification;
- department or responsibility;
- premium skills (especially rigging);
- supervisory/head assignments;
- seniority/referral rules;

rather than a national Stagehand I → II → III system.

IATSE Local 18 in Milwaukee explains publicly that wage rates differ by employer/venue and that rates cannot simply be listed as one universal scale because contracts roll and renew independently. It also states that, across many employers, **rates are the same across departments except rigging**—for example, an electrician can receive the same base rate as a carpenter. This is useful because it shows that union compensation does not necessarily map to a simple hierarchy of technical prestige.

Source:
- IATSE Local 18, Pay and Compensation: https://iatse18.org/pay-and-compensation/

IATSE Local 13's job-posting index is even more revealing structurally: it simultaneously recognizes titles including Stagehand, Head Stagehand, Crew Chief, Head Carpenter, Head Electrician, Head Audio, Head Video, Payroll Steward, and Stage Technician. That looks much more like a **matrix of job responsibility and venue roles** than a single vertical stagehand ranking system.

Source:
- https://www.iatse13.org/

IATSE Local 26's member resources refer jointly to **Stewards/Head Carpenters**, illustrating how union call leadership may include both craft/job leadership and contract administration.

Source:
- https://www.iatse26.org/member-resources/

### Labor providers

Private labor providers are often clearer about leadership tiers than about formal skill grades.

Examples:

- **Frontline Event Labor:** stagehand crews with optional **lead hand**; experienced On-Site Lead Hands interface with production managers and direct crews.
- **Rise Event Labor:** publicly distinguishes **Stagehand**, **Crew Chief**, and **Project Management**.
- **Crew One:** publicly distinguishes Stagehand from specialized classifications and markets "opportunities for advancement," but its public site does not expose a Stagehand I/II/III ladder.
- **AEG:** publishes a direct Stagehand Crew Chief job description with supervisory duties and 2–4 years related experience.

This supports a practical U.S. labor-provider ladder of:

**Stagehand → trusted/senior hand → Lead Hand (where used) → Crew Chief → site/project/production management**

with the middle "senior hand" step often being informal.

## 4. Is there a recognized top of the general-hand track?

### Yes — but it is leadership, not a nationally standardized master-skill grade

The strongest defensible answer is **Crew Chief / Stagehand Crew Chief**, with **Head Stagehand** and **Head Carpenter** serving as house-specific equivalents or adjacent top roles.

Evidence:

1. AEG explicitly defines Stagehand Crew Chief as the person who oversees and assigns the stagehand crew.
2. Frontline explicitly sells lead hands to direct stagehand crews and interface with production managers.
3. IATSE Local 13 lists Stagehand, Head Stagehand, and Crew Chief as distinct live-event jobs.
4. IATSE Local 357 instructs workers to take direction from Head Carpenter or Crew Chief and uses those positions as sources of worker-performance feedback.
5. Rise Event Labor's role taxonomy moves directly from Stagehand/Crew Chief into Project Management rather than showing additional grades of "general hand."

### Does staying general become a dead end?

**Not exactly, but there is a ceiling to purely individual-contributor advancement.**

A worker can build a long career as a highly trusted general stagehand and may get better calls because of reliability, breadth, institutional knowledge, or local seniority. But the public evidence does **not** reveal a broadly recognized sequence such as:

Stagehand I → Stagehand II → Senior Stagehand → Master Stagehand → Expert Stagehand.

Instead, once a worker becomes one of the strongest general hands, additional advancement typically takes one of four forms:

1. **Leadership:** Lead Hand → Crew Chief / Head Stagehand / Head Carpenter.
2. **Operations/management:** Stage Supervisor, Site Coordinator/Supervisor, Project Manager, Production Manager.
3. **Union/jobsite administration:** Steward, Call Steward, dispatcher, referral/crew administration.
4. **Specialization:** rigging, audio, lighting, video, carpentry, equipment operation, etc.

Therefore a person absolutely can advance without becoming an audio/lighting/rigging specialist, but the advancement becomes **leadership and operational accountability** rather than endlessly deeper general-labor grades.

## 5. Industry language is inconsistent and regional

This should be explicitly taught rather than hidden.

### Terms with high confidence

- Stagehand
- Production Stagehand
- Crew Chief
- Stagehand Crew Chief
- Head Stagehand
- Head Carpenter / Head Carp (with context warning)
- Steward / Call Steward (union function)

### Terms that are real but employer-specific

- Lead Hand
- Crew Lead
- Stage Supervisor
- Site Supervisor
- Site Coordinator
- Utility

### Terms that should NOT be presented as standardized hand ranks

- Key
- Point Person
- Swing

These can be valid words on particular crews, but current research does not support them as portable U.S. stagehand career classifications.

## 6. Community/trade discussion: why formal level systems break down

A long-running ControlBooth discussion titled **"Input on Stagehand Classification/Job Levels"** is useful as qualitative industry evidence. Participants include working technicians and an identified IATSE production carpenter. The conversation repeatedly notes that skill levels vary widely by person, venue, duty, and production. One participant who had hired technicians described using a dual pay scale plus separate skill-proficiency tracking rather than pretending every technician advanced uniformly across departments. Another described "box pushers" as a general entry layer with crew heads/experts above them.

This is not an authoritative contract source and is older (2010), so it should not drive wage claims. Its value is that it explains **why a universal hierarchy has not emerged**: live-event labor is highly task- and venue-dependent, and workers develop uneven skill portfolios.

Source:
- ControlBooth — Input on Stagehand Classification/Job Levels: https://www.controlbooth.com/threads/input-on-stagehand-classification-job-levels.20987/

A separate ControlBooth hierarchy discussion similarly emphasizes that organizational reporting structures differ substantially from theatre to theatre and distinguishes permanent house titles from production-specific assignments.

Source:
- https://www.controlbooth.com/threads/theater-hierarchy.36401/

These community sources align with the contemporary employer/union evidence: **responsibility and trust are more portable markers than numbered stagehand grades.**

## 7. Recommended Crew Blueprint model

For educational purposes, The Crew Blueprint should avoid inventing false national titles. A useful model would label its levels as a **competency framework**, not claim they are universal payroll classifications.

### Level A — New / Entry Stagehand

Industry titles likely encountered: Stagehand, General Stagehand, Production Stagehand, Overhire.

Core distinction: can safely function under direct supervision.

### Level B — Proven / Independent Stagehand

Industry title often still simply: Stagehand.

Core distinction: reliable, repeat-call worker who needs substantially less supervision, understands load flow, communicates well, and can be trusted with normal general-hand tasks.

Important: **this level often exists without a formal title change.**

### Level C — Lead Hand / Crew Lead

Use only as "common employer terminology," not universal classification.

Core distinction: directs a small pod/team, relays instructions, interfaces upward, and remains hands-on.

### Level D — Crew Chief / Head Stagehand / House Head

Core distinction: accountable for crew deployment, safety, timeline, communication, and completion of the call.

Contextual equivalents/adjacent titles: Head Carpenter, Stage Supervisor.

### Level E — General-Labor Operations Leadership

Possible real titles: Site Supervisor, Site Coordinator, Project Manager, Production Manager, labor coordinator/dispatcher depending employer.

This is no longer purely a "hand" classification, but it is the realistic continuation for a person who deliberately remains a generalist rather than becoming a technical specialist.

---

## 8. Key curriculum implications

1. **Do not tell beginners that specialization is the only way to advance.** Leadership provides a real parallel track.
2. **Do not promise a standardized title progression.** A worker may remain "Stagehand" on payroll for years while becoming dramatically more trusted and capable.
3. Teach that the most important promotion currency is **demonstrated reliability, safe judgment, communication, breadth, and the ability to direct others.**
4. Teach learners to ask each employer/local what its actual classifications mean. A "Head Carpenter," "Crew Chief," and "Lead Hand" can have overlapping functions in different markets.
5. Treat **Crew Chief** as the most portable high-level general-hand title found in this research.
6. Teach **Head Stagehand** as a strong venue/theatre-specific senior title.
7. Treat **Head Carpenter** carefully: common and powerful, but not synonymous everywhere with "senior general hand."
8. Treat "key," "swing," "utility," and "point person" as contextual vocabulary rather than universal career levels.

## Confidence assessment

**High confidence:** Stagehand, Crew Chief/Stagehand Crew Chief, Head Stagehand, Head Carpenter, and union steward/head structures are real; general-hand advancement commonly becomes crew leadership rather than a numbered mastery ladder.

**Moderate confidence:** Lead Hand/Crew Lead is sufficiently documented to teach as common staffing-provider terminology, but prevalence varies by company and region.

**Low confidence as standardized career titles:** Key, Point Person, Swing, Utility. These words may be used on crews but should not be represented as national general-stagehand ranks without employer-specific evidence.

## Bottom line

The U.S. live-events industry has a **real general-hand career track, but not a standardized general-hand credential ladder**. The practical progression is less about accumulating official numbered levels and more about earning enough competence and trust to be given responsibility for other workers. The most defensible model is:

**New Stagehand → Proven Stagehand → Lead Hand/Crew Lead (where used) → Crew Chief / Head Stagehand / Head Carpenter (house-dependent) → Site/Production Operations Leadership**

A worker does not have to become an audio, lighting, video, or rigging specialist to advance. But once they reach the top of general stage labor, the next differentiator is **leadership, logistics, safety, and accountability**, not simply being an even more experienced box pusher.
