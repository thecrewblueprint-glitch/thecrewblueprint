# Executive conclusion

The most defensible Course 2 mental model is:

> **Recognize the system → understand the approved build intent → stage the correct components in the correct sequence → participate in assembly only within assigned, supervised tasks → verify obvious fit/condition/alignment cues → stop and escalate when the build no longer matches the plan, manufacturer system, or safe condition.**

The central distinction is:

> **Assembly participation is not structural authority.**

A junior staging/carpentry worker can reasonably learn to identify decks, support frames/legs, stairs, guardrails, ramps, bracing, connectors, skirting, scenic elements, and related hardware; understand how those parts interact; help move, stage, position, and assemble modular systems under direction; and recognize obvious mismatches or incomplete conditions.

That worker should **not** be taught that Course 2 authorizes them to determine load capacity, change support geometry, redesign bracing, substitute hardware, modify structural components, set anchoring/ballast, calculate wind loads, decide code compliance, fabricate structural components, or accept a finished structure as safe for use.

Current ESTA and manufacturer evidence strongly supports this boundary. ANSI E1.21-2024 establishes design/performance requirements for temporary ground-supported structures used in outdoor entertainment production, while current StageRight, Wenger, and Bil-Jax documentation demonstrates that actual assembly details, bracing, connectors, support systems, guardrails, stairs, ramps, and leveling methods are system-specific.

**Course 2 should therefore teach supervised build literacy, not structural design.**

# 2. The core system model

A modular stage or platform should be taught as an interdependent system rather than a pile of decks.

A useful beginner-level conceptual chain is:

**site / floor condition → support system → deck/platform surface → connections and bracing → access components → edge protection → finish/accessories → inspection/acceptance**

The learner does not need to engineer any of these layers. They do need to understand that each layer can affect the others.

## 2.1 Site / floor condition

The support system sits on something: a venue floor, slab, field surface, temporary floor, uneven outdoor grade, or another approved support condition. Manufacturer systems respond differently to these conditions.

StageRight's ME-1000 line, for example, distinguishes arena-style and all-terrain support systems, with the all-terrain version specifically intended for uneven terrain. StageRight's Z-800HD uses adjustable screw feet for fine leveling, while the ME-500 uses a different bridging-deck support architecture with height selectors and adjustable feet.

Sources:

- StageRight — ME-1000 Portable Stage Supports  
  https://performance.stageright.com/products/portable-stages/me-1000-stage-supports/
- StageRight — Z-800HD Portable Stage Supports  
  https://performance.stageright.com/products/portable-stages/z-800hd-stage-supports/
- StageRight — ME-500 Portable Stage Supports  
  https://performance.stageright.com/products/portable-stages/me-500-stage-supports/

**Curriculum implication:** teach learners to recognize that the support method is intentional and system-specific. Do not teach them to improvise leveling or support corrections.

## 2.2 Support system

Common support concepts include:

- fixed or adjustable legs;
- folding frames or riser frames;
- one-piece support assemblies;
- bridging supports;
- base pads/feet;
- height-selection devices;
- horizontal or diagonal bracing;
- stabilizer braces;
- locator nodes, pockets, sleeves, pins, or other proprietary interfaces.

StageRight's Z-800 uses one-piece supports with horizontal bracing, while the ME-500 uses a bridging support architecture and locator assemblies. Wenger StageTek publishes separate instructions for guardrails, stabilizer bracing, leg retention, flat-stage bracing, and tiered-riser bracing. These examples demonstrate that "stage support" is not one universal construction method.

## 2.3 Deck/platform surface

Decks may differ in dimensions, surface type, edge detail, structural construction, and connection method. StageRight, for example, lists multiple standard deck sizes and multiple surface options depending on application.

Source:

- StageRight — All-Purpose Stage Deck  
  https://performance.stageright.com/products/stage-decks/all-purpose-stage-deck/

A Course 2 learner should be able to identify the correct deck family and orientation **from the plan, labels, system markings, or lead direction**, not from assumption.

## 2.4 Connections and bracing

Connections and bracing are safety-critical because they make separate pieces behave as an intended system. Their exact geometry and hardware are manufacturer-specific.

Wenger publishes separate StageTek instructions for stabilizer bracing, flat-stage bracing, tiered-riser bracing, leg retention, and multiple guardrail types. StageRight's Z-800 emphasizes integrated horizontal bracing. Bil-Jax systems use dedicated system hardware and proprietary connection approaches.

Sources:

- Wenger — StageTek Staging resources

- StageRight — Z-800 Portable Stage Supports  
  https://performance.stageright.com/products/portable-stages/z-800-stage-supports/
- Bil-Jax — Event Staging  
  https://biljax.com/product-category/event/event-staging/

**Curriculum implication:** a missing brace, wrong connector, unseated interface, or hardware mismatch is a **stop-and-escalate condition**, not an invitation for the junior worker to invent a substitute.

## 2.5 Access components: stairs and ramps

Stairs and ramps are part of the system, not afterthoughts. They may include dedicated attachment geometry, handrails, filler pieces, landings, and system-specific interfaces.

StageRight's folding adjustable stairs are explicitly compatible only with StageRight platforms. Its access ramp is a dedicated system with guardrails and defined configuration options. Bil-Jax's AS2100 ramp documentation uses dedicated ramp decks, special guardrail locks, filler components, and system-specific hardware.

Sources:

- StageRight — Folding Adjustable Stairs  
  https://performance.stageright.com/products/accessories/stairs/folding-adjustable-stairs/
- StageRight — Access Ramp  
  https://performance.stageright.com/products/accessories/stage-access-ramp/
- Bil-Jax — AS2100 Ramp / Guardrail guidance  
  https://biljax.com/how-to-build-ada-ramp-for-the-bil-jax-as2100-aluminum-stage-system

The curriculum should teach recognition of access-system completeness and mismatch, but not ask the learner to independently design an accessible route or determine code compliance.

## 2.6 Edge protection: guardrails, handrails, chair stops, closures

Guardrails and related edge/access components are safety components. StageRight's guardrails attach to its staging decks using a specific clamping system and are 42 inches high. Wenger StageTek likewise publishes dedicated guardrail assemblies and identifies specific IBC-related performance features.

Sources:

- StageRight — Guardrails  
  https://performance.stageright.com/products/accessories/guardrails/
- Wenger — StageTek Guardrails  
  https://shop.wengercorp.com/education/stagetekr-guardrails.html

A Course 2 learner should understand:

- an exposed edge may be an incomplete condition;
- the correct rail/handrail must match the approved system and configuration;
- edge protection should not be removed, relocated, or improvised independently;
- a visually complete deck is not necessarily a finished or accepted stage.

The learner should **report missing or questionable edge/access protection rather than decide whether it is legally sufficient**.

## 2.7 Finish/accessories

Skirting, closure panels, chair stops, scenic fascia, and similar accessories may not be primary structural members, but they can affect access, visibility, trip exposure, falling-object exposure, or use of the system. They should be installed only according to the assigned plan and system instructions.

StageRight lists skirting, guardrails, chair stops, ramps, and stairs as distinct accessories across its staging systems; Wenger publishes closure-panel and brace instructions for StageTek.

# 3. Correct Course 2 build-sequence mental model

The learner should not memorize a universal "Step 1 / Step 2 / Step 3" stage-building recipe. Manufacturer evidence shows that support and connection architectures vary too much.

Instead, teach a transferable **dependency model**:

1. **Identify the approved system and build intent.**
2. **Confirm the assigned work zone and component family.**
3. **Stage materials in the order the supervised build needs them.**
4. **Establish the support/base layer according to the specific system and lead direction.**
5. **Place/connect deck sections only as assigned.**
6. **Install required system-specific bracing/connectors/access components under direction.**
7. **Recognize obvious fit, alignment, completeness, and condition problems.**
8. **Stop and report mismatches instead of improvising.**
9. **Leave final inspection/acceptance to the designated responsible authority.**

This is a **Crew Blueprint curriculum recommendation synthesized from manufacturer and safety evidence**, not a universal manufacturer assembly procedure.

The key learning principle is:

> **Follow the current system's approved procedure; do not generalize yesterday's hardware to today's stage.**

# 4. Layout, sorting, and material-flow coordination

Course 2 should meaningfully advance beyond carrying pieces by teaching learners how a build consumes components.

A junior worker can reasonably learn to:

- read labels and basic plan references;
- distinguish deck sizes/types and support-component families;
- sort stairs, rails, frames, legs, braces, connectors, and finish parts by area or build phase;
- stage materials close enough to be useful without obstructing aisles or creating unstable stacks;
- preserve emergency and material-handling paths;
- avoid mixing hardware between systems or component families;
- keep damaged/questionable components separate and report them;
- understand that poor staging can create repeated handling, congestion, and unnecessary lifting.

OSHA's general material-handling rules require aisles and passageways to remain clear and stored material to be stable and secure against sliding or collapse. Construction storage rules similarly require materials to be stacked/racked/blocked/interlocked or otherwise secured, and require safe load limits not to be exceeded.

Sources:

- OSHA 29 CFR 1910.176 — Handling materials, general  
  https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.176
- OSHA 29 CFR 1926.250 — General requirements for storage  
  https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.250

**Curriculum implication:** material flow is not merely "organization." It is part of safe build coordination.

# 5. Level, plumb, square, alignment, stability, and connection awareness

These concepts should be taught as **recognition cues**, not independent acceptance authority.

## Level

Learners should understand that an intended horizontal platform may require leveling and that adjustable support systems exist to accommodate floor/terrain variation. They may help perform assigned adjustments under direct supervision on the specific system, but Course 2 should not authorize them to invent shims/support methods or declare the platform accepted.

## Plumb / vertical alignment

Where vertical frames, legs, rails, or scenic support components are intended to be vertical, visibly leaning or displaced components can be a cue to stop and ask. The learner does not need to determine structural tolerance.

## Square / layout alignment

A learner should understand that deck grids and scenic layouts depend on consistent geometry and that a cumulative alignment error can create downstream connection problems. Their role is to notice mismatch and report it rather than force components together.

## Stability

A deck that rocks, a support that is not fully bearing, an unexpected gap, missing brace, damaged connector, or assembly that moves when it should not are all escalation cues.

## Connection verification

Course 2 can teach the worker to visually confirm that the connection expected by the current system appears present and fully engaged **according to the training they have received for that product**. It should not teach them to certify structural adequacy.

This distinction mirrors OSHA's broader competent-person framework: frequent jobsite/material/equipment inspections may be assigned to competent persons designated by the employer, while qualified persons are defined by demonstrated specialized knowledge/training/experience.

Sources:

- OSHA 29 CFR 1926.20(b)(2)-(4) — inspections and qualified operators  
  https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.20
- OSHA 29 CFR 1910.21 — definition of qualified  
  https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.21

# 6. Manual handling and team movement

Staging components are often large, bulky, awkward, or difficult to grip even when a single piece is not exceptionally heavy. Course 2 should therefore frame manual handling around **control and coordination**, not strength.

NIOSH identifies lifting, pushing, pulling, carrying irregular objects, awkward posture, twisting, and bulky loads as musculoskeletal risk factors. OSHA's materials-handling eTool recommends keeping loads in a more favorable lifting zone where possible and using multiple people or mechanical assistance for heavier loads.

Sources:

- NIOSH — About Ergonomics and Work-Related Musculoskeletal Disorders  
  https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/ergonomics/about/index.html
- NIOSH — Step 1: Identify Risk Factors  
  https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/ergonomics/ergo-programs/risk-factors.html
- NIOSH — Ergonomic Guidelines for Manual Material Handling  
  https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/docs/2007-131/
- OSHA — Materials Handling: Heavy Lifting  
  https://www.osha.gov/etools/electrical-contractors/materials-handling/heavy

Course 2 should reinforce:

- route and destination awareness before movement;
- enough people/mechanical help for the shape and control demands;
- coordinated starts/stops/set-downs;
- keeping hands and feet away from mating edges and support points;
- avoiding uncontrolled twisting while carrying;
- not catching or stopping a shifting component with the body;
- stopping if control is lost.

These are **safe-handling principles**, not permission for learners to undertake a particular hazardous lift without onsite supervision.

# 7. Hazard model for supervised staging builds

The course should organize hazards around **how the assembly changes state**.

## 7.1 Pinch and crush exposure

Typical exposure points include mating deck edges, folding frames, adjustable legs, casters, stairs, rail interfaces, supports being lowered, and components being positioned against fixed surfaces.

Course 2 should teach the learner to identify the zone where two objects can close together and keep body parts out of that zone.

OSHA requires training on crush/pinch points in applicable equipment contexts and broadly requires workers to be instructed in recognition and avoidance of unsafe conditions.

Sources:

- OSHA 29 CFR 1926.1430(e) — crush/pinch point training in covered crane-equipment work  
  https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.1430
- OSHA 29 CFR 1926.21(b)(2) — hazard recognition/avoidance instruction  
  https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.21

The crane standard does **not** make stage assembly crane work; it is supporting evidence for the training principle that pinch/crush zones require explicit recognition.

## 7.2 Falling objects

Tools, hardware, components, and materials near elevated edges can create falling-object exposure. OSHA requires falling-object protection in applicable walking-working-surface situations and recognizes barricading/exclusion as one available control in relevant circumstances.

Source:

- OSHA 29 CFR 1910.28(c) — falling-object protection  
  https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.28

## 7.3 Incomplete structure exposure

A partially assembled platform may look usable before all supports, rails, stairs, braces, connectors, or acceptance steps are complete. Course 2 should explicitly teach:

> **Incomplete does not mean temporarily usable.**

Do not climb onto or place loads on an incomplete assembly unless the responsible lead has specifically established that the current state is intended and safe for the assigned work.

## 7.4 Fall exposure

OSHA fall-protection requirements vary by industry classification, task, walking-working surface, and specific standard. General-industry rules include a specific exception for exposed entertainment-stage perimeters, while other walking-working surfaces and construction contexts use different thresholds and requirements.

Sources:

- OSHA 29 CFR 1910.28  
  https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.28
- OSHA 29 CFR 1926.501  
  https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.501

**Curriculum implication:** do not teach learners a simplistic universal "X feet always requires a rail" rule for all stage work. Teach them to follow the employer/site fall-protection plan and to recognize unprotected elevation as a condition requiring awareness and possibly escalation.

# 9. Hardware substitution and unauthorized modification

Course 2 should explicitly teach that "close enough" hardware is not an acceptable reasoning model for a modular structural system.

Examples of unacceptable junior-level decisions include:

- replacing a missing proprietary connector with a visually similar fastener;
- drilling a new hole to make a part fit;
- omitting a brace because the platform feels stable;
- changing leg/support spacing;
- adding an improvised shim/support not specified by the system/lead;
- modifying a guardrail, stair, or ramp interface;
- mixing components from different staging systems without approved direction;
- forcing damaged or misaligned parts together.

This is a Crew Blueprint curriculum boundary supported by the product-specific nature of current staging systems and OSHA's general prohibition on unsafe/noncompliant equipment.

Source:

- OSHA 29 CFR 1926.20(b)(3) — noncompliant machinery/tools/material/equipment must not be used  
  https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.20

A junior worker encountering missing/incorrect hardware should **stop and escalate to the lead, manufacturer-qualified installer, competent/qualified person, engineer, or other designated authority as applicable**.

# 10. Structural-load boundary

Course 2 should teach **load awareness without load engineering**.

Learners should understand:

- stages/platforms have intended load limits;
- concentrated loads can matter differently from evenly distributed loads;
- supports, braces, connections, decks, stairs, rails, and terrain/floor conditions are part of one system;
- equipment placement can affect the structure;
- a stage may have approved zones or configurations;
- loads should not be added simply because there is physical space.

They should **not** calculate or approve:

- allowable platform loads;
- point loads;
- distributed loads;
- leg/support reactions;
- bearing pressure;
- ballast;
- anchors;
- wind loads;
- overturning/sliding resistance;
- temporary-structure structural design;
- changes to engineered layouts.

ANSI E1.21-2024 is explicitly a design/performance standard for temporary outdoor entertainment structures. Its existence reinforces that these decisions belong to a higher technical/design authority than a junior Course 2 learner.

Source:

- ESTA TSP — ANSI E1.21-2024  
  https://tsp.esta.org/tsp/documents/published_docs.php

OSHA's construction storage standard also provides a useful general principle that stored material must not exceed safe floor load limits.

Source:

- OSHA 29 CFR 1926.250(a)(2)  
  https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.250

# 11. Inspection awareness vs. inspection authority

A Course 2 learner should be trained to **notice and report**, not certify.

## Appropriate junior observation cues

- visibly bent/cracked/deformed components;
- damaged deck surface or edge;
- missing expected connector/brace;
- visibly unseated connection;
- unexpected gap or interference;
- support not bearing as expected;
- obvious rocking or instability;
- missing stair/rail/access component shown in the plan;
- wrong component family or hardware present;
- blocked access/egress created by material staging;
- component does not match current build documentation;
- assembly has been altered since the last direction/check.

## Not a junior Course 2 determination

- whether a damaged component remains structurally acceptable;
- whether a brace may be omitted;
- whether a support spacing change is acceptable;
- whether a substitute fastener is equivalent;
- whether an anchor/ballast layout is adequate;
- whether a platform is code compliant;
- whether a completed structure is accepted for occupancy/use;
- whether an engineered drawing can be deviated from.

OSHA construction rules assign frequent and regular jobsite/material/equipment inspections to competent persons designated by employers. OSHA also defines "qualified" in terms of specialized demonstrated knowledge/training/experience.

Sources:

- OSHA 29 CFR 1926.20(b)(2)  
  https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.20
- OSHA 29 CFR 1910.21  
  https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.21

These definitions should not be used to imply that every live-event staging build is governed by the same OSHA subpart; they are useful to preserve the distinction between **worker observation** and **designated inspection/technical authority**.

# 12. Powered machinery, lifts, saws, fabrication, and welding boundary

Course 2 should not become a powered-tool/fabrication qualification course.

OSHA requires tools and equipment to be maintained safely, requires guarding on power tools where designed, contains specific woodworking-tool guarding requirements, and permits only workers qualified by training or experience to operate machinery/equipment in applicable construction contexts.

Sources:

- OSHA 29 CFR 1926.20(b)(4)  
  https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.20
- OSHA 29 CFR 1926.300 — general tool requirements  
  https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.300
- OSHA 29 CFR 1926.301 — hand tools  
  https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.301
- OSHA 29 CFR 1926.302 — power-operated hand tools  
  https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.302
- OSHA 29 CFR 1926.304 — woodworking tools  
  https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.304

The higher-level boundary should therefore include:

- saw operation and woodworking machinery;
- fabrication and field modification;
- welding/cutting;
- powered lifts/aerial work platforms;
- automated staging/machinery;
- powered material-handling equipment;
- structural repairs;
- specialized anchoring or drilling;
- any operation requiring employer-specific authorization or separate qualification.

Course 2 may teach learners to recognize these operations, maintain exclusion/clearance as instructed, and understand that separate training/authorization is required.

# 13. Troubleshooting and escalation model

Troubleshooting in Course 2 should mean **finding the mismatch**, not redesigning the system.

A useful troubleshooting sequence is:

> **What should be here? → What is actually here? → Is it the correct component/system? → Is the condition visibly complete and undamaged? → Does it match the current plan/direction? → If not, stop and escalate.**

## Missing component

Action: stop that dependency, identify what is missing, tell the lead.  
Do not substitute or omit it independently.

## Misalignment

Action: confirm component identity/orientation and build reference, then ask before forcing or altering anything.

## Damaged hardware/component

Action: remove it from the active build path if instructed, mark/segregate according to employer procedure, and escalate. Do not decide structural acceptability.

## Unstable assembly

Action: stop loading/using/moving onto the questionable portion and alert the lead. Do not "test" stability by adding weight or body force.

## Incorrect placement

Action: compare to the approved layout/task direction and escalate before continuing dependent work.

## Incomplete connection

Action: stop the next dependent step and get the assigned responsible person to verify the connection method.

## Conflict with plan or manufacturer system

Action: the plan/system wins over memory. Escalate rather than adapting the structure independently.

This is the central Course 2 diagnostic habit:

> **Detect → isolate → communicate → wait for authorized correction.**

# 14. Recommended three-module Course 2 architecture

The original CA-002 queue item does not mandate an exact module count, but the material organizes cleanly into three instructional modules.

## Module 1 — Staging Systems, Components & Build Logic

### Goal
Understand a stage/platform as a system and identify the parts and dependencies.

### Core lessons

1. Stage system anatomy: site, supports, decks, bracing, access, edge protection, finish
2. Decks, frames, legs, risers, stairs, ramps, guardrails, braces, connectors, skirting
3. Why staging systems differ by manufacturer
4. Reading basic layout/build intent without engineering it
5. Material sorting and staging for workflow
6. Build dependencies: what has to exist before the next piece can be added
7. What "complete" does and does not mean

### Assessment
Component recognition, plan-to-component matching, and dependency-order scenarios.

---

## Module 2 — Supervised Assembly, Handling & Verification Awareness

### Goal
Participate safely in assigned modular-stage assembly and recognize obvious problems.

### Core lessons

1. Role of manufacturer instructions and supervisor direction
2. Manual handling and coordinated movement
3. Pinch/crush zones and changing assembly states
4. Level/plumb/square/alignment as recognition concepts
5. Connection/brace completeness awareness
6. Stairs, ramps, rails, and access as system components
7. Stable material staging and housekeeping
8. Inspection awareness vs. inspection authority

### Assessment
"Proceed / stop / escalate" scenarios and visual identification of obvious incomplete conditions.

---

## Module 3 — Build Coordination, Troubleshooting & Authority Boundaries

### Goal
Understand how to keep a build moving without freelancing structural decisions.

### Core lessons

1. Detecting a mismatch between plan and reality
2. Missing/damaged/wrong components
3. Hardware substitution: why "close enough" is not acceptable
4. Structural-load awareness without calculation
5. Temporary outdoor structures and why engineering exists
6. Powered machinery/fabrication/lift boundaries
7. Escalation chain: lead → competent/qualified/design authority as applicable
8. Final acceptance belongs to the designated authority

### Assessment
Integrated scenario in which the learner must identify several mismatches, decide which work can continue, and escalate the rest.

# 16. Assessment design without false qualification

Course 2 assessment should test **mental models and judgment**, not simulate professional authorization.

Recommended assessment types:

## 16.1 Component/system recognition

Ask learners to identify what category a component belongs to and what system function it serves.

## 16.2 Dependency ordering

Given an approved simplified build diagram, ask which component/work package logically must precede another. Avoid asking learners to derive their own structure.

## 16.3 Visual defect/mismatch recognition

Show obvious examples such as missing expected components, wrong component family, blocked egress, visible damage, or an incomplete connection and ask whether to continue or escalate.

## 16.4 Authority-boundary questions

Examples:

- A brace shown in the approved plan is missing. What do you do?
- A bolt looks almost identical but has a different marking. Can you substitute it?
- A deck will not align without forcing it. What is the correct next action?
- Someone wants to place heavy equipment in an unplanned location. Who decides whether the stage can carry it?

Correct reasoning should consistently favor **verify, stop, and escalate** over improvisation.

## 16.5 Integrated build-coordination scenario

Learners can be asked to coordinate materials and identify issues on a fictional build without calculating loads or designing the structure.

### Required course language

Passing should mean:

> The learner demonstrated Course 2 knowledge of staging/carpentry build coordination and supervised participation concepts.

It should **not** mean:

- certified stage builder;
- qualified carpenter;
- competent person;
- structural inspector;
- engineer;
- authorized lift/operator;
- authorized fabricator;
- authorized to accept a stage for use.

# 17. Established guidance vs. Crew Blueprint recommendations vs. variable practice

## Established / authoritative guidance

- ANSI E1.21-2024 covers design/performance parameters for temporary ground-supported outdoor entertainment structures.
- OSHA requires stable material storage and clear aisles/passages in applicable material-handling contexts.
- OSHA requires safe tool condition, guarding, and appropriate training/qualification in applicable tool/machinery contexts.
- OSHA distinguishes competent/qualified responsibilities in various regulated contexts.
- Manufacturer staging systems use product-specific supports, bracing, decks, rails, stairs, ramps, and connection methods.
- NIOSH identifies force, awkward posture, twisting, carrying, and bulky-load handling as musculoskeletal risk factors.

## Crew Blueprint curriculum recommendations

- Teach the system as: **site → support → deck → connection/bracing → access → edge protection → finish → acceptance**.
- Teach a transferable dependency model instead of one universal stage-building recipe.
- Teach level/plumb/square/alignment as awareness cues rather than acceptance criteria.
- Teach troubleshooting as **detect → isolate → communicate → wait for authorized correction**.
- Make hardware substitution, structural modification, load decisions, and final acceptance explicit hard boundaries.
- Use scenario assessment centered on judgment and escalation.

## Practices that vary

- actual deck/support geometry;
- connector type;
- required tools;
- bracing method;
- rail/stair/ramp attachment;
- leveling method;
- component naming;
- who performs particular assembly steps;
- whether work falls under general-industry, construction, venue, local-code, union, manufacturer, or engineered-event procedures;
- authority chain for inspection/acceptance;
- PPE and fall-protection plan;
- employer training/authorization requirements.

The course should repeatedly tell learners:

> **The current manufacturer's instructions, approved build documentation, employer procedures, site rules, and designated authority control the actual job.**

# 18. Final answer to the decision question

## What is the correct Course 2 mental model for participating in staging/carpentry builds and load coordination without crossing into structural authority?

The learner should think like a **supervised system participant**, not a junior engineer.

Their mental model should be:

> **Identify the system. Understand the approved build intent. Keep the correct materials flowing to the correct place. Perform only the assembly tasks you have been assigned and trained to perform. Watch for obvious damage, misalignment, missing components, incomplete connections, unstable-looking conditions, blocked access, or deviations from the plan. Never solve structural mismatches by improvising. Stop, communicate, and let the designated technical authority decide the correction and final acceptance.**

That is a meaningful advancement from Course 1 without falsely claiming structural, supervisory, competent-person, engineering, or fabrication qualification.

# Evidence-quality note

The strongest evidence in this package comes from current ESTA standards listings, OSHA regulations/guidance, NIOSH ergonomics guidance, and current manufacturer documentation. Manufacturer pages are authoritative for their own products but should not be generalized into universal staging procedures. OSHA standards cited here span multiple regulatory contexts; the package uses them carefully for hazard/authority principles and does not claim that every cited provision applies identically to every live-event stage build. Actual applicability depends on the employer, task, industry classification, jurisdiction, venue, and work being performed.
