# Research Package 07 — Ground-Hand Support for Lighting Departments

**Research date:** 2026-08-15  
**Scope:** U.S. live-events and theatrical production, with examples from arenas, theaters, festivals, and corporate-event environments.

## Research question

What does a general stagehand / ground hand — **not a lighting technician** — actually do to support a lighting department during load-in, hang, trim, and focus, and where should the boundary be drawn between useful support and lighting-specific technical work?

---

# Executive conclusion

The evidence supports a clear operating principle:

> **A ground hand supports the physical workflow of the lighting department under direction; the lighting department retains responsibility for technical configuration, focus, electrical decisions, patch/addressing, troubleshooting, and show control.**

PRG’s current crew portal explicitly distinguishes **Stagehands** from Lighting Technician, Lighting Network Technician, Lighting Desk Operator, Lighting Designer, and Lighting Crew Chief. PRG states that an experienced stagehand may, **under guidance**, connect lights and assemble truss, while its Lighting Crew Chief is expected to plan, execute, and monitor lighting-specific requirements and possess substantial lighting/electrical knowledge. This is one of the clearest current employer statements of the support boundary.

**Sources:**
- PRG Crew Portal — Stagehand and Lighting classifications: https://crew.prg.com/en/
- PRG Crew Portal French/German mirrors contain the same stagehand wording and classification split: https://crew.prg.com/fr/ and https://crew.prg.com/de/

IATSE Local 114 similarly describes lighting work as including **assembling truss, running cable to power lights, assisting in pre-focus while fixtures are still on deck, and setting up FOH lighting control**. Local 193’s stagehand lighting material describes the load-in sequence as arranging distro/dimmer equipment, building truss/electrics, running power/data cable, flying the system to trim, focusing, and cleanup — while assigning technical oversight to the Head Electrician.

**Sources:**
- IATSE Local 114, Positions — Lighting and Sound: https://www.ia114.com/positions
- IATSE Local 193, Lighting Basics: https://www.iatselocal193.com/lighting-basics

These sources support teaching ground hands to be useful in the **physical build, movement, cable, housekeeping, and directed-assistance portions of the workflow** without presenting them as electricians or lighting technicians.

---

# 1. Tasks a ground hand can reasonably be assigned

## 1.1 Truss assembly assistance

This is strongly supported.

PRG states that stagehands with experience may, **under guidance**, screw/assemble truss. IATSE Local 122’s 2026 Stagehand Skills Primer treats **truss assembly** as a core stagehand competency and describes it as learning to identify truss components and follow safe assembly practices.

**Sources:**
- PRG Crew Portal: https://crew.prg.com/en/
- IATSE Local 122, Stagehand Skills Primer: https://www.iatse122.org/event/primer-20-stagehand-skills-primer-3/

### Curriculum interpretation

Ground hands can be taught to:

- identify common truss sections and hardware;
- move and stage truss where directed;
- assist with assembly/disassembly under the lighting/rigging lead’s direction;
- keep hardware organized;
- understand that truss used overhead becomes part of a safety-critical system and therefore requires inspection/approval by qualified responsible personnel.

Do **not** turn this into instruction on designing rigging systems, determining load paths, choosing hang points, or approving flown assemblies.

---

## 1.2 Un-casing, staging, and re-casing fixtures

This is consistent with normal stagehand/material-handling work, though fewer employers publish this exact task as a standalone title.

IATSE Local 122 explicitly includes best practices for handling heavy and delicate production equipment in its introductory stagehand training. PRG’s stagehand classification includes working under guidance with lights, and IATSE Local 114 places stagehands within the lighting setup workflow.

**Sources:**
- IATSE Local 122, Stagehand Skills Primer: https://www.iatse122.org/event/primer-20-stagehand-skills-primer-3/
- PRG Crew Portal: https://crew.prg.com/en/
- IATSE Local 114, Positions: https://www.ia114.com/positions

### Curriculum interpretation

Ground hands may be assigned to:

- open cases when directed;
- remove fixtures carefully using approved handles/lift points;
- stage fixtures in plot/order as instructed;
- keep lids, foam, accessories, clamps, safety cables, and case labels together;
- re-case fixtures in the correct case/slot during load-out.

Fixture-specific handling details are **manufacturer- and model-specific**. ETC’s fixture documentation, for example, identifies a fixture handle as an approved carrying/focus point while separately identifying safety-cable attachment points. This supports a general teaching rule: **use intended handles and attachment points; do not improvise by grabbing optical assemblies, cabling, or adjustment mechanisms.**

**Source:** ETC Source Four LED Series 3 fixture setup: https://www.etcconnect.com/webdocs/Fixtures/SourceFourLED_Series3/Content/S4S3/Overview_S4S3.htm

---

## 1.3 Running and dressing lighting cable under direction

This is strongly supported.

IATSE Local 114 identifies running cable to power lights as lighting-call work. IATSE Local 193 describes running power and data cable to fixtures, including looms. PRG states that stagehands may connect lights under guidance. Encore’s rigging descriptions also distinguish cable-running/dressing as a directed trade task within the larger hang workflow.

**Sources:**
- IATSE Local 114: https://www.ia114.com/positions
- IATSE Local 193: https://www.iatselocal193.com/lighting-basics
- PRG Crew Portal: https://crew.prg.com/en/
- Encore Event Rigger role, 2026: https://jobs.encoreglobal.com/en/job/mississauga/event-rigger-toronto/6228/95423040032

IATSE Local 122’s 2026 primer specifically calls **over-under cable wrapping** a core stagehand skill and connects correct coiling with cable longevity and efficient deployment.

**Source:** IATSE Local 122 Stagehand Skills Primer: https://www.iatse122.org/event/primer-20-stagehand-skills-primer-3/

### Curriculum interpretation

A ground hand can learn to:

- identify cable by type/label at an awareness level;
- carry, deploy, and retrieve cable where instructed;
- maintain clean routing and avoid creating trip/pinch hazards;
- coil cable in the method required by the employer/department;
- leave patching, addressing, power-system decisions, troubleshooting, and final routing approval to the lighting lead/electrician.

The exact coiling method can vary by cable type and employer practice. Over-under is widely taught for many entertainment cables, but Crew Blueprint should avoid claiming that every cable is always handled identically.

---

## 1.4 Holding/passing tools and supporting elevated workers

This is a legitimate support task when assigned.

Encore’s current Event Rigger description explicitly includes **staging equipment under work points, handing up chain/tools/equipment, and spotting lift movements** as ground support. This is a rigging-role posting rather than a generic stagehand posting, so the exact task set should not be generalized to every hand. It does, however, document that ground support around elevated entertainment work commonly includes material/tool support and spotting under qualified supervision.

**Source:** Encore Event Rigger, 2026: https://jobs.encoreglobal.com/en/job/mississauga/event-rigger-toronto/6228/95423040032

### Curriculum boundary

Teach a general hand to:

- pass or receive tools only when instructed;
- keep the work area below/around elevated work organized;
- stay clear of exclusion zones and suspended loads;
- understand that lift spotting or hoist signaling can be a **qualified or employer-designated role**, not something to improvise because a worker happens to be standing nearby.

---

## 1.5 Ground support while truss/battens move to working height or trim

This is real, but the terminology and responsibility vary significantly by venue and lifting system.

IATSE Local 193 describes the Head Electrician checking the system and then flying lights to trim. ETC’s rigging-system material describes lighting battens moving between working height and focus/show positions. Encore’s rigging postings show trained rigging staff remaining involved until motors are raised to trim.

**Sources:**
- IATSE Local 193: https://www.iatselocal193.com/lighting-basics
- ETC rigging systems: https://www.etcconnect.com/products/rigging-systems/test.aspx
- Encore Event Rigger: https://jobs.encoreglobal.com/en/job/mississauga/event-rigger-toronto/6228/95423040032

### Curriculum interpretation

For an ordinary hand, the correct lesson is **how to behave during a move**, not how to operate the hoist/fly system:

- listen for the designated caller;
- maintain visibility/communication;
- keep clear of the moving system and suspended load;
- watch for obvious snags or cable conflicts and report them;
- stop work/escalate if communication is lost or an unsafe condition develops.

Do not teach an entry-level hand to operate motors, a counterweight fly system, or rigging control equipment without employer-specific training and authorization.

# 2. What a ground hand should generally NOT adjust independently

This is where the curriculum should be especially strict.

## 2.1 Fixture focus and optical adjustments

ETC identifies yoke tilt, beam-focus controls, shutters, barrel rotation, and optical accessories as fixture-focus/configuration controls. Its focusing documentation describes changing tilt and beam focus deliberately and warns about unintended fixture movement during adjustment.

**Sources:**
- ETC fixture setup: https://www.etcconnect.com/webdocs/Fixtures/SourceFourLED_Series3/Content/S4S3/Overview_S4S3.htm
- ETC, Focus the Fixture: https://www.etcconnect.com/webdocs/Fixtures/SourceFourLED_Series3/Content/CommonSoftware/Focus.htm

IATSE Local 822 places **handling and focusing lighting instruments** under the Head Electrician’s supervision.

**Source:** IATSE Local 822, People We Work With: https://iatse822.com/resources/people-we-work-with

### Teaching rule

A ground hand should **not independently change**:

- pan/tilt/focus;
- shutters;
- lens/barrel position;
- barn-door orientation;
- gel/color/media already installed;
- fixture position once the lighting department has set it;

unless the responsible lighting person explicitly assigns that action.

A fixture that looks “crooked” may be intentionally focused that way.

---

## 2.2 Addressing, patch, personality/mode, and console configuration

ETC documentation places DMX address/mode, fixture configuration, and patch functions within fixture/console setup workflows. PRG separates Lighting Technician, Lighting Network Technician, Lighting Desk Operator, and Lighting Crew Chief from Stagehand classifications.

**Sources:**
- ETC Source Four LED Series 3 setup: https://www.etcconnect.com/webdocs/Fixtures/SourceFourLED_Series3/Content/S4S3/Overview_S4S3.htm
- ETC Hang-to-Focus/Patch documentation: https://support.etcconnect.com/ETC/Consoles/Augment3d/Hang_to_Focus_Offsets
- PRG Crew Portal: https://crew.prg.com/en/

### Teaching rule

A general hand should not independently:

- change DMX address or mode;
- alter fixture profiles;
- repatch fixtures;
- change console settings;
- troubleshoot control/data systems by guessing;
- swap signal/power paths because they “look wrong.”

Report the issue to the lighting technician/lead.

---

## 2.3 Electrical distribution and feeder decisions

IATSE Local 193 explicitly states that feeder power is dangerous and should only be connected by qualified stagehands such as the Head Electrician in its described context. OSHA also distinguishes qualified from unqualified electrical workers and requires training appropriate to exposure.

**Source:** IATSE Local 193: https://www.iatselocal193.com/lighting-basics

### Teaching rule

Ground hands may be asked to **move or stage** cable and equipment, but Crew Blueprint should not teach them to independently energize, reconfigure, troubleshoot, or approve temporary power distribution.

Employer/local rules can differ on exactly which trained classifications are permitted to connect particular systems.

# 3. Common mistakes that damage gear or slow the hang

Direct employer incident lists are rarely public, so this section distinguishes **source-backed equipment facts** from **curriculum inference**.

## 3.1 Grabbing or carrying fixtures by adjustment/optical components

ETC identifies dedicated fixture handles and separate focus/adjustment controls. This supports a safe handling principle: carry using intended handles/lift points rather than lens tubes, shutters, cables, yoke locks, or other adjustment hardware.

**Source:** ETC Source Four LED Series 3 fixture setup: https://www.etcconnect.com/webdocs/Fixtures/SourceFourLED_Series3/Content/S4S3/Overview_S4S3.htm

**Curriculum inference:** Mishandling optical assemblies, yoke locks, barn doors, or fixture cabling can alter focus, damage parts, or make the lighting crew re-check equipment.

---

## 3.2 Touching lamps or optical components improperly

IATSE Local 193 specifically warns against direct skin contact with theatrical lamps because contamination can damage lamps in use.

**Source:** IATSE Local 193 Lighting Basics: https://www.iatselocal193.com/lighting-basics

This guidance is most directly applicable to lamp-based fixtures and should not be generalized as an identical rule for every modern LED fixture component.

---

## 3.3 Poor cable handling

IATSE Local 122 explicitly teaches proper coiling because incorrect handling causes tangles, slows deployment, and reduces cable life.

**Source:** IATSE Local 122 Stagehand Skills Primer: https://www.iatse122.org/event/primer-20-stagehand-skills-primer-3/

**Curriculum inference:** common support failures include mixing cable types/lengths, creating knots/twists, coiling against department practice, crossing paths unnecessarily, or moving a connected cable without checking with the department.

---

## 3.4 Moving or “fixing” an already-set fixture

Because fixture focus involves deliberate yoke/tilt, lens, shutter, and optical settings, unsolicited adjustment can destroy focus and create rework.

**Sources:**
- ETC fixture setup: https://www.etcconnect.com/webdocs/Fixtures/SourceFourLED_Series3/Content/S4S3/Overview_S4S3.htm
- ETC focus documentation: https://www.etcconnect.com/webdocs/Fixtures/SourceFourLED_Series3/Content/CommonSoftware/Focus.htm

### Strong curriculum phrase

> **If you did not set it, do not “correct” it because it looks odd. Ask the lighting lead.**

---

## 3.5 Missing a stop command or continuing after communication is lost

OSHA’s crane/derrick signal requirements provide an important safety analogue: signaling must remain effective; if communication is interrupted, operations requiring signals stop until communication is re-established. Only one designated signal person normally gives operational signals, while anyone who recognizes a safety problem can give a stop/emergency-stop signal.

**Source:** OSHA 29 CFR 1926.1419: https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.1419

This OSHA rule is written for cranes/derricks in construction and should **not be misrepresented as the universal legal standard for every entertainment chain-hoist operation**. It is useful evidence for the broader communication principle: one clear caller, agreed signals, immediate stop on confusion.

# 4. Hand signals and verbal communication

## 4.1 What is genuinely standardized

OSHA publishes a Standard Method of hand signals for cranes/derricks, including **STOP, EMERGENCY STOP, HOIST, LOWER, MOVE SLOWLY**, and other commands.

**Source:** OSHA Standard Hand Signals, Appendix A to Subpart CC: https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926SubpartCCAppA

OSHA further requires that non-standard signals be agreed upon in advance by the relevant operator/signal personnel when its crane standard applies.

**Source:** OSHA 1926.1419: https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.1419

OSHA voice-signal rules use the pattern **function → direction → distance/speed → stop command** when that standard applies.

**Source:** OSHA 1926.1421: https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.1421

## 4.2 What is NOT established as a universal entertainment-lighting signal code

The research did **not** find a single U.S.-wide entertainment-industry hand-signal vocabulary specifically governing ordinary lighting truss/chain-hoist trim calls across all arenas, theaters, festivals, and corporate shows.

Companies, locals, venues, and rigging systems can use their own established calls and procedures.

### Curriculum implication

Teach:

- recognize **STOP** as universal safety intent;
- identify the designated caller before movement begins;
- use only the signal vocabulary established by the crew/employer for that operation;
- never invent or relay ambiguous signals;
- if visual/verbal communication becomes unclear, stop and re-establish communication.

Do **not** teach a homemade “Crew Blueprint motor signal system” as if it were an industry standard.

# 5. PPE and safety for ground hands supporting lighting hangs

## 5.1 Overhead-work awareness

The major ground-hand hazard is not simply “lighting equipment”; it is working near **moving and suspended systems, elevated workers, tools, truss, cable, and fixtures**.

OSHA steel-erection rules require routes for suspended loads to be planned so workers generally are not required directly beneath them except for limited necessary activities. Although that rule is construction-specific, it reinforces the established safety principle of minimizing exposure beneath suspended loads.

**Source:** OSHA 1926.753, Hoisting and Rigging: https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.753

Encore’s entertainment rigging postings similarly emphasize safety checks, qualified riggers, lift certification, proper truss protection, and remaining involved through trim.

**Source:** Encore Event Rigger, 2026: https://jobs.encoreglobal.com/en/job/mississauga/event-rigger-toronto/6228/95423040032

## 5.2 Hard hats and site PPE

Specific PPE requirements vary by employer/site and hazard assessment. IATSE Local 53’s current safety library explicitly includes guidance on **Appropriate Clothing & Personal Protective Equipment**, lighting/electrical systems, lift safety, and rigging standards.

**Source:** IATSE Local 53 Safety Library: https://www.iatse53.org/safety/

### Curriculum implication

For ground-hand lighting support, teach workers to expect site-required PPE such as:

- hard hat where overhead-work policy requires it;
- work gloves appropriate to material handling;
- protective footwear;
- high-visibility apparel where required by the site;
- eye/hearing protection when identified by the task/site.

The exact PPE set is **site- and employer-specific**. Crew Blueprint should teach compliance with the call’s PPE requirements and hazard assessment rather than presenting one universal kit as legally sufficient everywhere.

# 6. Arena, theater, festival, and corporate-event differences

## Arena / festival

More likely to involve:

- large truss systems;
- touring packages;
- extensive cable looms;
- local stagehands working under touring lighting/rigging leads;
- motorized trim operations;
- heavy interaction between lighting, rigging, video, staging, and truck-load workflows.

PRG’s stagehand model is especially relevant here because it explicitly describes stagehands supporting lights/truss **under guidance** while separating lighting-specialist classifications.

## Theater

More likely to involve:

- fixed battens/electrics;
- house circuits and dimming/control infrastructure;
- conventional fixtures alongside moving/LED fixtures;
- fly-system interfaces;
- focus calls with a Head Electrician/lighting team.

IATSE Local 193 and Local 822 provide strong theater-oriented examples of this hierarchy.

## Corporate / convention

More likely to combine lighting support with broader AV setup. Encore’s venue model shows technicians and riggers working within hospitality/convention-center SOPs, with strong emphasis on cable routing, truss, lifts, and coordination among trades.

The division between “stagehand” and “AV technician” may be less rigid at some employers than on a large concert tour. Company-specific SOPs matter.

# 7. Recommended Crew Blueprint teaching boundary

## Teach the ground hand to DO under direction

- move and stage truss/fixtures/cable;
- assist with truss assembly where assigned;
- open/repack cases carefully;
- deploy/retrieve cable using department practice;
- maintain clean work areas and routes;
- pass tools/materials when instructed;
- observe moving systems for obvious snags/conflicts and report them;
- follow the designated caller and stop on confusion;
- protect already-set equipment from accidental movement;
- ask before touching anything that appears configured, patched, focused, or energized.

## Teach the ground hand to RECOGNIZE but not independently perform

- fixture focus;
- DMX addressing/modes;
- patch;
- console/network configuration;
- temporary electrical distribution decisions;
- rigging-point selection;
- load calculations;
- motor/fly-system operation;
- qualified signal-person duties;
- technical troubleshooting beyond obvious reportable conditions.

## Teaching principle

> **Support the workflow. Preserve the setup. Follow the department lead. Do not diagnose, reconfigure, or “fix” lighting systems unless that task has been explicitly assigned within your training and authorization.**

# 8. Confidence / standardization notes

### Strongly supported across sources

- stagehands can assist with truss, cable, and lighting equipment under guidance;
- lighting-specialist roles remain distinct from stagehand roles;
- proper cable handling is a foundational stagehand skill;
- focus/configuration belongs under lighting leadership;
- overhead work requires controlled communication and safety boundaries;
- exact procedures depend on venue/company/local.

### Employer/local-specific or context-dependent

- whether a hand plugs individual fixtures;
- whether a hand assembles particular truss systems;
- what PPE is mandatory beyond baseline site rules;
- whether a worker may serve as lift spotter or signal person;
- exact verbal/hand signals used for entertainment hoists;
- how feeder/power tasks are allocated among trained classifications;
- how much “pre-focus” a hand is permitted to perform.

### Not supported as a universal industry rule

- one standardized national entertainment-lighting hand-signal system;
- one universal definition of “lighting hand”;
- a rule that all stagehands may connect all lighting power/data;
- a rule that all ground hands participate in focus.

# Bottom line

For Crew Blueprint, lighting support should be taught as a **department-support competency**, not a miniature lighting-technician course.

The useful ground hand is the person who can safely move, stage, assemble, route, protect, observe, communicate, and follow direction while leaving **focus, power, addressing, patch, control, troubleshooting, and rigging authority** to the people assigned and qualified to own those systems.
