# 39 — Production Department Category: Stage Management & Coordination

**Research Queue ID:** CD-003  
**Status:** Complete research packet  
**Research date:** 2026-08-17  
**Depends on:** CB-009 — Event Operations / Supervisor Tier  
**Purpose:** Define whether Crew Blueprint should create a production/coordination category distinct from technical departments and from venue operations.

---

## Decision question

**How should stage management, production management, event management, and cross-department coordination fit into the Crew Blueprint ecosystem?**

## Bottom-line finding

Crew Blueprint should create a **Production & Coordination** career branch, but it should not flatten several distinct professions into one promotion ladder.

The evidence supports three related but different functions:

1. **Stage management** — production process, rehearsal/performance coordination, cues, performer/crew communication, paperwork, and show continuity.
2. **Production management** — technical advance, labor/resources, budgets, schedule, backstage/technical operations, vendors, and production-wide execution.
3. **Venue/event management** — client coordination, facility operations, staffing plans, security/public safety interfaces, guest operations, permits, event plans, settlement/billing, and venue readiness.

These functions frequently interact and may overlap in small organizations. In larger venues and tours, they are clearly separable roles.

The correct Crew Blueprint architecture is therefore a **cross-functional production branch with parallel specialties**, not a single vertical ladder above stagehand or department lead.

---

# 1. Why this needs its own category

Existing Crew Blueprint research established that department leads are responsible for the outcome of a defined technical system or crew. CB-009 then distinguished that from production/event supervision, where responsibility spans multiple departments and operational interfaces.

Current venue job descriptions reinforce the distinction.

IAVM-listed Event Manager roles coordinate clients, venue departments, vendors, staffing, equipment, budgets, public-safety needs, and event execution from load-in through load-out. An Associate Event Production Manager at Ohio State combines technical production oversight with staffing, guest-services/security coordination, payroll/training records, event documentation, and client liaison work. A current Production Manager posting for Sangamon Auditorium assigns event production management, technical advancement, staff supervision, budgeting, equipment maintenance, backstage operations, and safety-policy responsibilities.

These are not merely "senior stagehand" duties.

---

# 2. Stage management remains a distinct discipline

The Stage Managers' Association describes stage management as a professional craft with its own community, education, mentoring, advocacy, and career recognition.

Crew Blueprint's earlier Package 05 found a real theatrical progression such as:

**production assistant / early-career support → assistant stage manager → stage manager → production stage manager**

That is a stage-management progression, not a general-stagehand seniority ladder.

A stagehand can move into stage management, but the worker is changing disciplines and building new competencies.

### Core stage-management competencies

A stage-management curriculum should emphasize:

- rehearsal/performance documentation;
- production communication;
- call/cue discipline;
- change tracking;
- performer/crew coordination;
- backstage traffic and readiness;
- safety communication and escalation;
- maintaining show continuity;
- confidentiality/discretion;
- production paperwork and information control.

It should not imply that stage managers automatically possess authority to redesign rigging, power, audio, video, or structural systems.

---

# 3. Production management is cross-technical integration

Production managers are typically accountable for integrating technical requirements rather than replacing each department's craft authority.

Current IAVM production-management evidence includes responsibility for:

- technical advancement;
- backstage operations;
- staff supervision;
- budgets;
- equipment/resources;
- safety policy;
- touring production interface;
- event scheduling and execution.

The production manager therefore needs enough technical literacy to recognize dependencies and ask the right questions, while department heads retain specialist authority within their systems.

### Correct mental model

**production intent → advance → resource plan → department plans → schedule/dependencies → load-in coordination → readiness → show operations → strike → post-event closeout**

The production manager owns integration and constraint management across that cycle.

---

# 4. Venue/event management is broader than production management

Current IAVM Event Manager postings repeatedly add responsibilities that technical production managers may not own:

- primary client contact;
- facility policy and event-contract compliance;
- guest experience;
- security and public-safety coordination;
- parking and building operations;
- crowd-flow/occupancy awareness;
- permits;
- event diagrams;
- estimates and settlement;
- post-event reports;
- vendor management;
- staffing levels across multiple service providers.

This makes venue/event management a broader operational profession.

Crew Blueprint should not teach "Production Manager" and "Event Manager" as synonymous titles.

---

# 5. Recommended Production & Coordination branch

## Track A — Stage Management

### Foundation

- backstage vocabulary;
- chain of communication;
- production paperwork;
- rehearsal/show process;
- cues and standby discipline;
- basic emergency communication.

### Intermediate

- ASM-level task ownership;
- scene/changeover coordination;
- deck traffic;
- tracking props/costumes/performers;
- report writing;
- coordination with departments.

### Advanced

- stage-manager/PSM responsibilities;
- rehearsal leadership;
- cue systems;
- show continuity;
- production communication architecture;
- managing multiple assistants/decks;
- emergency and disruption response within role.

## Track B — Production Management

- technical advance;
- labor and equipment planning;
- schedule development;
- budget awareness;
- vendor coordination;
- cross-department dependencies;
- production documentation;
- safety interfaces;
- load-in/show/strike management;
- change control and escalation.

## Track C — Venue/Event Operations

- client advance;
- facility capabilities/limitations;
- guest/security/public-safety coordination;
- permits and event plans;
- staffing orders;
- venue diagrams;
- operations meetings;
- estimates/settlement;
- post-event reporting;
- continuous improvement.

---

# 6. Shared competencies

Although the tracks differ, all three require:

- clear written and verbal communication;
- accurate status reporting;
- schedule awareness;
- documentation discipline;
- role/authority boundaries;
- stakeholder management;
- anticipatory problem solving;
- escalation judgment;
- safety leadership appropriate to role;
- ability to coordinate people who have deeper craft expertise than the coordinator does.

This last point is critical.

> **Cross-functional authority is not the same as being the highest technical expert in every department.**

---

# 7. Authority model

Crew Blueprint should explicitly separate:

- **creative authority** — designer/director/artist decisions;
- **department technical authority** — technical leads/heads;
- **production coordination authority** — production manager/PSM/event leadership;
- **venue authority** — facility management and house rules;
- **safety/code authority** — employer, qualified persons, AHJ/public safety, etc.;
- **labor/union authority** — steward, hiring hall, agreement-specific functions.

Many event failures arise when a worker assumes one kind of authority implies all the others.

---

# 8. Curriculum boundary

A Production & Coordination course may teach learners to:

- build schedules and checklists;
- map dependencies;
- construct responsibility matrices;
- prepare advance questions;
- write event/show reports;
- conduct handoffs;
- run coordination scenarios;
- identify when specialist approval is required;
- manage status and escalation.

It must not represent completion as authority to:

- approve structural designs;
- approve electrical systems;
- certify rigging;
- override an AHJ or venue policy;
- perform craft work for which the learner lacks qualification;
- assume legal Crowd Manager or safety-officer status merely through Crew Blueprint coursework.

## Curriculum decision

Create **Production & Coordination** as a separate ecosystem branch with parallel stage-management, production-management, and venue/event-operations pathways. Do not use it as an automatic promotion rung above every technical department. Technical leads own their systems; production and event leaders own integration, communication, resources, and cross-functional execution.
