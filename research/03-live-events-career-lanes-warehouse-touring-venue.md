# Research Package 03 — U.S. Live-Events Career Lanes: Warehouse/Shop, Touring/Field, and Fixed-Site Venue Technician Work

**Research date:** 2026-08-15

## Research question

How does U.S. live-events production work split between (1) warehouse/shop roles, (2) touring/field roles, and (3) fixed-site venue or in-house AV roles? How transferable are skills between these lanes, how separate are the career tracks in practice, and what are the schedule/lifestyle differences?

---

## Executive conclusion

The three lanes are **real and visible in current employer structures**, but they are **not sealed career tracks**.

Large employers commonly post them as separate job families or operating groups:

- **Warehouse/shop**: inventory, shipping/receiving, QC, prep, staging, repair, and logistics.
- **Touring/field**: traveling with a show or event system, repeated load-in/show/load-out, operation, maintenance, local-crew direction, and travel.
- **Fixed-site/venue**: operating and supporting AV/production systems at a hotel, convention center, theatre, arena, church, campus, or installed venue while serving a recurring local client base.

Encore, for example, currently publishes separate **Warehouse** and **Audio Visual** categories and describes itself as an on-site event technology provider in more than 1,500 venues. Feld Entertainment separately recruits into a dedicated **Show Unit/Traveling Show** structure for stagehand sound, projection/video, electric, carpenter, rigging, and other departments.

The important career finding is that **workers can move between these lanes, but movement is not automatic**. Shared technical skills transfer strongly; lane-specific operating experience does not.

A useful Crew Blueprint model is:

> **Shop/warehouse competence → field/event competence → system ownership → venue or touring specialization → leadership**

But workers may enter at any point depending on training and experience.

# 1. Warehouse / shop-based production work

## What employers actually call it

Current examples include:

- Warehouse Technician
- Sr. Warehouse Technician
- Warehouse & Driver Technician
- Warehouse Operations Supervisor / Manager
- Shop Technician
- Prep Technician
- Repair / QC Technician
- Shipping / Receiving Technician

Encore currently posts **Warehouse Technician** and **Warehouse & Driver Technician** roles in the U.S. Its Coppell, Texas Warehouse Technician posting defines the core job as shipping/receiving, quality control, inventory control, equipment picking, scanning, labeling, staging, and logistics. Its Dedham, Massachusetts Warehouse & Driver Technician similarly centers on shipping/receiving, quality control, inventory control, and transportation support.

Encore also posts a **Sr. Warehouse Technician** role in Oahu whose scope expands into leading equipment handling/logistics, staging, truck loading/unloading, QC, annual inventory, scheduling, and direct supervision of warehouse personnel.

- Encore — Sr. Warehouse Technician, Oahu: https://jobs.encoreglobal.com/en/job/honolulu/sr-warehouse-technician-oahu/6228/96798748912

## Typical day-to-day

The strongest current job-ad evidence describes work such as:

- receive and check returned gear;
- scan and track inventory;
- pick gear for outgoing orders;
- label, pack, and stage equipment;
- perform basic QC;
- load and unload trucks;
- maintain warehouse organization;
- support repair or maintenance workflows;
- move gear between warehouses and venues;
- at higher levels, supervise warehouse staff and scheduling.

This lane is therefore not just "moving boxes." It develops **equipment recognition, inventory discipline, prep accuracy, QC habits, failure recognition, packing logic, and production logistics**.

## What transfers well out of the warehouse

Strongly transferable:

- gear identification;
- cable and accessory recognition;
- equipment handling;
- case organization and labeling;
- truck-pack awareness;
- basic troubleshooting;
- maintenance habits;
- QC mindset;
- inventory discipline;
- forklifts/material handling where properly trained;
- understanding what a complete show package requires.

Less transferable by itself:

- operating a system in front of a live audience;
- show-cue execution;
- client interaction;
- venue-specific workflow;
- production timing under show pressure;
- supervising local stagehands;
- adapting a system during an active show.

A warehouse technician can know equipment extremely well while still lacking live-show operating experience.

## Schedule and lifestyle

Compared with touring, warehouse jobs are usually the most geographically stable. Many are full-time positions attached to a branch or production facility.

However, **"warehouse" does not necessarily mean Monday–Friday 9–5**. Encore job descriptions explicitly note that warehouse/event support can include irregular hours, evenings, weekends, holidays, and event-driven demand. Therefore Crew Blueprint should describe warehouse work as **more location-stable and often more schedule-predictable than touring**, not universally regular.

## Typical day-to-day

Feld's touring descriptions show a repeated operating pattern:

1. arrive in a new city/venue;
2. unload trucks;
3. direct or work with local labor;
4. assemble department equipment;
5. test and troubleshoot;
6. operate the live show or follow production cues;
7. maintain and repair gear between performances;
8. tear down;
9. load trucks;
10. move to the next market and repeat.

Its Stagehand Sound role combines board operation, sound mixing, local-crew direction, loading/unloading, assembly/disassembly, and ongoing maintenance. Its Projection/Video role similarly requires setup/tear-down, operation, maintenance, power/signal troubleshooting, LED/projector systems, and directing local crews.

The Supercross Operations Crew role adds logistics, truck loading, equipment assembly/disassembly, regular maintenance, repair, warehouse/off-road forklift operation, and preparation for movement to the next event.

## Touring-specific skills that do not fully transfer from a shop

Touring adds competencies that are hard to acquire without field experience:

- repeating the same production efficiently across different buildings;
- working from venue-to-venue under compressed timelines;
- directing local crews who may change every day;
- making show-critical repairs away from the home shop;
- loading a touring truck consistently;
- adapting to different docks, roof systems, power, stage dimensions, and local constraints;
- operating during live performance;
- maintaining professional consistency while fatigued or traveling;
- living and working as part of a traveling unit.

Feld explicitly screens applicants for whether they have **toured before**, whether they have worked with a touring show as a **local stagehand**, and whether they have the **ability and desire to tour year round**. Its video application also explicitly notes that touring includes living in hotels.

## Schedule and lifestyle

Touring has the clearest lifestyle distinction.

Current Feld postings require willingness to tour year-round, international travel for some roles, extended hours, weekends/holidays, and physically demanding production days. Feld's Head Electrician posting states that hours may **exceed 12 hours per day**.

This means touring offers concentrated show experience and potentially faster technical growth, but the cost is substantial travel, reduced location stability, long production days, and a schedule controlled by routing and show dates rather than a conventional workweek.

A 2026 live-music business discussion cited in Hypebot also reported that tour-crew wages had risen materially since the post-pandemic period, reflecting the increased cost and scarcity of experienced touring labor. That does not establish a universal wage premium for every touring worker, but it supports the broader conclusion that experienced touring labor is a distinct labor market.

## Typical day-to-day

Venue/in-house work often combines:

- pre-event setup;
- system checks;
- operation during meetings/shows;
- client or event-planner interaction;
- troubleshooting during the event;
- room turns and resets;
- breakdown;
- maintaining installed equipment;
- documenting or tracking event requirements;
- training junior technicians or volunteers at higher levels.

The critical difference from touring is **repeated ownership of one venue or venue network rather than one traveling show**.

Workers become familiar with:

- house signal paths;
- installed consoles and control systems;
- venue power and patching;
- room acoustics;
- local rigging limits;
- internal event procedures;
- banquet/event workflows;
- venue management and recurring clients.

## Schedule and lifestyle

Fixed-site work is geographically stable, but event schedules are still irregular.

Encore's technician descriptions explicitly state that hotel/convention-center work may include **days, evenings, weekends, holidays, on-call status, and irregular hours**.

Therefore the correct distinction is not:

> warehouse = regular, venue = regular, touring = irregular.

It is closer to:

> **warehouse = fixed facility and logistics-driven schedule; venue = fixed location but event-driven schedule; touring = event-driven schedule plus continuous travel.**

# 4. Skills that transfer between all three lanes

The strongest transferable skills are:

- safe equipment handling;
- equipment recognition;
- cable management;
- signal-flow fundamentals;
- power awareness within one's authorization;
- troubleshooting discipline;
- maintenance and QC;
- reading equipment lists and plots;
- load-in/load-out awareness;
- teamwork;
- communication;
- documentation;
- organization;
- time management;
- reliability.

These skills are repeatedly visible across warehouse, touring, and venue postings.

# 5. Skills that are lane-specific

## Warehouse/shop-specific

- inventory systems;
- large-scale order picking;
- prep workflow;
- repair benches;
- bulk QC;
- shipping/receiving;
- package completeness;
- warehouse logistics.

## Touring-specific

- routing/travel tolerance;
- repeatable truck pack;
- local-crew direction;
- fast adaptation to unfamiliar venues;
- touring maintenance away from the shop;
- live operation under show cues;
- long-day endurance;
- sustaining a production across multiple cities.

## Venue-specific

- house-system mastery;
- client/hospitality interaction;
- repeated room turns;
- installed-system troubleshooting;
- local venue procedures;
- relationships with venue management;
- supporting recurring clients/events.

# 6. Are these separate career tracks?

## Employer structure: yes

Major employers visibly separate them organizationally.

Encore distinguishes **Warehouse** from **Audio Visual/Event Technology** jobs, and Feld distinguishes **Show Unit/Traveling Show** from its non-touring operations.

That means the lanes are real enough to affect hiring, job descriptions, managers, schedules, and performance expectations.

## Worker career reality: often fluid

The lanes are not sealed.

Evidence of crossover includes:

- Encore venue technicians may also load/unload, store, prepare, label, and scan gear — tasks that overlap directly with warehouse work.
- Feld touring workers conduct maintenance and repair work that resembles shop responsibilities.
- Feld applicant questionnaires explicitly ask whether touring applicants have worked as **local stagehands**, demonstrating that local venue/field experience is recognized as relevant preparation for touring.
- Encore markets internal "Career Path Opportunities" and tells workers their careers are not limited to one location.

The best conclusion is:

> **The industry treats warehouse, touring, and venue work as distinct operating lanes, but experienced workers frequently cross between them when their technical skill set matches the new environment.**

What usually blocks movement is not the title of the previous job; it is missing proof of the new lane's operating competence.

# 7. Realistic movement between lanes

## Warehouse → venue technician

Usually plausible when the worker has:

- strong equipment knowledge;
- basic system setup competence;
- troubleshooting ability;
- professional communication;
- enough live-event exposure to operate under event pressure.

Main development gap: **operation and customer-facing event execution**.

## Warehouse → touring

Plausible, particularly for workers who know rental packages and maintenance well.

Main development gaps:

- touring experience;
- live operation;
- local-crew leadership;
- truck-pack discipline;
- travel endurance;
- field troubleshooting away from shop support.

## Venue → touring

Very plausible for technically strong technicians.

Venue technicians already understand event execution and live troubleshooting. Their development gap is usually **road workflow**: touring logistics, repetitive truck pack, unfamiliar venues, travel, and local-crew direction.

## Touring → venue

Usually highly transferable technically.

Touring workers may need to adapt to a stronger **hospitality/client-service orientation**, more stable house procedures, and slower recurring operational cycles.

## Touring → warehouse/shop

Technically transferable, especially into QC, prep, repair, or department lead roles.

The major adjustment is moving from show execution toward **process consistency, inventory accuracy, and equipment lifecycle management**.

# 8. Pay differences: what can and cannot be concluded

There is **no defensible single national rule** that touring always pays more, warehouse always pays less, or venue work always sits between them.

Compensation varies by:

- city and labor market;
- union vs nonunion status;
- technical department;
- overtime rules;
- per diem and travel arrangements;
- full-time vs part-time/on-call classification;
- employer size;
- supervisory responsibility;
- specialty certifications.

The more defensible lifestyle conclusion is:

- **Warehouse:** usually most location-stable; often full-time; event peaks may still create irregular shifts.
- **Venue:** location-stable but strongly event-driven; nights/weekends/holidays and on-call schedules are common.
- **Touring:** least location-stable; travel is inherent; long production days and year-round routing are common; total compensation may include travel-related structures unavailable to local roles.

Crew Blueprint should avoid teaching a fixed pay hierarchy unless a specific market, employer, or union contract is being discussed.

# 9. Recommended Crew Blueprint teaching model

Rather than presenting these as three levels, present them as **three work environments** that can exist at several technical skill levels.

## Lane A — Shop / Warehouse

**Core identity:** prepare, maintain, track, and move production equipment.

Typical progression:

Warehouse Technician → Senior/Lead Warehouse Technician → Warehouse Supervisor/Manager → department shop specialist / operations leadership

## Lane B — Field / Touring

**Core identity:** travel with or deploy a production, execute load-ins, operate shows, maintain systems, and load out.

Typical progression:

Local/field technician → touring technician → department lead/head → crew chief / production leadership

## Lane C — Venue / Installed Systems

**Core identity:** operate and maintain systems within a recurring venue or venue group and support event clients.

Typical progression:

Event/AV Technician → Technical Lead → Technical Supervisor / Operations Supervisor → Director of Event Technology / venue production leadership

These are **parallel environments, not a universal rank order**.

---

# 10. Bottom line

The user's original three-lane model is substantially supported by current industry evidence.

The needed correction is that **warehouse, touring, and venue work are not mutually exclusive identities**. They are operating contexts.

A worker can build a career entirely inside one lane, or move between them. The transferable core is technical knowledge, safety, troubleshooting, equipment handling, and production discipline. The non-transferable portion is the operational environment: inventory logistics in the warehouse, road/show ownership on tour, and house/client-system mastery at a fixed venue.

For The Crew Blueprint, these should therefore be taught as **career lanes with crossover bridges**, not as a single ladder and not as three isolated professions.
