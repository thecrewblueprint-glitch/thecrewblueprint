## 1. Executive architecture decision

The evidence does **not** support one generic “production management ladder.”

At minimum, four parallel systems should remain distinct:

1. **Stage Management** — show/rehearsal communication, cue/process ownership, company/show continuity.
2. **Production Management** — technical/logistical scope, vendors, labor, schedules, budget/resource coordination, advance/closeout.
3. **Venue/Event Operations** — house/site/facility/client/event readiness, permanent-system boundaries, dock/facilities/life-safety interfaces.
4. **Crew/Labor Leadership** — check-in, assignments, sequencing, handoffs, performance/correction, break/timekeeping/employer/local-rule execution.

Workers can transfer among them, but advancement in one does not automatically create authority in another.

# MDQ-090 — Stage Management role architecture

## Current role evidence

A current 2026 touring Stage Manager posting describes the Stage Manager as the central communication hub for day-to-day show operation and cue calling. Theatre and touring sources consistently frame the role around coordination, communication, rehearsal/show continuity and adaptation to venue conditions.

## Competency families

### Entry/support interfaces

- understand who owns show/rehearsal communication;
- recognize stage-management paperwork and call/cue authority;
- report status/issues through the correct channel;
- preserve performer/company privacy and professional boundaries;
- understand that backstage crew may take operational direction from stage management while technical ownership remains departmental.

### Assistant / stage-management depth

Research should include:

- rehearsal/process tracking;
- scheduling/communication interfaces;
- preset/checklist systems;
- cue documentation;
- company/department coordination;
- notes and change tracking;
- performance reports;
- venue/tour handoffs;
- emergency/escalation communication according to production policy.

### Stage Manager / touring responsibility

- central show communication;
- cue/process ownership;
- adapting to venue conditions while preserving production intent;
- coordinating departments/company management;
- maintaining documentation and changes;
- communicating unresolved conditions.

## Sector variation

- theatre/musical;
- touring family/theatrical production;
- concert/festival stage-management equivalents;
- corporate/show-caller-adjacent systems;
- broadcast/event-production equivalents.

Titles and authority vary materially by sector.

**State:** `researched` → `draftable` for role/process architecture.

# MDQ-091 — Production Management role architecture

## Core responsibility domains

Production management may include, depending on production:

- technical advance;
- production schedule/milestones;
- labor planning;
- vendor/rental coordination;
- venue/site coordination;
- transportation/logistics interfaces;
- budget/resource tracking;
- technical requirement reconciliation;
- permits/insurance/AHJ interfaces at awareness/coordination level;
- show-day command/communication structure;
- changes/substitutions;
- closeout/reporting.

## Distinction from technical department heads

Production managers coordinate requirements and constraints but do not automatically replace the qualified technical authority of rigging, electrical, engineering, security, medical, fire/life-safety or other specialist functions.

## Distinction from Stage Management

- Stage Management primarily owns show/rehearsal process and cue/company communication.
- Production Management primarily owns production delivery across resources, departments, vendors, schedules and constraints.

Actual division varies by organization.

## Competency map

### Coordinator/support

- document/version discipline;
- contacts/action items;
- schedule awareness;
- scope/change tracking;
- vendor/venue communication;
- status escalation.

### Production Manager

- integrated advance;
- labor/vendor/resource planning;
- dependencies and critical-path reasoning;
- change approval/escalation architecture;
- budget/schedule tradeoffs;
- cross-department risk and handoff management;
- closeout/post-show documentation.

**State:** `draftable`; direct production-manager practitioner interviews remain important.

# MDQ-092 — Venue / Event Operations role architecture

## Core systems

Venue/event operations can involve:

- event advance/client handoff;
- house production systems;
- loading dock/freight access;
- permanent electrical/rigging/audio/video systems;
- rooms/stage/audience configuration;
- facilities/engineering interface;
- security/crowd/guest-services interface;
- fire/life-safety/AHJ interface;
- accessibility/public routes;
- housekeeping/turnover;
- event-day coordination;
- incident/escalation systems;
- post-event turnover.

## Role-family distinction

Potential roles include:

- venue crew / house technician;
- house operations support;
- venue production coordinator;
- venue/event operations manager;
- production manager/technical manager;
- facilities/security/guest-service partners.

Small venues may combine functions that larger venues separate.

## Authority rule

Venue operations coordinates many controlled systems but should not be taught as automatically possessing technical authority over each system. House rigging, power, fire/life safety, security/crowd management and facilities functions retain their controlling authorities.

## Current Event Safety evidence

Event Safety Alliance guidance treats emergency planning, crowd management, weather preparedness, communications and site/venue design as connected event-safety systems. Current **ANSI ES1.9-2026** addresses crowd management as distinct from crowd control.

This supports teaching venue/event operations as an integrated coordination domain with specialist interfaces.

**State:** `researched` → `draftable` for role/system architecture.

# MDQ-093 — Crew Chief / Labor Leadership deepening

## Role scope

Crew/labor leadership is operational people/workflow ownership rather than automatically technical department authority.

Competency families:

- call/check-in organization;
- worker/department placement;
- assignment clarity;
- sequencing and resource movement;
- task completion/report-back;
- reassignment/standby;
- break/meal/timekeeping processes according to controlling employer/local/CBA/venue rules;
- performance/correction communication;
- injury/incident escalation;
- department/tour/house handoffs;
- load-in/load-out closeout;
- records and unresolved issues.

## Current IATSE evidence

IATSE touring guidance demonstrates that working conditions vary by contract/tier and local conditions. Yellow/White Card systems document department labor needs for load-in, performance and load-out. This supports teaching **labor planning as contextual and documented**, not as one national staffing formula.

## Leadership behavior

Teach:

- assignment ownership;
- concise status communication;
- fair/consistent rule application;
- correction without humiliation;
- recognizing when a technical question belongs to a department authority;
- reporting/recording rather than hiding unresolved problems;
- no invention of break/pay rules outside the actual controlling agreement/policy/law.

**State:** `draftable`; local/employer practitioner validation needed.

# MDQ-094 — Production paperwork and information flow

## Core artifact families

The full-project evidence model should understand at least:

- contact sheets;
- labor calls;
- call sheets;
- schedules;
- run of show;
- cue sheets;
- stage plots;
- input lists;
- patch sheets;
- lighting plots/schedules;
- screen/video maps;
- rigging plots/point information;
- power one-lines/load documentation;
- RF/network plans;
- trucking/parking/dock plans;
- manifests/case lists;
- venue specifications;
- permits/insurance/reference documents where relevant;
- incident/show reports;
- change/revision logs.

## Information-flow model

Teach every artifact with:

1. **owner** — who creates/controls it;
2. **consumer** — who needs it;
3. **effective version/date**;
4. **scope** — what it does and does not authorize;
5. **change path** — who can approve revisions;
6. **handoff point** — when another role takes action from it;
7. **archive/supersession** — what remains historical vs current.

This maps directly to the future backend/version architecture.

## Core rule

A document can communicate a technical requirement without granting the reader authority to execute controlled work.

# MDQ-095 — Client / artist / vendor communication

## Scope-confirmation loop

A transferable professional pattern is:

**request → clarify → document → identify owner/approver → assess impacts/dependencies → confirm accepted change → distribute current version → verify handoff → record unresolved items**

This is process architecture, not contractual/legal authority.

## Competency families

- distinguish preference from technical requirement;
- distinguish technical fact from contractual decision;
- confirm scope and acceptance criteria;
- document substitutions/change requests;
- escalate cost/schedule/safety impacts;
- avoid promising another department's controlled work;
- service recovery after errors/delays;
- professional handoff and closeout.

## Artist/performer interface

Respect privacy, consent, role boundaries and show-critical preferences. Technical departments should communicate through established production channels rather than bypassing responsible artist/company personnel without reason.

**State:** `draftable`; B2B/customer research should later refine the buyer-facing side.

# MDQ-096 — Incident, near-miss and post-show learning loop

## Scope

Teach organizational learning and reporting without pretending Crew Blueprint replaces emergency procedures, medical care, legal reporting or employer incident policy.

## Event-safety context

Event Safety Alliance explicitly centers life safety and identifies accidents, near-misses, emergency planning, communications, weather, crowd management and site conditions as critical live-event learning/safety domains.

## General learning loop

At a high level:

1. protect people / follow immediate site emergency process;
2. notify the responsible authority;
3. preserve/report relevant facts according to policy;
4. distinguish observation from assumption;
5. identify unresolved hazards/work impacts;
6. review causes/contributing conditions after immediate danger is controlled;
7. define corrective actions and owners;
8. update documentation/training/process where appropriate;
9. verify closure;
10. retain history without silently rewriting prior records.

## Near-miss principle

Near-miss reporting should be framed as learning/risk-reduction evidence, not blame or retaliation.

## Future backend implication

The product may eventually need separate records for:

- learner feedback;
- content errors;
- safety concerns;
- workplace observations;
- incidents/near misses only if business/customer/legal requirements justify storing such sensitive data.

Do not collect high-risk incident/health data merely because the backend can.

**State:** `draftable` for learning/reporting architecture; legal/employer-specific incident requirements remain external.
