# 1. Housekeeping, clear work areas, and trip-hazard prevention

## OSHA 29 CFR 1910.22 — Walking-Working Surfaces

OSHA requires employers to keep places of employment, passageways, storerooms, service rooms, and walking-working surfaces clean, orderly, and sanitary. Floors must be maintained in clean and, to the extent feasible, dry condition, and walking-working surfaces must be free of hazards such as loose boards, leaks, spills, snow, and ice.

**Primary source:** OSHA, 29 CFR 1910.22  
https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.22

### Stagehand Fundamentals support

Strongly supports beginner lessons on:

- cleaning tape/trash/debris;
- keeping work paths clear;
- reporting wet/slippery conditions;
- maintaining safe access/egress;
- avoiding creating trip hazards with gear or loose materials.

This is stronger than treating housekeeping as merely a professionalism preference; it is part of workplace safety.

# 2. Emergency exits and keeping egress unobstructed

## OSHA 29 CFR 1910.37

OSHA requires exit routes to remain free and unobstructed and prohibits placing materials or equipment, even temporarily, in an exit route. Exit routes must also be adequately lighted and clearly marked.

**Primary source:** OSHA, 29 CFR 1910.37  
https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.37

**Supplemental OSHA eTool:** Evacuation Plans and Procedures — Exit Routes  
https://www.osha.gov/etools/evacuation-plans-procedures/emergency-standards/maintenance-safeguards-features

### Stagehand Fundamentals support

Strongly supports claims that beginners should not stage cases, carts, cable, props, scenery, tables, or other equipment in emergency exits or designated egress paths.

Recommended wording:

> Keep exit routes and designated egress paths clear. Do not assume temporary placement is harmless; OSHA expressly prohibits equipment or materials from obstructing exit routes.

# 3. Near misses / close calls should be reported

OSHA Recommended Practices for Safety and Health Programs explicitly identifies close calls/near misses as conditions workers should report so employers can investigate and correct hazards before someone is hurt.

**Primary source:** OSHA — Worker Participation  
https://www.osha.gov/safety-management/worker-participation

OSHA recommends processes that allow workers to report:

- injuries and illnesses;
- hazards;
- safety/health concerns;
- close calls / near misses.

### Stagehand Fundamentals support

The course's incident-reporting lesson should explicitly include **near misses**, but should avoid implying one mandatory universal event-industry form or reporting chain. The mechanism is employer/site specific.

Recommended wording:

> Report injuries, hazards, equipment problems, and close calls through the reporting process established for that call. Near misses matter because they can reveal hazards before someone is injured.

# 4. Pushing vs. pulling road cases, carts, and material-handling equipment

OSHA's ergonomics guidance states that high-force push/pull requirements are undesirable and that **pushing is generally preferable to pulling** because body weight can assist the movement and pulling can cause carts to strike workers' legs/ankles.

**Primary source:** OSHA Technical Manual, Section VII, Chapter 1 — Ergonomics  
https://www.osha.gov/otm/section-7-ergonomics/chapter-1

OSHA's Electrical Contractors material-handling eTool provides more explicit operational guidance:

- use transport devices such as hand trucks and pallet jacks;
- use appropriate handles;
- avoid pulling when possible;
- pushing generally requires less effort;
- do not stack materials on a cart above eye level when that blocks visibility;
- maintain carts/wheels so excessive force is not required.

**Primary source:** OSHA eTool — Pushing, Pulling and Carrying  
https://www.osha.gov/etools/electrical-contractors/materials-handling/pushing

### Stagehand Fundamentals support

This fills a major handling gap and supports teaching:

- prefer controlled pushing when conditions and equipment allow;
- maintain a clear line of sight;
- get a spotter/guide when the load obstructs vision or the route is tight;
- avoid placing hands/feet in pinch/crush paths;
- do not force a damaged or jammed caster/cart.

This is ergonomic/material-handling guidance, not a rule that every road case must always be pushed rather than pulled. Ramp geometry, steering, door thresholds, local procedure, and crew assignment can require different handling.

# 5. Basic hand-tool condition and use

## OSHA 29 CFR 1910.242

OSHA states that employers are responsible for the safe condition of tools and equipment used by employees, including employee-furnished tools.

**Primary source:** OSHA, 29 CFR 1910.242  
https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.242

**OSHA Hand and Power Tools standards page:**  
https://www.osha.gov/hand-power-tools/standards

### Stagehand Fundamentals support

This supports awareness-level rules such as:

- inspect a hand tool before use;
- do not use obviously damaged tools;
- use the correct tool for the assigned task;
- return/report defective tools;
- do not improvise specialized or powered-tool work outside training/authorization.

### Remaining limitation

OSHA does not publish a special "stagehand C-wrench/multitool" standard. Specific wrench, multitool, knife-free multitool, ratchet, or hardware technique remains employer/craft practice and should not be presented as OSHA procedure.

# 6. Gaff tape, spike tape, and electrical tape — real product distinctions

## Gaffer tape

Pro Tapes describes professional gaffer tape as widely used in arts/entertainment for cable hold-down/bundling, temporary holding, marking/labeling cases and equipment, stage marking, and safety marking.

**Manufacturer source:** Pro Tapes — Pro Gaff  
https://www.protapes.com/products/pro-gaff-tape-premium-professional-grade-gaffer-tape

## Spike tape

Pro Tapes describes spike tape as narrow matte cloth tape intended for stage spiking, high-visibility marks, labeling, and set/stair/exit marking.

**Manufacturer source:** Pro Tapes — Pro Spike  
https://www.protapes.com/products/pro-spike-matte-cloth-gaffers-tape/

**Manufacturer theater/stage overview:**  
https://www.protapes.com/industries/arts-entertainment/theater-and-stage/

## Electrical tape

3M describes vinyl electrical tape as designed for electrical insulation/protection, including dielectric performance, abrasion/environmental protection, sealing, and color coding depending on product.

**Manufacturer source:** 3M — Vinyl Electrical Tapes  
https://www.3m.com/3M/en_US/electrical-construction-maintenance-us/products/electrical-tapes/vinyl-electrical-tape/

### Curriculum implication

The existing course distinction is valid if taught as **different products for different purposes**, not as interchangeable rolls of tape:

- **Gaff tape:** temporary entertainment-production holding, cable management, labeling, marking.
- **Spike tape:** narrow stage-position/identification marking.
- **Electrical tape:** electrical insulation/protection/color-coding applications when used by appropriately assigned personnel.

A beginner should **not** infer that having electrical tape authorizes them to repair or modify electrical systems.

# 8. Pinch/crush awareness and obstructed-view handling

There is no single "road-case pinch point" OSHA rule. However, OSHA material-handling and ergonomics guidance directly recognizes pinch/cut hazards at handles and emphasizes visibility when moving carts.

OSHA's pushing/pulling guidance says handles should not contain rough or sharp areas that could cut or pinch hands and warns against stacking carts above eye level where workers cannot see the path.

**Primary source:** OSHA eTool — Pushing, Pulling and Carrying  
https://www.osha.gov/etools/electrical-contractors/materials-handling/pushing

### Curriculum implication

For road cases and carts, teach awareness of:

- hands between cases/walls/doorframes;
- feet around casters;
- ramps and uncontrolled rolling;
- blind corners;
- blocked sightlines;
- damaged wheels or handles;
- loads that require a guide/spotter.

These are applications of material-handling principles, not a stagehand-specific federal standard.

# 9. Communication vocabulary: what remains unsupported as a universal standard

Packages 15–19 support checking in, reporting problems, following assigned hierarchy, asking when unsure, and concise professional communication.

However, this audit still found **no authoritative national U.S. stagehand standard** requiring the exact phrases:

- `Copy`
- `Heard`
- `Standing by`
- any single universal set of non-rigging hand signals

### Curriculum recommendation

Teach these as **common examples that may be used on some crews**, while the actual communication protocol is call-/employer-/department-specific.

Recommended wording:

> Use concise acknowledgments that your crew recognizes. If a lead uses a specific radio phrase, hand signal, or call-and-response convention, learn that system rather than assuming every venue uses the same vocabulary.

Formal crane/derrick signaling standards should not be generalized into universal stagehand hand signals.

# 10. Phone etiquette and staying available

Packages 15 and 19 already contain local/employer evidence that professional conduct, check-in/check-out, punctuality, and staying available to the assigned department matter.

The specific rule "never use your phone except on break" is **not a universal U.S. stagehand standard**. Some employers/locals publish restrictive phone policies, but this varies.

### Curriculum recommendation

Teach:

> Personal phone use must not interfere with safety, instructions, radio awareness, or availability for assigned work. Follow the call's employer/venue/local policy.

Do not present one employer's phone rule as an industry-wide law.

# 11. First-15-minutes / first-30-days frameworks

## First 15 minutes on site

The course's practical sequence — check in, identify the lead, receive briefing/assignment, identify work area and hazards — is strongly consistent with union/employer practices documented in Packages 15 and 19.

There is no national regulation naming a standardized **"first 15 minutes"** stagehand process.

Treat it as a Crew Blueprint orientation framework derived from real check-in/assignment expectations.

## First 30 days

This audit again found no national U.S. stagehand onboarding standard organized as Week 1/2/3/4 or a formal 30-day competency sequence.

### Curriculum recommendation

Keep the 30-day plan, but label it clearly:

> **Crew Blueprint suggested development plan — not an industry certification or standardized employer probation program.**

# 12. Load-out risk comparison — final qualification

Package 18 provides evidence that load-out combines teardown, packing, manual material handling, docks/trailers, and often late/extended work. OSHA/NIOSH sources independently recognize fatigue, poor visibility, excessive force, slips/trips, and vehicle/material-handling exposure as hazards.

What remains unsupported is a universal empirical statement that:

> "Load-out is always more dangerous than load-in."

### Recommended course wording

> Load-out can present an intensified risk profile because it may occur after a long workday or performance, often at night or under time pressure, while crews are striking and moving large quantities of equipment. Those conditions make fatigue, visibility, communication, and controlled sequencing especially important. Actual risk depends on the production and site.

This preserves the useful teaching point without presenting an unsupported universal ranking.

# 14. Remaining claims that should stay explicitly labeled as course guidance

Even after Packages 15–20, the following should **not** be presented as national standards unless future source work produces stronger evidence:

1. A universal stagehand phrasebook (`Copy`, `Heard`, `Standing by`).
2. One national first-15-minutes procedure.
3. One national first-30-days development schedule.
4. One universal PPE kit for every call.
5. One universal rule that beginners must do load-in/load-out before show call.
6. One universal statement that load-out is always riskier than load-in.
7. One universal list of personal tools every stagehand must carry.
8. One universal phone-use policy.
9. One universal road-case stacking/packing sequence; truck pack is production/equipment specific and controlled by the responsible loader/lead.
10. One universal stagehand hand-signal system outside standards that apply to specific equipment/operations.

These can still be taught as **Crew Blueprint best-practice frameworks or common industry examples**, but they should be labeled accordingly.

# Final conclusion

After Packages **15–20**, the Stagehand Fundamentals research backfill has good source coverage for the course's major safety, role, handling, jobsite, load-in/load-out, employment, and career-development claims.

The remaining weak areas are no longer missing evidence so much as **claims that are inherently local, employer-specific, or instructional frameworks rather than national standards**. Claude should preserve those boundaries instead of trying to force every course statement into OSHA, ANSI, or IATSE-national language.

The most important distinction for final course revision is:

> **Use primary sources to support the safety and operational principles; label local customs, common vocabulary, and Crew Blueprint development frameworks honestly as practice rather than universal standard.**
