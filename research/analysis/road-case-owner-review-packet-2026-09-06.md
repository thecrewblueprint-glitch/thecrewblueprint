## What is already resolved

The existing research supports a bounded general lesson about wheeled-case movement without claiming one universal live-event technique. The current route already does several things correctly:

- checks the case and route before movement;
- treats defects, unstable loads, blocked sightlines, and changed conditions as stop/escalate points;
- avoids one universal case-weight cutoff;
- avoids claiming one fixed caster layout or steering end;
- says push/pull orientation depends on case, route, assignment, and responsible lead;
- separates flat-floor movement from ramps/slopes/docks/lifts/vehicle paths;
- protects fingers/feet from pinch and wheel paths;
- uses an agreed stop call rather than declaring one word universal;
- makes completion of an observed practice specific to the recorded case/route rather than standing authorization.

The research package explicitly labels several common road-case practices as variable/unverified rather than established rules. That distinction should remain.

## Why owner review is still needed

The requested owner pass was intended to answer a different question from the source research: **does the proposed lesson match credible field practice as the owner has actually experienced it, and where is the copy too rigid, too formal, or unlike how a good lead would teach it on a real call?**

## Owner decisions needed

You can answer these from your own field experience in plain language. No formal terminology is required.

### 1. What does a good lead usually say before two hands move a normal road case?

Current draft assumes the pair should know destination, roles, pace, transitions, and a stop word before starting. Tell us which of those are realistic every time, which are usually implicit, and what wording you actually hear/use.

### 2. What communication calls feel authentic?

The research found inconsistent public usage of `stop`, `hold`, and warnings such as `fingers/toes`. The course currently avoids declaring one universal phrase. Identify any calls you think are useful examples, and any that sound artificial or market-specific.

### 3. How should the basic two-person positions be described?

The current course deliberately avoids saying one person must always push while the other walks backward/steers. Describe the common arrangement you have actually seen for an ordinary flat-floor case, and the exceptions that matter.

### 4. What changes at doors, corners, and thresholds in real calls?

The current draft treats each transition as a reason to slow/reset and sometimes stop. Which transitions normally require a full stop, which are commonly handled while moving under control, and what should a brand-new hand notice?

### 5. What should we say about ramps/slopes?

The current learner boundary is conservative: stop before an unplanned ramp/slope and use the assigned crew or mechanical plan. Confirm whether you want the Field Skill to remain recognition/escalation-only for ramps, or whether there is any additional non-procedural context that would help a new hand understand why the task changes.

### 6. What case defects or setup problems have you actually seen matter?

Current checks include casters, brakes, handles, latches, lids/drawers, stacking, stability, labels, and sightlines. Identify the few defects or mistakes that are genuinely worth emphasizing to a first-call hand so the lesson does not become an inspection checklist for its own sake.

### 7. Parking / boneyard handoff

What is the realistic field expectation after the case reaches its destination? For example: brakes when applicable, orientation/labels, keeping aisles clear, grouping with similar cases, reporting completion, or waiting for a pack plan. Identify what should be general and what should remain lead/site-specific.

### 8. What should be removed because it sounds like classroom overkill?

The current lesson is research-heavy. Identify anything a competent lead would consider obvious, distracting, or falsely precise for a new hand.
