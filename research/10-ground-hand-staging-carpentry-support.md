# Research Package 10 — Ground-Hand Support for Staging and Carpentry

**Research date:** 2026-08-15  
**Scope:** What a general stagehand / ground hand can realistically do to support staging and carpentry departments at arenas, theaters, festivals, and corporate events without being treated as a scenic carpenter, structural designer, or qualified staging lead.

## Executive conclusion

A general hand can legitimately support staging/carpentry by moving and staging deck sections, risers, stairs, guardrails, scenic pieces, and related hardware; helping assemble modular staging systems **under direction**; following drawings or layout instructions at the level assigned; and helping strike, case, cart, and load those systems.

The important boundary is that **assembly labor is not the same thing as structural authority**. Product manuals, employer role descriptions, and safety standards consistently support separating physical assembly from decisions about structural configuration, load capacity, alteration, final leveling, fall-protection configuration, and final acceptance of the system.

That boundary is especially important because modular staging systems are product-specific. Hardware, locking methods, bracing, leg systems, guardrails, stair adapters, and permissible configurations vary by manufacturer and system.

# 1. Tasks a ground hand may realistically support

## Moving and staging deck sections, risers, stairs, guardrails, and scenic pieces

Current live-event stagehand postings explicitly assign stagehands to build stage risers and assist technical departments with placement/build work. Live Nation's current Union Stagehand posting for the Virginia Beach Amphitheater includes load-in/load-out, assisting audio/lighting/video with placements/builds/cabling, hanging backdrops, and **building stage risers**.

Source: Live Nation — Union Stagehand (VB Amphitheater)  
https://livenation.wd503.myworkdayjobs.com/en-US/LNExternalSite/job/Union-Stagehand--VB-Amphitheater-_JR-87529

General event-labor postings also describe junior staging crews setting up and dismantling staging and risers while following supervisor instructions and floor plans.

Source: Cooper's Inc. — General Labour - Staging  
https://builtin.com/job/general-labour-staging/9331757

These are strong evidence that modular staging setup/strike is not automatically specialist-only work. The competency boundary is **what part of the build the worker is assigned and trained to perform**.

## Modular stage assembly under direction

Manufacturer documentation shows that modular stage systems are intentionally designed for repeatable assembly. For example, Bil-Jax's AS2100 system uses snap-in legs and locking hooks, while guardrail components use dedicated locks that engage the deck channel. The ST8100 system uses defined legs, sleeves, base pads, bracing, build-up frames, guardrails, and accessories.

Sources:

Bil-Jax — AS2100 staging system overview  
https://biljax.com/bil-jax-staging-elevates-events-to-a-whole-new-level

Bil-Jax — ST8100 Steel Multi-Stage  
https://biljax.com/product-category/event/event-staging/st8100

This supports teaching a general hand to assist with **a specific manufacturer's assembly method after receiving instruction**, rather than memorizing a universal deck-building procedure.

## Guardrails and stairs under direction

Bil-Jax documents a specific guardrail assembly sequence for its AS2100 ramp system: seat the lower lip of the guardrail locks in the deck channel, pivot until the T-Lock fully seats, and tighten the hardware. The manufacturer explicitly warns that incorrectly installed handrails can result in serious injury or death.

Source: Bil-Jax — AS2100 ADA Ramp assembly guidance  
https://biljax.com/how-to-build-ada-ramp-for-the-bil-jax-as2100-aluminum-stage-system

This is useful evidence for the Crew Blueprint distinction:

- A trained hand **may physically install guardrail/stair components under the lead's direction**.
- The hand should not independently decide whether a rail is required, which rail configuration satisfies the event design/code requirement, or whether the finished installation is acceptable.

## Scenic-piece load-in

Theatrical carpentry job descriptions consistently separate skilled scenic fabrication from load-in/install labor. A current Kansas City Repertory Theatre Stage Carpenter posting assigns the carpenter to build/install scenic elements from technical drawings and participate in load-ins and strikes, while collaborating with the Technical Director, ATD, and Head Carpenter.

Source: Playbill — Stage Carpenter, Kansas City Repertory Theatre  
https://playbill.com/job/stage-carpenter/a6db2061-3a5e-4728-87df-57cd328534b6

A general hand can therefore support by **moving and positioning scenic units where directed**, without being taught that they have authority to modify, repair, rig, or structurally evaluate them.

# 2. What stays with the staging/carpentry lead or other qualified role

## Structural modifications and fabrication

Skilled scenic carpenter roles include construction, fabrication, tool/machinery use, materials selection, and working from technical drawings. Encore's Scenic Fabricator II position, for example, requires skilled carpentry on exhibits, scenery, crates, decking, walls, and platforms using woodworking machinery under project drawings/specifications.

Source: Encore — Scenic Fabricator II  
https://jobs.encoreglobal.com/en/job/mississauga/scenic-fabricator-ii/6228/96833091840/822130

For Crew Blueprint, that supports a hard beginner boundary around:

- cutting or altering deck/scenic components;
- drilling new attachment points;
- changing bracing or support geometry;
- substituting hardware;
- modifying stairs/guardrails;
- repairing structural elements;
- making custom field-fabrication decisions.

Those tasks are not ordinary general-hand support merely because a worker has access to tools.

## Load-bearing and configuration decisions

OSHA repeatedly assigns platform/load design and inspection responsibilities to qualified or competent persons in contexts involving elevated work platforms. For example, OSHA's personnel-platform rules require a qualified person familiar with structural design to design the platform/attachment system, and require rated capacity to be posted and not exceeded.

Source: OSHA — 29 CFR 1926.1431, Hoisting personnel  
https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.1431

OSHA's marine-terminal platform rule similarly requires platforms/skids to be strong enough for the loads handled and to have safe working loads posted or marked.

Source: OSHA — 29 CFR 1917.115, Platforms and skids  
https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1917/1917.115

These standards do not create a single live-event staging rule, but they reinforce a broad safety principle: **the worker assembling a piece is not automatically the person authorized to determine its allowable load or structural configuration**.

## Final leveling and safety acceptance

OSHA staging guidance emphasizes that elevated platforms should be safely secured/supported, level, adequately decked, safely accessible, and provided with guardrails where required.

Source: OSHA — Scaffolds (Staging) eTool  
https://www.osha.gov/etools/shipyard/general-requirements/scaffolds

That supports teaching hands to **recognize obvious problems and report them**, while leaving final inspection/acceptance to the designated lead, competent person, manufacturer-authorized installer, venue authority, engineer, or other responsible role as applicable.

# 3. Common ground-hand mistakes and why they matter

## Wrong locking/connection method

Modular staging systems do not share one universal connector. Bil-Jax systems alone use different hardware across product families: AS2100 uses locking hooks/snap-in components and dedicated guardrail locks, while ST8100 uses legs, sleeves, base pads, bracing, snap pins, T-handles, F-clips, riser sockets, and other dedicated accessories.

Sources:

https://biljax.com/product-category/event/event-staging/as2100

https://biljax.com/product-category/event/event-staging/st8100

**Curriculum implication:** the mistake to teach against is not merely "forgetting to lock the deck." It is **assuming a familiar locking method applies to the current system**. A hand should identify the current product/system and follow the lead/manufacturer procedure.

## Missing or incomplete guardrails/handrails

Bil-Jax's ramp instructions explicitly warn that incorrectly installed handrails can cause serious injury or death and require all fastening hardware to be secured.

Source:  
https://biljax.com/how-to-build-ada-ramp-for-the-bil-jax-as2100-aluminum-stage-system

OSHA standards in multiple elevated-platform contexts also require guardrails/fall protection when platform-edge exposure reaches the applicable threshold.

Sources:

https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.451

https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1915/1915.71

A general hand does **not** need to memorize every code threshold for every jurisdiction to understand the bystander-level rule:

> An elevated/open edge is a safety-critical condition. Do not assume a platform is finished because the deck surface is assembled. Follow the installation plan and report missing edge protection, stairs, access components, or incomplete rails immediately.

## Poor material staging and awkward lifting

OSHA's materials-handling guidance notes that poorly planned staging increases awkward lifting and unnecessary carrying. It recommends staging materials on stands/racks or other devices that reduce bending/reaching.

Source: OSHA — Materials Handling: Staging & Housekeeping  
https://www.osha.gov/etools/electrical-contractors/materials-handling/staging

For ground hands moving deck sections and scenic pieces, this supports practical curriculum about:

- clearing a route before the carry;
- using enough people for the size/weight/shape;
- communicating before lifting or setting down;
- protecting fingers at mating edges;
- staging pieces so the next crew can access them without rehandling;
- not leaving heavy sections leaning or balanced insecurely.

## Damaging scenic finishes

Scenic carpenter roles treat scenic elements as finished production assets, not generic construction lumber. They may include painted, fabricated, or specialty surfaces. A ground hand should therefore follow handling instructions, avoid dragging finished faces/edges, avoid using scenic pieces as worktables or supports, and ask where a piece can safely be gripped.

This is an **analysis/curriculum inference** from scenic-fabrication and installation responsibilities rather than a universal published industry rule.

Encore — Scenic Fabricator II

# 4. Bystander-level structural and edge awareness

A general hand should understand **that structural capacity exists**, without being trained to calculate it.

Useful awareness-level concepts include:

- platforms have rated/allowable loads;
- legs, frames, bracing, base pads, and connections all contribute to the intended configuration;
- missing components can change safety even if the deck "feels solid";
- uneven or inadequate support can create instability;
- heavy concentrated loads should not be placed wherever convenient without direction;
- temporary stage systems can have product-specific limits and permitted configurations;
- guardrails, stairs, access points, and edge protection are part of the system, not cosmetic accessories.

OSHA's platform standards reinforce that platforms must support their intended loads and that rated capacities must not be exceeded in applicable regulated contexts.

Sources:

https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1917/1917.115

https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.1431

The Crew Blueprint should teach the hand to **recognize, stop, and escalate** rather than calculate engineering limits.

# 7. Recommended Crew Blueprint teaching boundary

## Appropriate general-hand knowledge

Teach the learner to:

- identify common deck, riser, stair, rail, scenic, bracing, and support components at a basic level;
- move and stage them safely;
- use team-carry communication;
- assemble only the system/components they have been shown and assigned;
- follow the layout and sequence from the lead;
- protect finished scenic surfaces;
- watch fingers, feet, pinch/crush zones, and path-of-travel hazards;
- recognize incomplete connections, missing rails, unstable/uneven conditions, or obvious missing components;
- report and stop when the build does not match the instruction or something appears unsafe.

## Do not present as general-hand authority

Do not teach a beginner to independently:

- redesign the stage layout;
- calculate structural or live-load capacity;
- decide whether bracing can be omitted;
- substitute hardware;
- cut, drill, weld, or modify structural components;
- alter scenic units;
- choose a leveling workaround outside the manufacturer's/lead's procedure;
- decide which guardrails or stairs are legally required;
- approve the finished stage for occupancy/use;
- rig/suspend scenic or stage elements without separate training and authorization.

# 8. Important terminology and standardization caution

There is **no single universal modular-stage assembly method** across the live-events industry.

Different product families use different:

- deck-to-deck connections;
- leg systems;
- braces;
- locking hardware;
- guardrail clamps;
- stair interfaces;
- allowable heights;
- support geometries;
- storage/cart arrangements;
- load limits.

Therefore Crew Blueprint should teach **system recognition + following the current manufacturer's/lead's procedure**, not a memorized hardware sequence presented as universal.

This parallels the finding from Research Package 08 for LED video walls.

# Bottom line

For a general stagehand, staging/carpentry support should be taught as:

**Move it safely → stage it correctly → assemble the assigned modular components under direction → preserve the designed system → recognize obvious incomplete/unsafe conditions → escalate structural questions to the responsible lead.**

The hand can be a useful participant in deck, riser, stair, rail, and scenic load-in work without being taught that they are qualified to make structural, fabrication, load-bearing, or final inspection decisions.
