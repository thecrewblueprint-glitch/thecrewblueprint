# 36 — Electrics Course 3: System Design & Load Planning

**Research Queue ID:** CC-005  
**Status:** Complete research packet — safety-bounded  
**Research date:** 2026-08-17  
**Purpose:** Define the curriculum boundary and mastery competencies for entertainment electrical-system planning without providing a substitute for qualified-person training, licensing, code interpretation, employer authorization, or supervised practical experience.

---

## Decision question

**What belongs in a mastery-level Crew Blueprint electrics course, and what must remain dependent on formal electrical qualification, current code, employer procedures, and supervised practical competence?**

## Bottom-line finding

Entertainment electrical mastery is a **hard qualification boundary**. ETCP maintains separate Entertainment Electrician and Portable Power Distribution Technician certifications with substantial experience-based eligibility requirements. OSHA likewise distinguishes qualified from unqualified persons and requires specific training for people permitted to work on or near exposed energized parts.

Accordingly, Crew Blueprint Course 3 should teach **system-planning literacy, documentation, risk reasoning, code/standards navigation, verification concepts, and authority boundaries**. It should not function as a procedural manual for constructing, energizing, modifying, or servicing temporary power systems.

The progression is:

**electrical awareness → authorized technical competence → lead accountability → requirements/load-planning literacy → documented system design review → qualified implementation → inspection/verification → controlled handoff**

---

# 1. Qualification boundary is part of the curriculum

OSHA 29 CFR 1910.332 states that qualified persons permitted to work on or near exposed energized parts require additional training, including skills to distinguish live parts, determine nominal voltage, and understand applicable clearance distances. The degree of training is tied to employee risk.

ETCP’s Entertainment Electrician program requires 30 eligibility points derived from professional work experience and other recognized experience/education pathways before a candidate may sit for the examination.

This means an online mastery course cannot credibly claim to create an entertainment electrician by information transfer alone.

---

# 2. What Course 3 should teach

At a safe and educational level, learners should understand the planning lifecycle:

**production requirements → equipment/load inventory → system requirements → qualified design/code review → documented distribution plan → authorized installation → inspection/testing → energization under appropriate authority → operational monitoring → shutdown/handoff**

The focus is on how professional decisions fit together and who owns them.

---

# 3. Requirements and load-planning literacy

Learners should understand conceptually that a professional plan must account for:

- connected equipment requirements;
- expected operating demand;
- distribution capacity;
- circuit/system protection;
- grounding/bonding requirements;
- environmental conditions;
- cable/distribution equipment ratings;
- applicable code and authority-having-jurisdiction requirements;
- manufacturer requirements;
- redundancy/critical-load considerations;
- interfaces with generators, venue service, lighting, audio, video and other departments.

Crew Blueprint should teach learners how to recognize these as required design inputs, not give unsupervised instructions for building or energizing a distribution system.

---

# 4. Standards and code navigation

The mastery learner should know that entertainment power is governed by overlapping sources including:

- OSHA workplace electrical-safety rules;
- adopted electrical codes such as NFPA 70/NEC as applicable;
- local code/AHJ requirements;
- equipment listings/labels and manufacturer instructions;
- employer procedures;
- entertainment-industry standards;
- ETCP professional competency frameworks.

The correct educational habit is **verify the current applicable requirement**, not memorize one rule and treat it as universal across jurisdictions or jobsites.

---

# 5. Documentation and change control

A professional system requires documentation sufficient for qualified personnel to review and execute safely.

Course 3 can teach the purpose of:

- load/equipment inventories;
- system diagrams;
- distribution schedules;
- equipment identification;
- inspection/verification records;
- change records;
- responsibility/authorization records;
- handoff documentation.

The learner should understand that an undocumented field change can invalidate assumptions elsewhere in the system.

---

# 6. Verification and authority

Course 3 should distinguish:

- **designer/planner:** develops or reviews system requirements within competence;
- **qualified installer/technician:** performs work they are trained and authorized to perform;
- **lead/supervisor:** coordinates people and verifies process within assigned authority;
- **AHJ/code authority:** enforces applicable adopted requirements;
- **employer:** determines assignments and qualification/authorization under applicable rules.

One person may fill several roles on a production, but the roles should not be conceptually collapsed.

---

# 7. Troubleshooting boundary

The course can teach a non-procedural diagnostic model:

1. define the symptom;
2. identify the affected system boundary;
3. consult documentation/status information;
4. determine whether the issue is within the learner’s authorization;
5. isolate or stop work according to established procedures;
6. escalate to appropriately qualified personnel when required;
7. document the discrepancy and resolution.

It should not teach unqualified learners how to access exposed energized components or bypass protective systems.

---

# 8. Recommended course structure

### Module 1 — Professional Scope, Qualification & Code Ecosystem
OSHA qualified-person distinction, ETCP scope, AHJ/employer roles.

### Module 2 — Requirements & Load-Planning Concepts
Equipment requirements, capacity, protection, environmental and interface considerations at a planning level.

### Module 3 — System Documentation
Diagrams, schedules, inventories, identification, change control.

### Module 4 — Design Review & Risk Analysis
Missing inputs, conflicting requirements, single points of failure, authorization boundaries.

### Module 5 — Inspection, Verification & Handoff
Why professional systems require documented checks and controlled status transitions.

### Module 6 — Scenario-Based Decision Making
Learners identify missing information, unsafe assumptions, authority conflicts, and when qualified review is mandatory.

---

# 9. Assessment model

Assess decisions rather than procedural electrical work.

Examples:

- identify missing information in a production power brief;
- distinguish a planning decision from work requiring qualified execution;
- identify when a requested field change requires review;
- interpret responsibility boundaries;
- choose correct escalation when system status is uncertain;
- audit documentation for inconsistencies.

No online assessment should be represented as proof that the learner can safely construct or energize an entertainment power system.

## Curriculum decision

**Electrics Course 3 should be a mastery-level planning, documentation, verification, and professional-boundary course—not a self-study authorization to perform hazardous electrical work.** Practical electrical competence must remain tied to appropriate formal training, supervised experience, current code, employer authorization, and any required certification/licensing.
