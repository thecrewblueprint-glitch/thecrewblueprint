# 23 — Backline / Props / Wardrobe Course 2: Ownership & Accountability in Setup

**Research Queue ID:** CA-003  
**Status:** Complete research packet  
**Research date:** 2026-08-17  
**Purpose:** Define the progression from general-hand support into junior specialist responsibility for show-critical assets without implying department-head authority.

---

## Decision question

**What does asset ownership and accountability look like for junior specialists?**

## Bottom-line finding

Course 2 should teach **custody, state preservation, verification, documentation, and escalation** rather than generic “careful handling.” The key transition from Course 1 is that the learner is no longer merely moving an assigned object; the learner begins to become accountable for whether an assigned asset is the correct item, in the correct state, in the correct location, at the correct time, and returned or handed off correctly.

The strongest transferable model is:

**identify → verify condition/state → stage/setup to instruction → protect/preserve → confirm readiness → monitor/hand off → strike/reset → reconcile → escalate discrepancies**

This is a Crew Blueprint synthesis, not a nationally standardized industry checklist. It is supported by current touring department structures, specialist role descriptions, and professional live-event equipment-accountability practices.

---

# 1. Evidence that these are ownership-sensitive departments

Current IATSE Yellow Card touring records consistently separate **Props** and **Wardrobe** into their own departments with traveling heads and local take-in/performance/put-out labor. Examples include *The Outsiders Tour*, *The Notebook Tour*, *Suffs*, *Hamilton – Philip Tour*, and *Beauty and the Beast*. These records identify a **Head Prop-Person** and **Head Wardrobe** separately from Carpentry, Electrics, and Audio and show that Props and Wardrobe retain dedicated performance labor.

This matters because these departments are not simply load-in labor pools. Their assets remain show-critical through performance and strike.

PRG’s current crew classification separately identifies **Backline Technician** as a specialist role and expects extensive backline knowledge, artist-facing experience, and increasing years of event backline experience. More senior backline technicians are expected to instruct other backline technicians.

Together, the evidence supports treating backline, props, and wardrobe as domains where **asset identity, preset/state, performer needs, and continuity matter**.

---

# 2. The junior-specialist accountability model

A useful Course 2 concept is **assigned custody**, not ownership in the legal/property sense.

A junior specialist may be responsible for an assigned subset of assets or setup tasks while a department head retains final department authority.

The learner should be able to answer:

1. What exactly is this item?
2. Who or what does it belong to in the show workflow?
3. What condition or preset/state should it be in?
4. Where does it go?
5. When must it be ready?
6. What may I change without approval?
7. How do I know it is ready?
8. Who receives the handoff?
9. Where does it return after use?
10. What discrepancy requires immediate escalation?

The progression from Course 1 is therefore:

**move what you are told → understand what you are responsible for → verify and preserve its intended show state**.

# 3. Module 1 — Asset Ownership, Identity & State

## 3.1 Asset identity

Teach learners to distinguish between:

- generic consumable/support items;
- rental-house assets;
- production-owned assets;
- venue/house assets;
- performer-owned or performer-specific items;
- show-specific props/costumes;
- preset-dependent equipment.

The point is not legal title. The point is that different assets have different tolerance for substitution, adjustment, damage, loss, or unauthorized use.

## 3.2 State is part of the asset

For show-critical equipment, “the item is present” is insufficient.

Relevant state can include:

- physical condition;
- orientation/location;
- accessory set;
- labels/identifiers;
- settings or adjustments;
- packing arrangement;
- performer assignment;
- preset location;
- clean/dirty/repair status;
- charged/battery status where applicable.

Course 1 already established the boundary that a hand should not casually alter sensitive show items. Course 2 should explain *why*: the configured state can be part of what makes the asset show-ready.

## 3.3 Chain-of-custody thinking

The learner should practice a simple handoff discipline:

**received from → verified → used/staged → handed to → returned to**.

This need not become bureaucratic paperwork for every cable or stand. It should be applied proportionately to assets whose loss, substitution, damage, or wrong preset can disrupt the show.

# 4. Module 2 — Setup Workflow & Readiness Verification

## 4.1 Backline

PRG treats Backline Technician as a distinct specialist classification and explicitly requires extensive knowledge of backline plus experience dealing with artists. It separately lists drum and guitar technician knowledge areas.

Course 2 should therefore move beyond “carry the instrument/amp carefully” into supervised setup-accountability concepts:

- identify the correct rig/item for the correct performer/position;
- preserve existing settings unless assigned to change them;
- verify required accessories/components are present;
- place equipment according to the stage plot or department instruction;
- keep performer-owned items controlled and protected;
- distinguish setup from tuning/repair/custom adjustment that exceeds the learner’s assignment;
- confirm readiness with the responsible technician/department lead.

The course should **not** imply that a junior specialist may independently alter an artist’s instrument setup, amplifier configuration, pedalboard, drum tuning, electronics, or other performer-specific settings merely because they helped place it.

## 4.2 Props

IATSE touring records show Props as a dedicated department with a traveling Head Prop-Person and local crews for take-in, performance, and put-out. That structure supports teaching props as a continuity-sensitive show department.

Junior-specialist training should emphasize:

- correct prop identity;
- correct preset location;
- orientation/readiness;
- safe handoff and reset;
- damage/missing-item reporting;
- no unapproved substitution or modification;
- awareness that a prop may have timing, performer, scenic, effects, or safety implications not obvious from appearance alone.

## 4.3 Wardrobe

IATSE Yellow Card records likewise show Wardrobe maintaining substantial dedicated performance crews and frequently a separate laundry assignment. This supports a workflow extending well beyond unloading costume cases.

Course 2 should teach awareness of:

- performer assignment;
- preset/location;
- condition and repair status;
- change/reset sequence;
- separation of clean/used/repair-needed items according to department procedure;
- accessories as part of the complete costume package;
- escalation of fit, damage, missing-item, contamination, or repair issues.

Detailed costume repair, fitting, alteration, quick-change choreography, laundry chemistry, and performer dressing practices should remain under the applicable department procedure and supervision.

# 5. Module 3 — Escalation, Discrepancies & Handoffs

The most important Course 2 behavior is **do not silently improvise around a discrepancy**.

Teach a discrepancy ladder:

1. Stop the affected setup/reset if continuing could create the wrong show state.
2. Identify the discrepancy precisely.
3. Preserve the current condition rather than making speculative changes.
4. Notify the assigned lead/head/technician.
5. Record or mark the issue using the department’s actual workflow if instructed.
6. Resume only after the responsible person gives direction.

Examples suitable for scenario assessment:

- the correct case is present but one expected accessory is missing;
- two visually similar props have conflicting labels;
- a performer-specific backline item arrives with an unfamiliar setting;
- a costume component is damaged during reset;
- a preset location is occupied or unsafe;
- an item is found in the wrong case at strike;
- a rental asset’s identifier does not match the expected package.

The desired behavior is **accurate escalation, not heroics**.

---

# 6. Inventory and reconciliation

The course should introduce reconciliation as a practical habit:

**what should be here → what is actually here → what condition is it in → what action is required before closeout**.

At junior level this may be a checklist, case count, labeled position check, accessory check, or lead-directed inventory pass. It should not be presented as one universal touring SOP.

The learner should understand that strike is not complete merely because equipment is offstage. Correct packing, case assignment, count, condition reporting, and handoff matter because the next venue or next performance depends on them.

---

# 7. Communication architecture

Course 2 should teach closed-loop department communication:

- state the item/location/problem specifically;
- identify what has and has not been changed;
- repeat critical instructions when necessary;
- confirm completion;
- avoid vague reports such as “something is wrong with the prop case.”

Useful format:

**asset + location + observed state + action already taken + requested decision**.

Example structure: “Guitar rack B, stage right: the labeled power supply is not in the case. I have not substituted anything. Who should verify the spare?”

This is a Crew Blueprint communication framework, not a quoted industry standard.

---

# 8. Recommended terminal capability

A learner completing Course 2 should be able to:

> Accept responsibility for a defined set of backline, prop, or wardrobe setup/reset tasks; identify and preserve the intended state of assigned assets; follow plots, labels, checklists, and department instructions; verify readiness and handoffs; reconcile assigned items at strike; and escalate missing, damaged, mismatched, unsafe, or ambiguous conditions without making unauthorized substitutions or changes.

This does **not** make the learner a Head Prop-Person, Head Wardrobe, artist instrument technician, department supervisor, or independent repair specialist.

---

# 9. Recommended three-module architecture

## Module 1 — Asset Ownership, Identity & State

- department structure;
- asset classes;
- custody/accountability;
- labels and identifiers;
- performer/show sensitivity;
- state preservation;
- condition awareness.

## Module 2 — Setup Workflow & Readiness

- plots/checklists/instructions;
- staging and preset;
- accessory completeness;
- readiness verification;
- handoffs;
- strike/reset/repack;
- basic reconciliation.

## Module 3 — Escalation & Exception Handling

- missing items;
- damage;
- conflicting labels/instructions;
- wrong preset/state;
- unsafe setup;
- unauthorized substitution boundary;
- clear escalation communication;
- post-use discrepancy reporting.

---

# 10. Evidence limitations

There is no single public national U.S. SOP governing asset accountability across backline, props, and wardrobe. Practices vary substantially by production, rental vendor, artist, venue, union local, department head, and asset-management system.

Therefore Crew Blueprint should distinguish:

- **documented department structure and specialist roles** from IATSE/industry sources;
- **company/product-specific procedures** where used;
- **Crew Blueprint instructional frameworks** such as the identify–verify–preserve–reconcile model.

Do not present the proposed workflow as an IATSE rule or universal touring SOP.

## Curriculum decision

**Adopt Course 2 as an accountability course, not merely a more advanced handling course.**

The defining junior-specialist capability is reliable custody of a limited show-critical scope: knowing what the assigned asset is, what state it should be in, where and when it belongs, how to verify it, and when to stop and escalate.
