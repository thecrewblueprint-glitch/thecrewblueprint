# Note to Research Package 11 — Sensitive Show Items and Performer-Owned Gear

**Date:** 2026-08-15  
**References:** `11-ground-hand-backline-props-wardrobe-support.md`

## Purpose of this note

Research Package 11 sharpened an important curriculum point: **backline, props, and wardrobe should not be taught as one generic category of miscellaneous production gear.**

All three can involve ordinary physical labor from local/general hands, but the consequences of casual handling differ because the items may be performer-owned, preset-dependent, show-critical, fragile, custom-built, fitted, or irreplaceable.

The transferable ground-hand skill is therefore not specialist operation. It is **ownership sensitivity, permission discipline, and preservation of the department's setup.**

## Recommended teaching distinction

Teach learners to identify four increasing levels of handling sensitivity:

1. **General production support item** — tables, chairs, generic work lights, empty carts, general support cases.
2. **Department-owned technical or show equipment** — backline cases, prop trunks, wardrobe trunks/racks, specialty support items.
3. **Show-critical preset or sequence-dependent item** — props in exact preset positions, wardrobe quick-change layouts, instruments or hardware already arranged for performance.
4. **Performer-owned or performer-specific item** — instruments, personal accessories, fitted costume pieces, artist-specific setups.

As sensitivity increases, the hand should require increasingly explicit direction before moving, opening, rearranging, adjusting, cleaning, or repacking the item.

This hierarchy is a **Crew Blueprint curriculum model**, not an industry-standard published classification.

## The key distinction: moving vs. changing

A ground hand may be assigned to **move** a case, trunk, rack, riser, amp, prop, table, or other item.

That does not automatically authorize the worker to **change**:

- settings;
- contents;
- preset location;
- orientation;
- packing method;
- labeling;
- fit;
- repair condition;
- cable arrangement;
- artistic appearance.

A concise teaching rule is:

> **Moving an assigned item is labor. Changing its setup, contents, preset, settings, condition, or placement logic is department work unless specifically assigned.**

## Why backline deserves special treatment

Backline commonly includes artist-facing equipment and exact performer preferences. PRG's specialist Backline Technician role explicitly requires extensive backline knowledge and experience dealing with artists. This supports treating performer equipment as more sensitive than ordinary freight.

A general hand should therefore avoid assuming that an instrument, amplifier, pedalboard, keyboard, drum component, or microphone position is free to adjust simply because it is physically accessible.

## Why props deserve special treatment

Props can be visually similar while serving different cue functions. Their exact backstage/stage preset can be part of the performance.

The novice mistake to prevent is **"cleaning up" a preset**. An item placed alone in a wing, on a table, or near an entrance may be exactly where it needs to be for a cue.

The correct default is:

> If you do not know whether an item is preset, ask before moving it.

## Why wardrobe deserves special treatment

Wardrobe is a distinct craft with fit, continuity, maintenance, laundry, repair, and quick-change responsibilities. A closed trunk or rack can be moved as freight when the wardrobe lead directs it; the contents should not therefore be treated as general-hand material.

New hands should be taught not to reorganize costume racks, place tools/drinks on wardrobe surfaces, move labeled performer pieces, or open costume trunks simply to help.

## Communication model

For sensitive items, teach **confirm-before-intervention** language:

- “Is this ready to move?”
- “Is this preset, or can I move it?”
- “Do you want the case moved, or the item itself?”
- “Where do you want this staged?”
- “Do you want this opened or left closed?”
- “I haven’t handled this before—show me where you want me to grab it.”

These are Crew Blueprint teaching phrases, not standardized national callouts.

## Curriculum implication

For entry-level stagehand education, the important competence is:

**Identify ownership → identify the responsible department → ask before intervening → handle carefully → preserve the setup → report problems instead of improvising.**

This framing should apply not only to backline, props, and wardrobe, but also to any production item that is custom, calibrated, preset, labeled for a performer, or otherwise show-critical.

The lesson is broader than “do not touch expensive gear.” It is:

> **Respect the invisible information attached to an object: ownership, preset, cue, configuration, fit, packing logic, and department responsibility.**
