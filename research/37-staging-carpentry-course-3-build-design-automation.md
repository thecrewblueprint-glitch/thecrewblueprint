# 37 — Staging/Carpentry Course 3: Build Design & Automation

**Research Queue ID:** CC-006  
**Status:** Complete research packet — safety-bounded  
**Research date:** 2026-08-17  
**Purpose:** Define the mastery-level curriculum progression from staging/carpentry lead into design coordination, temporary-structure documentation, automated/moving-scenery systems awareness, commissioning, and authority boundaries.

---

## Decision question

**What belongs at mastery level after a staging/carpentry worker can already lead supervised modular builds and verify work against an approved plan?**

## Bottom-line finding

Mastery should shift from **executing and leading a build** to **understanding how a build is specified, documented, reviewed, integrated, commissioned, and changed safely**.

The progression is:

**component handling → supervised assembly → build leadership → requirements/design interpretation → structural/automation interface awareness → documentation → qualified review → commissioning/inspection → controlled change management**

Crew Blueprint must preserve a hard boundary: a course does not create structural-engineering authority, competent-person status, machinery/automation authorization, rigging qualification, or permission to modify load-bearing systems.

ESTA’s Technical Standards Program is the principal entertainment-industry standards anchor. Its standards cover temporary structures, stage machinery, performer flying, rigging and related entertainment technology. These standards demonstrate that advanced staging is an engineered systems domain, not simply advanced carpentry technique.

---

# 1. Mastery is design coordination, not unrestricted design authority

A mastery learner should understand how to work with:

- production/design intent;
- approved drawings and specifications;
- manufacturer systems;
- structural-engineering requirements;
- rigging interfaces;
- electrical/automation interfaces;
- venue/site constraints;
- inspection requirements;
- change-control procedures;
- responsible qualified specialists.

They should be able to identify when a proposed change affects assumptions that require engineering or specialist review.

---

# 2. Temporary-structure systems thinking

Teach a stage/platform/scenic build as a system of interacting requirements:

- intended use;
- geometry/configuration;
- manufacturer or engineered system;
- support/load path concept;
- access/egress interfaces;
- guard/protection requirements where applicable;
- environmental/site conditions;
- connection to other production systems;
- inspection and handoff.

The learner’s mastery skill is to recognize dependencies and documentation requirements—not independently calculate or approve structural capacity without appropriate qualification.

---

# 3. Drawings and documentation

Course 3 should teach learners to read and coordinate professional documentation at a higher level:

- plan views;
- elevations;
- sections/details;
- component schedules;
- assembly/manufacturer documentation;
- revision identifiers;
- interface notes;
- inspection/check records;
- change records.

The central rule is:

> **The field build must remain traceable to the approved information governing it.**

If field conditions require a material change, the learner should know that approval may need to return to the appropriate designer, engineer, manufacturer, production authority, or other responsible specialist.

---

# 4. Structural interface awareness

Teach concepts only to the level necessary for safe coordination:

- load path as a concept;
- distributed versus concentrated demand awareness;
- stability/bracing as engineered or manufacturer-defined features;
- why hardware substitutions can matter;
- why removing/adding components can alter system behavior;
- why uneven ground/site conditions affect temporary structures;
- why suspended/rigged interfaces require qualified rigging coordination.

Do not teach learners to improvise structural modifications.

---

# 5. Automation and moving scenery

Automation should be introduced as a separate machinery/control discipline that interfaces with staging/carpentry.

Mastery-level staging learners should understand:

- moving scenery creates changing hazard zones;
- mechanical, electrical, control, sensing and emergency systems interact;
- commissioning and operation require defined authority;
- guarding/access and communication matter;
- changes to scenery can affect automation assumptions;
- rehearsals and controlled testing are part of system readiness;
- faults must be escalated to authorized automation personnel.

The course should not provide procedural instructions for operating, bypassing, modifying, or servicing hazardous machinery.

---

# 6. Cross-department interfaces

Advanced staging is highly interdependent.

Teach interface management with:

- rigging;
- lighting;
- audio;
- video/LED;
- electrics;
- automation;
- scenic;
- stage management/production;
- venue operations.

The mastery question is often not “Can staging do this?” but **“Whose system changes if staging does this?”**

---

# 7. Commissioning and inspection

A completed build should transition through defined readiness checks rather than becoming usable simply because assembly is finished.

Teach the workflow:

**approved design/instructions → controlled assembly → progressive checks → discrepancy correction → responsible inspection/acceptance → handoff/use → change control → strike**

The specific legal/engineering inspection responsibility varies by structure, jurisdiction, employer, venue, manufacturer, and project.

Course 3 should train learners to identify the required acceptance authority rather than assume the crew chief automatically owns it.

---

# 8. Change management

Field changes are a central mastery problem.

Learners should classify requested changes:

- within approved configuration and assigned authority;
- manufacturer-approved alternate configuration;
- production/design change requiring approval;
- structural change requiring engineering review;
- rigging/electrical/automation change requiring specialist review;
- unsafe/unclear condition requiring work to stop pending clarification.

This is a more valuable mastery skill than teaching improvisational “fixes.”

---

# 9. Recommended course structure

### Module 1 — Design Intent & Documentation
Drawings, revisions, specifications, manufacturer information, responsibility map.

### Module 2 — Temporary-Structure Systems Awareness
Configuration, load-path/stability concepts, site/environment and interfaces.

### Module 3 — Cross-Department Integration
Rigging, power, LED, scenic and production dependencies.

### Module 4 — Automation & Moving-System Awareness
Machinery/control architecture, hazard zones, authorization and escalation.

### Module 5 — Commissioning, Inspection & Change Control
Readiness, discrepancies, acceptance authority, revisions and handoff.

### Module 6 — Integrated Design-Coordination Scenario
A changing production brief requiring documentation review, interface identification, safe escalation and controlled handoff.

---

# 10. Assessment model

Use scenarios requiring the learner to:

- detect conflicts between drawings and field conditions;
- identify missing approval or documentation;
- recognize a proposed change that crosses into engineering/rigging/electrical/automation authority;
- coordinate several departments around a staging change;
- identify what must be verified before handoff;
- maintain revision/change traceability.

Do not assess by asking learners to perform unsupervised structural calculations or hazardous machinery procedures.

## Curriculum decision

**Staging/Carpentry Course 3 should teach advanced design coordination, documentation, systems interfaces, commissioning, and change-control judgment.** Structural engineering, rigging, electrical work, and automation operation/service remain separately qualified disciplines and should never be implied by completion of this course.
