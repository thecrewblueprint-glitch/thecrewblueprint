# 1. Show blacks — documented purpose and context

Package 16 established that show blacks are real event-production attire but are not OSHA PPE. These additional sources document the reason for the convention.

Western Oregon University's Theatre Student Handbook requires black clothing for run crew and explains that crew members may work in front of the audience in near-blackout conditions; the clothing provides a uniform, discreet appearance.

**Source:** Western Oregon University — Theatre Student Handbook  
https://wou.edu/theatre-dance/theatre/theatre-student-handbook/

The University of Minnesota Duluth's shift-crew guidance states that backstage crew default to all-black clothing because crew generally are not supposed to be noticed by the audience.

**Source:** University of Minnesota Duluth — Shift Crew Practicum Information  
https://www.d.umn.edu/~mharvey/th1699informationshiftcrew.html

Radford University's technical-theatre guidance lists running-crew blacks as black shirt, pants, shoes, and socks and separately lists a flashlight with clip among running-crew supplies.

**Source:** Radford University — Theatre & Cinema Current Students / Technical Requirements  
https://www.radford.edu/visual-performing-arts/theatre-and-cinema-department/current-students/index.html

IATSE Local 19 supplies useful professional context: load-ins/load-outs typically use work clothes, while many show calls use show blacks or dress blacks, with the exact requirement varying by venue and call.

**Source:** IATSE Local 19 — Instructions to Referral Stagehands / Code of Conduct  
https://www.iatse19.org/index.php/about/code-of-conduct/

## Curriculum conclusion

> **Show blacks are low-visibility black work clothes commonly required when crew may be visible during a performance. Their purpose is to keep backstage/run crew visually unobtrusive and professionally consistent. Exact requirements are production-, venue-, employer-, and call-specific.**

Do not teach show blacks as PPE or as a universal requirement for load-in/load-out.

# 2. Real stagecraft tool-list evidence

IATSE Local 99's referral rules publish a tools list and require new referrals to complete orientation/safety training before receiving work. The list includes photo identification, pen/pencil, and a tool bag/backpack, with additional tools depending on assignment.

**Source:** IATSE Local 99 — Job Referral Procedure and Rules / Tools List  
https://iatse99.org/about/job-referral-rules/

Radford University lists a tape measure and **8-inch crescent wrench** for technical-theatre work and a **flashlight with clip** for running crew.

**Source:** Radford University — Theatre & Cinema Technical/Design Requirements  
https://www.radford.edu/visual-performing-arts/theatre-and-cinema-department/current-students/index.html

SUNY New Paltz's production handbook lists a **Maglite/flashlight** and **multi-tool** among backstage/production supplies.

**Source:** SUNY New Paltz — Theatre Production Handbook  
https://www.newpaltz.edu/fpa/theatre/productions/production-handbook/

IATSE Local 122 requires referents to report with appropriate tools for assigned jobs and refers workers to its Required Tool List.

**Source:** IATSE Local 122 — Referral Hall Rules  
https://www.iatse122.org/resources/referral-hall-rules/

## Curriculum conclusion

A small adjustable/crescent wrench, flashlight, writing tool, and compact tool bag are well-supported examples of common stagecraft supplies. Exact required tools remain local/call-specific.

Do not imply that every new general hand should carry a knife, meter, drill, specialized rigging tool, or department-specific equipment.

# 3. Manufacturer-supported over-under cable coiling

Package 19 already supported over-under cable coiling. These sources strengthen the evidence by adding manufacturer training.

QSC's live-sound guidance states that repeated flexing and bending can damage cables internally and recommends proper coiling without sharp bends to extend cable life. QSC specifically says it favors the **over/under technique**.

**Source:** QSC — How to Ready Your Gear for the First Gig Back  
https://blogs.qsc.com/live-sound/how-to-ready-your-gear-for-the-first-gig-back/

QSC's training portal provides a step-by-step “How to Properly Roll a Cable” lesson using alternating over/under loops.

**Source:** QSC Training — How to Properly Roll a Cable  
https://training.qsc.com/mod/book/tool/print/index.php?id=1715

## Curriculum conclusion

> Over-under coiling is a professional cable-care technique that reduces stored twist, helps cable deploy cleanly, and can reduce repeated bend/kink stress. It is not an OSHA requirement, and specialty cables may have manufacturer-specific bend-radius or storage rules.

# 4. Show-call assignment and building trust — stronger local evidence

IATSE Local 101's hiring-hall rules are unusually explicit about the relationship between capability, reliability, load-in/load-out participation, and show calls.

Its referral criteria include:

- capability / demonstrated job skills;
- industry and recent job-specific experience;
- reliability;
- worker status such as journeyman, apprentice, casual, or new hire.

The rules state that the Job Steward/Leadman makes assignments according to production needs and worker abilities.

Most notably, Local 101 states that **show call is offered first to workers who are working both load-in and load-out**, unless specialized skill requires otherwise.

**Source:** IATSE Local 101 — Hiring Hall Referral Work Rules  
https://iatse101.net/hiring-hall-referral-work-rules/

The City of La Mirada's Stagehand I/II/III classification provides a separate real-world progression example: Stagehand I handles general setup/operation tasks, while Stagehand II can lead Stagehand I workers and take on more complicated responsibilities.

**Source:** City of La Mirada — Stagehand I/II/III Classification  
https://www.governmentjobs.com/careers/cityoflamirada/classspecs/1563876

## Curriculum conclusion

> New hands can build trust through reliable load-in/load-out participation and demonstrated capability. Some real hiring halls explicitly prioritize workers covering both load-in and load-out when offering show calls. This is **real evidence of one industry model, not a universal U.S. rule**.

# 5. Onboarding and probation — real structures, no national 30-day standard

There is still no national first-30-days stagehand onboarding standard. Real locals demonstrate substantial variation.

### IATSE Local 99

New applicants complete mandatory Orientation and Safety training before receiving/performing work offers. New referrals must then work an entire call within 90 days of completing orientation or reapply.

**Source:** IATSE Local 99 — Job Referral Procedure and Rules  
https://iatse99.org/about/job-referral-rules/

### IATSE Local 101

New hires complete a **180-day probationary period** used to evaluate continued placement on the hiring list.

**Source:** IATSE Local 101 — Hiring Hall Referral Work Rules  
https://iatse101.net/hiring-hall-referral-work-rules/

### IATSE Local 122

New applicants enter a referral system with a **one-year probationary period** after first dispatch.

**Source:** IATSE Local 122 — Referral Hall Rules  
https://www.iatse122.org/resources/referral-hall-rules/

## Curriculum conclusion

Keep the course's 30-day plan, but label it:

> **Crew Blueprint 30-Day Development Plan — a suggested learning sequence, not a standardized union/employer probation period.**

# 6. Headset/radio discipline and acknowledgement

Western Oregon University's handbook requires headset conversations during performance to remain focused on show work and prohibits extraneous conversation.

**Source:** Western Oregon University — Theatre Student Handbook  
https://wou.edu/theatre-dance/theatre/theatre-student-handbook/

University of Kansas theatre guidance likewise limits communication lines to production business and directs workers to communicate problems promptly to stage management.

**Source:** University of Kansas — Theatre Practicum Job Descriptions  
https://kutheatre.ku.edu/practicum-job-descriptions

Gettysburg College's theatre handbook states that headsets are used for production-required communication such as calls, warnings, and cues rather than casual conversation.

**Source:** Gettysburg College — Theatre Arts Production Handbook  
https://www.gettysburg.edu/academic-programs/theatre-arts/productions/student-handbook-departmental-productions

Arkansas State's theatre handbook uses a local acknowledgement convention: backstage calls are answered with “thank you” so stage management knows the call was heard. This is useful because it shows both that acknowledgement matters and that the exact phrase is local.

**Source:** Arkansas State University — Theatre Student Handbook  
https://kb.astate.edu/books/department-of-theatre/page/theatre-student-handbook

## Curriculum conclusion

Teach principles before vocabulary:

- keep headset/radio traffic brief and job-relevant;
- acknowledge important instructions;
- report problems promptly;
- do not talk over cues or critical traffic;
- follow the communication convention established by the production.

`Copy`, `heard`, `standing by`, or `thank you` may be taught as examples, not universal mandatory terminology.

# 7. Arrival and check-in expectations

IATSE Local 122 requires referred workers to report to the Job Steward **15–20 minutes before** scheduled start time, arrive with appropriate tools, and follow site rules.

**Source:** IATSE Local 122 — Referral Hall Rules  
https://www.iatse122.org/resources/referral-hall-rules/

IATSE Local 205's published hall regulations similarly direct referents to report to the Job Steward about **15 minutes before** start time and sign in on arrival.

**Source:** IATSE Local 205 — Referral Hall Regulations  
https://old.iatse205.org/hiring-hall/hall-regulations/

## Curriculum conclusion

> Arrive early enough to locate the correct entrance/check-in point, report to the assigned steward or lead, receive instructions, and be ready to work at call time.

Do not present “15 minutes early” as a universal law; it is a documented local example of the broader readiness expectation.

# 8. Fatigue, low light, and load-out risk factors

NIOSH's current fatigue guidance states that work-related fatigue is associated with night work, extended hours, physically/mentally demanding work, stress, and hot environments. Fatigue can slow reaction time, reduce attention/concentration, impair short-term memory, and impair judgment.

**Source:** NIOSH/CDC — Fatigue and Work  
https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/fatigue/about/index.html

NIOSH separately notes that long hours and nonstandard schedules can increase fatigue-related safety risk.

**Source:** NIOSH — Working Hours and Fatigue  
https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/blogs/2023/fatigue.html

OSHA's materials-handling guidance states that low visibility or poor lighting increases the chance of trips and falls and recommends adequate lighting for low-light work areas.

**Source:** OSHA — Materials Handling: Heavy Lifting  
https://www.osha.gov/etools/electrical-contractors/materials-handling/heavy

## Curriculum conclusion

This strengthens Package 18's qualified formulation:

> Load-out may occur after a long work period or performance, at night or under reduced-light conditions. Fatigue and poor visibility are independently documented risk factors, so strike should remain controlled and sequenced rather than becoming a race.

This still does **not** establish that every load-out is statistically more dangerous than every load-in.

# 9. End-of-call cleanup and reset

East Texas Baptist University's theatre handbook states that after work calls, scenic/lighting/costume crews are responsible for removing debris/materials and thoroughly sweeping rehearsal/performance floors.

**Source:** East Texas Baptist University — Theatre Student Handbook  
https://www.etbu.edu/academics/academic-schools/school-communication-performing-arts/department-music-and-theatre-arts/theatre-student-handbook

OSHA 29 CFR 1910.22 independently requires walking-working surfaces to be maintained in clean, orderly, and hazard-free condition.

**Source:** OSHA — 29 CFR 1910.22, Walking-Working Surfaces  
https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.22

## Curriculum conclusion

“Trash, tape, sweep, and final cleanup” is defensible as real stagecraft practice supported by both documented theatre procedure and OSHA housekeeping principles. Exact end-of-call duties remain venue/employer-specific.

# Bottom line

Package 20 remains the primary gap audit. Package 20A closes several residual evidence-quality gaps by adding **professional/local tool and attire evidence, manufacturer cable guidance, explicit show-call referral rules, real probation structures, communication/check-in practices, and current fatigue/visibility evidence**.

The remaining limitations are genuine features of the U.S. industry rather than missing research: many work rules are local, employer-specific, venue-specific, or production-specific rather than nationally standardized.
