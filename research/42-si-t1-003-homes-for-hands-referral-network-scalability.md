# 42 — Is Homes for Hands Scalable as a Referral Network?

**Research Queue ID:** SI-T1-003  
**Status:** Complete research packet — model decision  
**Research date:** 2026-08-17  
**Purpose:** Determine whether Homes for Hands can scale as a housing-referral layer for mobile live-event workers without becoming an unmanageable housing operator, tenant-screening business, or high-liability marketplace.

---

## Decision question

**Can Homes for Hands scale as a referral network connecting traveling live-event workers with short-term housing?**

## Bottom-line finding

**Yes, but the scalable version is a referral/coordination network—not a landlord, housing guarantor, or tenant-screening bureau.**

The strongest operating model is:

**verified housing partner directory → standardized minimum listing data → worker request/match → direct provider-worker agreement → post-stay quality feedback → partner re-verification**

The network should minimize collection of sensitive personal data, avoid making eligibility decisions based on consumer-report information unless it is intentionally prepared to comply with the Fair Credit Reporting Act, and maintain clear fair-housing and nondiscrimination rules.

A 2026 Greenheart International partnership with Lanyard is direct evidence that seasonal-worker housing coordination is a real commercial/service problem: Greenheart partnered with a housing specialist specifically to help host organizations identify affordable short-term housing near job sites for seasonal workers.

The VA's common-referral work provides a second useful systems lesson: referral networks become more efficient when partners agree on **only the information actually necessary for placement**, rather than forcing every applicant through a different long form.

---

# 1. What problem Homes for Hands should solve

Mobile production workers often face a mismatch between:

- short calls or several-week runs;
- uncertain work schedules;
- hotel pricing;
- standard long-term lease requirements;
- deposits and application friction;
- distance between lodging and venue/site;
- limited local knowledge.

The network's useful function is therefore not simply "find a room." It is to reduce the transaction cost of finding **work-compatible temporary housing**.

Relevant listing dimensions include:

- location relative to major venues/work zones;
- date availability;
- nightly/weekly/monthly price;
- minimum stay;
- parking;
- late-night access;
- kitchen/laundry availability;
- basic house rules;
- whether multiple crew members can stay;
- accessibility information where supplied;
- contact and booking method.

Do not collect information merely because it might be interesting later.

---

# 2. Referral network versus housing operator

These are materially different businesses.

## Referral network

Homes for Hands:

- identifies or onboards housing partners;
- presents standardized listing information;
- routes inquiries;
- provides availability/request workflow;
- gathers feedback;
- removes unreliable partners.

The housing provider and worker contract directly.

## Housing operator

Homes for Hands leases or owns properties, collects rent, controls occupancy, handles maintenance, holds deposits, or guarantees stays.

That model creates substantially greater capital, landlord-tenant, insurance, operational, and local-regulatory exposure.

### Recommendation

Begin as a **referral and coordination layer**. Do not become the landlord merely to make the network feel more complete.

---

# 3. Standardized referral is a scaling mechanism

The Department of Veterans Affairs documented a useful referral-system redesign in Orlando. Nine temporary-housing providers previously used different referral forms ranging from a few pages to more than ten pages. The VA worked with partners to identify the common information actually needed for an admission decision and created a two-page common referral form.

The important systems lesson is transferable:

> **A network scales when the intake schema is standardized and minimal.**

For Homes for Hands, a common housing request could contain only:

- city/worksite area;
- arrival/departure dates;
- number of occupants;
- vehicle/parking need;
- approximate budget;
- basic accessibility need if voluntarily provided;
- preferred contact method.

Anything else should have a clear operational reason.

---

# 4. Partner model

Potential partners may include:

- short-term housing operators;
- extended-stay properties;
- hostels where appropriate;
- workforce/seasonal housing providers;
- private room providers operating lawfully;
- campgrounds/RV sites for workers using vehicles;
- organizations already housing seasonal or touring personnel.

The network should verify at least:

- real property/business identity;
- real contact information;
- stated location;
- pricing terms;
- availability process;
- basic safety/occupancy representations;
- nondiscrimination agreement;
- whether the provider—not Homes for Hands—handles lease/rental compliance.

The network should avoid describing a property as legally compliant unless it has a defensible verification process for that exact claim.

---

# 5. Trust and safety architecture

## 5.1 Listing verification

Use graduated verification badges such as:

- contact verified;
- address/property verified;
- business/property-manager verified;
- repeat-worker reviewed.

Do not use a vague "safe" badge that could be interpreted as a guarantee.

## 5.2 Incident reporting

Create a structured process for reports involving:

- listing mismatch;
- unsafe physical condition;
- payment dispute;
- harassment/discrimination;
- unauthorized entry/privacy problem;
- cancellation/nonavailability;
- serious misconduct.

The network should be able to suspend a listing while reviewing serious reports.

## 5.3 Review integrity

Only permit reviews from confirmed referrals/stays where feasible. Separate objective fields—cleanliness, accuracy, communication, worksite distance—from free-text comments.

---

# 6. Fair housing boundary

HUD states that the Fair Housing Act prohibits discrimination in housing-related activities based on race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, and disability.

Homes for Hands should therefore have an explicit nondiscrimination policy for participating housing providers and should not build matching logic that intentionally excludes protected classes.

Local and state protections may be broader than federal law and require separate legal review as the network expands.

---

# 7. Tenant screening / FCRA boundary

This is a major scalability risk.

The FTC explains that companies providing reports about an individual's credit, rental history, criminal history, character, reputation, personal characteristics, or mode of living for housing eligibility may be consumer reporting agencies under the Fair Credit Reporting Act.

In July 2026, the FTC announced a $2.25 million settlement involving RentGrow over alleged failures to ensure accuracy in tenant-screening reports. The enforcement action illustrates that tenant-screening data is not a casual feature.

### Recommendation

Homes for Hands should **not initially create its own tenant-screening score or background-report product**.

If a housing provider requires screening, the provider should manage its own lawful screening process or use an established compliant service directly.

Homes for Hands should not receive unnecessary copies of credit reports, criminal-history reports, Social Security numbers, or identity documents merely to facilitate a referral.

---

# 8. Privacy / data-minimization model

Separate data into three layers.

## Public listing data

Property/provider information intended for workers.

## Referral data

Only what is necessary to route the housing request.

## Sensitive transaction data

Payment details, identity verification, lease documents, screening reports, and other sensitive materials should normally remain between provider and worker or a specialized processor.

### Principle

> **Homes for Hands should know enough to make a good referral, not enough to become a high-value identity-data repository.**

---

# 9. Marketplace scaling stages

## Stage 1 — Curated directory

Manually verify a limited set of properties/providers in a few anchor markets.

Success metric:

- successful referrals;
- repeat providers;
- low complaint rate;
- meaningful savings versus ordinary short-term lodging.

## Stage 2 — Structured availability network

Providers maintain availability/pricing windows and workers submit standardized requests.

## Stage 3 — Partner portal

Providers manage listings, availability, house rules, and referral responses.

## Stage 4 — Integrated marketplace only if justified

Payments, deposits, insurance products, or platform-managed booking create additional regulatory and operational burden. Add them only when transaction volume justifies specialized legal/compliance infrastructure.

---

# 10. Network effects and cold-start problem

A housing referral network is useful only when both sides have density in the same geography and dates.

Therefore do not launch nationally with sparse listings.

Use a market-cluster strategy:

1. choose live-event markets with recurring production demand;
2. recruit enough housing supply to create actual choice;
3. route workers from known labor networks;
4. collect verified usage data;
5. expand to adjacent markets.

A city with 20 reliable beds can be more useful than a national map containing one stale listing in every state.

---

# 11. Business model options

Lower-risk options include:

- free worker referrals funded by provider subscription;
- provider listing fee;
- employer/labor-company partnership subscription;
- sponsorship by industry organizations;
- transparent referral fee where legally appropriate and disclosed.

Avoid making the model depend on opaque markups to housing costs, because affordability is part of the network's value proposition.

Any transaction/booking fee model should receive legal review for applicable lodging, marketplace, tax, and local licensing rules.

---

# 12. Key metrics

Track:

- active verified providers by market;
- beds/rooms available by date;
- worker requests;
- successful matches;
- time to match;
- average worksite distance;
- average worker-reported lodging cost;
- estimated savings versus reasonable local alternatives;
- cancellation rate;
- complaint rate;
- repeat-provider rate;
- repeat-worker usage;
- markets with enough supply to be considered operational.

Do not call a city "covered" merely because one provider signed up once.

---

# 13. Current answer to SI-T1-003

**Homes for Hands is scalable if it stays narrow.**

The viable first product is a trusted, standardized, low-data referral layer for work-compatible temporary housing. The riskiest premature moves are becoming the landlord, performing tenant screening, holding sensitive identity data, guaranteeing property safety, or launching nationally before local supply density exists.

## Systems decision

Proceed with Homes for Hands as a **curated referral network MVP**, concentrated in a small number of live-event markets. Standardize minimal referral data, make providers responsible for their own housing agreements and screening, maintain fair-housing rules, and delay payments/tenant-screening/landlord functions until usage proves that the additional compliance burden is justified.
