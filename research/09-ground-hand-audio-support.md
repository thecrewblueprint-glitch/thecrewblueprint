# Research Package 09 — Ground-Hand Support of Audio Departments in U.S. Live Events

**Research date:** 2026-08-15  
**Scope:** General stagehand / ground-hand support of professional audio departments during load-in, PA build, show support, and load-out. This is not an audio-technician training document.

## Research question

What does a general stagehand actually do to support an audio department at arenas, theaters, festivals, and corporate events, and where should the boundary be drawn between useful physical support and specialist audio work?

---

# Executive conclusion

The strongest evidence supports a clear distinction:

**Ground hands support the physical workflow of the audio department under direction. Audio technicians own the system decisions.**

Ground-hand support commonly includes moving and staging speaker cabinets, subs, cases, stands, wedges, cable trunks, and other audio equipment; helping position gear; running cable under direction; striking and coiling cable after the department clears it; and, in some companies, connecting loudspeakers under direct supervision.

PRG is unusually explicit: its current Stagehand competency profile says an experienced stagehand may, **under guidance**, connect loudspeakers and lights. The same PRG portal separately defines Audio Crew Chief, FOH Engineer, Monitor Engineer, System & PA Calibration Technician, Wireless Technician, Network Technician, and other specialist roles, which is strong evidence that physical support and audio-system ownership are not the same job.

**Primary source:** PRG Crew Portal  
https://crew.prg.com/en/

Encore's current event-technology postings similarly describe setup/strike, equipment movement, truck loading/unloading, and cable routing as baseline production duties, while reserving technical operation and troubleshooting for technicians.

**Source:** Encore Global, Event Audio Visual Technician and Technical Services Representative postings  
https://jobs.encoreglobal.com/

The key curriculum rule is:

> **Move it, stage it, carry it, run it, and connect it only when specifically directed. Do not reconfigure, tune, repatch, retune, troubleshoot, or alter audio systems unless you are assigned and qualified to do so.**

# 1. Tasks ground hands are actually assigned

## A. Moving and staging loudspeakers, subwoofers, wedges, and cases

This is one of the clearest support tasks.

Encore's current technical-services postings explicitly include loading/unloading trucks, moving heavy equipment, setting up and dismantling AV systems, and handling equipment with care. PRG's Stagehand profile describes the stagehand role as physically demanding event work and separates it from specialist audio positions.

**Sources:**

PRG Crew Portal — Stagehands  
https://crew.prg.com/en/

Encore Global — Technical Services Representative  
https://jobs.encoreglobal.com/

### Curriculum interpretation

A ground hand can reasonably be taught to:

- identify speaker and subwoofer cases;
- move cabinets only by intended handles or approved handling points;
- use team carries when size/weight requires it;
- stage cases where the audio lead requests them;
- keep grille faces, rigging hardware, connectors, and exposed control surfaces protected from impact;
- avoid inventing a stack or orientation that has not been requested.

This is **handling competence**, not authority to decide loudspeaker placement or system geometry.

---

## B. Assisting with line-array assembly under direction

This requires careful framing.

L-Acoustics documentation shows that professional line-array cabinets such as KARA II include dedicated handles and integrated rigging components, and that the manufacturer provides specific rigging accessories, inter-enclosure angle settings, bumpers, extension bars, pullback hardware, and dedicated owner manuals.

**Sources:**

L-Acoustics KARA II product page  
https://www.l-acoustics.com/products/Kara-II/

L-Acoustics Documentation Center — KARA II / K2 owner manuals  
https://www.l-acoustics.com/result-documentation-center/

This establishes an important boundary: line-array assembly is **manufacturer-specific mechanical work**. Cabinet pins, captive rigging hardware, angles, bumper geometry, stacking frames, and safe configurations differ by product family.

### Ground-hand role

Depending on the employer and crew structure, a general hand may:

- roll or carry cabinets to the build position;
- remove transport covers/case lids when directed;
- steady or position a cabinet while an audio/PA technician makes the approved mechanical connection;
- pass hardware or tools;
- help feed the next cabinet into position;
- help maintain a clear work area;
- follow the audio lead's sequence during the build or strike.

PRG's current Stagehand profile supports the idea that stagehands can connect loudspeakers **under guidance**, but this should not be interpreted as universal authorization for every stagehand to assemble any flown line array.

**Source:** PRG Crew Portal  
https://crew.prg.com/en/

### Curriculum boundary

Do not teach a generic online learner a universal line-array locking sequence. The safe procedure belongs to the specific loudspeaker system, manufacturer documentation, employer procedure, and supervising audio/rigging personnel.

# 2. Running and striking audio cable

Cable support is a recurring general-production task. Encore describes proper cable routing through venues as a foundational event-technology activity, and PRG allows stagehands to connect loudspeakers under guidance.

**Sources:**

Encore Event Audio Visual Technician  
https://jobs.encoreglobal.com/en/job/toronto/event-audio-visual-technician-gta-part-time/6228/95423032384

PRG Crew Portal  
https://crew.prg.com/en/

## Typical ground-hand support

Under direction, a hand may:

- pull speaker cable, analog multicore, XLR, network/data lines, or other audio cable along the route chosen by the department;
- carry cable trunks and looms;
- dress cable physically along an assigned path;
- keep connectors off dirty/wet surfaces where practical;
- stage slack where directed;
- strike cable only after the audio department clears it;
- coil or place cable in the case/bin specified by the department.

### Important distinction

The hand may physically route the cable without owning the signal path.

The audio department determines:

- what cable is used;
- what it connects to;
- the routing architecture;
- signal patching;
- network topology;
- amplifier/controller channels;
- labeling conventions;
- when a line is safe to disconnect.

# 3. Connector handling and strain relief

Neutrik's current XLR documentation emphasizes secure locking, rugged housings, and strain-relief systems specifically designed to protect the cable and connector from bending and pull-out forces.

**Sources:**

Neutrik NC3MXX / XLR connector documentation  
https://www.neutrik.com/en/product/nc3mxx-wob

Neutrik NC3FX-SPEC  
https://www.neutrik.com/en/product/nc3fx-spec

Neutrik 10-pole XLR documentation  
https://www.neutrik.com/en/product/nc10mxx-14-b

### Curriculum interpretation

For a general hand, the important transferable rules are:

- handle the connector body rather than yanking the cable;
- do not force connectors that do not align or seat normally;
- protect pins/contact surfaces from impact;
- do not use the cable as a lifting handle;
- do not create sharp bends or severe tension immediately behind the connector;
- if a connector does not seat normally, stop and get the audio technician rather than forcing it.

These principles are supported by the design intent of professional locking connectors and strain-relief systems. Exact connector families and locking methods vary.

# 4. Cable coiling: what can be said safely

There is broad industry practice favoring careful, non-twisted storage of professional cable, but no single U.S. law or universal entertainment-industry standard was found that mandates one exact hand-coiling method for every audio cable type.

Therefore Crew Blueprint should **not** present one shop's coiling convention as federal or universal industry law.

### Defensible curriculum framing

Teach the learner to:

- ask how that department wants the cable coiled or packed;
- preserve the cable's natural lay rather than introducing hard twists;
- avoid knots, kinks, tight bends, connector impacts, or dragging connector ends unnecessarily;
- keep different cable types separated according to the department's case/trunk system;
- never begin striking a cable merely because it appears unused.

The last point is especially important because a line may still be patched, powered, carrying signal, or needed for troubleshooting.

# 5. Stage monitors / wedges

Moving and placing monitor wedges under direction is consistent with general stagehand support, provided the worker is not deciding monitor layout, patch, gain, EQ, or mix.

PRG distinguishes the **Monitor Engineer** as a specialist with extensive experience in monitor consoles, speaker systems, and in-ear monitoring. This is useful evidence for the boundary.

**Source:** PRG Crew Portal — Audio Monitor Engineer  
https://crew.prg.com/en/

### Ground-hand support may include

- carrying wedges to stage positions identified by the audio team;
- orienting them as directed;
- bringing correct cases or cable trunks to the monitor world;
- assisting with cable runs;
- moving wedges during strike after clearance.

### Not ground-hand decisions

- monitor mix levels;
- EQ;
- feedback suppression;
- amplifier/controller settings;
- routing;
- patching;
- artist-specific monitor positions unless directed.

# 6. What a ground hand should NOT touch or adjust

## Console / mixer settings

PRG separately defines FOH and Monitor Engineers as roles requiring profound practical experience and knowledge of professional consoles and speaker systems.

**Source:** PRG Crew Portal  
https://crew.prg.com/en/

A general hand should therefore not adjust:

- faders;
- gain;
- EQ;
- dynamics;
- routing;
- snapshots/scenes;
- console preferences;
- monitor mixes;
- mute states;
- talkback or communications routing.

unless explicitly assigned within an authorized technical role.

## System tuning / calibration

PRG separately defines **System & PA Calibration Technician** as a specialist function requiring technical training/equivalent experience and product knowledge.

**Source:** PRG Crew Portal  
https://crew.prg.com/en/

Ground hands should not make decisions involving:

- system EQ;
- delay alignment;
- array processing;
- optimization measurements;
- loudspeaker presets;
- controller DSP;
- aiming or array geometry decisions.

## RF and wireless coordination

PRG separately lists an Audio Wireless Technician. Wireless coordination involves frequency planning and system-specific work beyond general labor support.

**Source:** PRG Crew Portal  
https://crew.prg.com/en/

A general hand may move wireless racks or microphone cases under direction but should not independently alter RF coordination, frequencies, gain structures, antenna distribution, or wireless configuration.

## Amplifier/controller settings

L-Acoustics treats amplified controllers and loudspeaker presets as defined technical system elements in its product ecosystem.

**Sources:**

L-Acoustics KARA II product/system documentation  
https://www.l-acoustics.com/products/Kara-II/

L-Acoustics Documentation Center  
https://www.l-acoustics.com/result-documentation-center/

A hand should not change amplifier/controller presets, routing, gain, limiting, or network settings.

## Microphone placement once set

Microphone placement is part of the audio engineer/technician's system and artistic setup. Unless told to reposition a mic or stand, a general hand should treat a set microphone position as deliberate.

This boundary is an **industry-practice inference** from the specialist role separation documented by PRG and the general chain-of-command model used throughout event production; no single universal U.S. standard was found saying "stagehands may never move a microphone." Therefore Crew Blueprint should phrase this as:

> **Do not move an already placed microphone, stand, DI, or patched input unless the audio lead asks you to.**

# 7. Common ground-hand mistakes

## Disconnecting cable because it "looks done"

This is a serious workflow mistake because physical appearance does not show whether a line is still patched, carrying signal, or required for testing.

### Curriculum rule

**Never strike or disconnect department cable until the audio lead clears it.**

This is a practical production rule rather than a specific federal standard.

## Pulling on the cable instead of the connector

Professional connectors are designed with locking mechanisms and strain-relief systems. Pulling from the cable places avoidable stress on those systems.

**Source:** Neutrik XLR documentation  
https://www.neutrik.com/en/product/nc3mxx-wob

## Forcing connectors

If an XLR, speaker connector, multipin, or data connector does not engage normally, forcing it can damage contacts, shells, keys, or locking mechanisms.

Connector families vary, so the correct teaching is **stop and ask**, not "push harder."

## Mishandling speaker cabinets

L-Acoustics KARA II provides dedicated side/rear handles and manufacturer-specific rigging components. This supports teaching workers to use designed handling points and avoid using rigging pins, connectors, grilles, or other components as improvised handles.

**Source:** L-Acoustics KARA II  
https://www.l-acoustics.com/products/Kara-II/

## Improvising rigging hardware or array geometry

Line-array angles, bumpers, pullbacks, frames, pins, and configurations are manufacturer-specific.

**Source:** L-Acoustics documentation  
https://www.l-acoustics.com/result-documentation-center/

A ground hand should never improvise those choices.

# 8. Communication between audio leads and ground crew

No single universal U.S. verbal vocabulary for PA builds was found that functions like a formal nationwide audio-call standard.

Actual calls depend on:

- touring company;
- rental house;
- venue;
- union local;
- loudspeaker system;
- audio crew chief;
- rigging setup.

### Common operational pattern

The audio lead or crew chief identifies:

- what gear moves next;
- where it is staged;
- which cable run is being pulled;
- when cabinets are connected;
- when the next enclosure is brought in;
- when a cable may be struck;
- when a completed task should stop and wait.

PRG's Audio Crew Chief description explicitly states that the role leads technicians and stagehands while planning, executing, and monitoring the audio technical requirements.

**Source:** PRG Crew Portal — Audio Crew Chief  
https://crew.prg.com/en/

### Best beginner communication model

Crew Blueprint should teach short confirmation rather than assumed terminology:

- "Which case?"
- "Where do you want it staged?"
- "Is this cable cleared to strike?"
- "Do you want this connected or just placed?"
- "Which side faces stage?"
- "I'm not familiar with this rigging hardware—who should make this connection?"
- "Copy."
- "Standing by."

These phrases are curriculum recommendations based on the chain-of-command model, not universal scripted industry calls.

# 9. Safety and PPE for audio ground support

## Overhead line-array work

The central hazard for a ground hand is exposure to a suspended or partially assembled loudspeaker array.

OSHA's crane/hoisting rules, although not written specifically for entertainment line arrays, provide strong general hazard principles: minimize worker exposure beneath hoisted loads and keep unnecessary employees outside the fall zone.

**Sources:**

OSHA 29 CFR 1926.1425 — Keeping clear of the load  
https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.1425

OSHA Steel Erection eTool — Working under loads  
https://www.osha.gov/etools/steel-erection/cranes

### Curriculum implication

A ground hand supporting a PA hang should:

- stay outside the fall zone unless their assigned task requires otherwise and site procedure permits it;
- never casually stand beneath a suspended array;
- follow the audio/rigging lead's exclusion zone and movement commands;
- stop moving cabinets or carts when a lift sequence requires the area to clear;
- not handle rigging hardware unless assigned, trained, and authorized.

## Heavy cabinet handling

Professional PA cabinets can be heavy. L-Acoustics lists KARA II at 26 kg / 57 lb per cabinet and provides multiple dedicated handles.

**Source:** L-Acoustics KARA II  
https://www.l-acoustics.com/products/Kara-II/

A general-hand course should emphasize team handling, intended handles, controlled movement, and avoiding pinch/crush zones rather than prescribing a one-person lift technique.

## Hearing protection

OSHA's occupational-noise standard requires employers to address hazardous noise exposure and establishes hearing-conservation requirements beginning at an 8-hour TWA of 85 dBA. Employers must provide appropriate hearing protectors under specified exposure conditions and train workers included in the hearing-conservation program.

**Source:** OSHA 29 CFR 1910.95 — Occupational noise exposure  
https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.95

For stagehands, practical exposure can occur during:

- PA testing;
- system tuning;
- soundcheck;
- show operation;
- high-output monitor checks.

### Curriculum implication

Teach workers to follow employer/site hearing-protection requirements and not assume hearing protection is unnecessary simply because they are not audio technicians.

## Basic PPE

Actual PPE is site- and task-specific, but audio ground support may involve:

- safety footwear;
- work gloves appropriate to handling cases/cabinets;
- hard hat where overhead-work rules require it;
- hearing protection where exposure requires it;
- eye protection when the task/site requires it;
- high-visibility apparel in traffic/logistics environments.

Crew Blueprint should not present a universal PPE list as a substitute for employer/site hazard assessment.

# 10. Arena, theater, festival, and corporate differences

## Arena / festival

More likely to involve:

- large flown line arrays;
- large subwoofer packages;
- extensive cable trunks;
- long case pushes;
- larger local crews;
- active rigging zones;
- high-output PA testing.

Ground hands may spend much of the audio call moving cabinets, feeding array builds, moving amp/control racks, pulling cable, and supporting strike.

## Theater

More likely to involve:

- installed house systems;
- touring package integration;
- tighter backstage spaces;
- carefully preserved microphone/monitor positions;
- house-specific cable routes;
- stricter coordination with resident staff.

The ground-hand role may therefore involve less large-scale PA assembly and more careful equipment movement and integration support.

## Corporate AV

Encore's current job descriptions show a stronger emphasis on smaller-scale system setup, cable routing, client/room requirements, and repeated setup/strike in hotels and convention centers.

**Source:** Encore Global Event Audio Visual Technician  
https://jobs.encoreglobal.com/en/job/toronto/event-audio-visual-technician-gta-part-time/6228/95423032384

Ground support may include portable speakers, stands, stage monitors, small mixers/racks, cable paths, breakout-room equipment movement, and strike.

# 11. Company- and product-specific guidance

The following should be explicitly flagged in future curriculum:

## Product-specific

- line-array cabinet pin/lock systems;
- allowable inter-cabinet angles;
- bumper/frame hardware;
- pullback hardware;
- speaker-cable connector types;
- amplifier/controller architecture;
- case/transport orientation.

L-Acoustics is used in this research as a concrete professional example, not as a universal model for all PA systems.

## Company-specific

PRG's rule that an experienced stagehand may connect loudspeakers under guidance is real evidence from PRG's current crew portal. It should not be converted into a universal statement that every general stagehand at every employer may connect every speaker system.

## Site/local-specific

- who may work inside the array-build area;
- who handles loudspeaker rigging pins;
- who operates motors;
- whether stagehands make speaker connections;
- required PPE;
- cable-coiling convention;
- callouts and radio vocabulary.

# 12. Recommended Crew Blueprint teaching model

For a beginner general-hand course, audio support should be taught as:

## Level 1 — Recognize and protect

Learner can identify:

- speaker/sub cases;
- wedges;
- cable trunks;
- audio racks;
- microphones/stands;
- obvious audio work zones;
- delicate or already configured equipment that should not be disturbed.

## Level 2 — Move and stage

Learner can, under direction:

- safely move cases and cabinets;
- stage gear in the requested location/orientation;
- place wedges where directed;
- keep paths and work zones organized.

## Level 3 — Cable support

Learner can, under direction:

- pull cable along an assigned route;
- protect connectors;
- avoid excessive tension/kinks;
- dress cable physically;
- wait for clearance before disconnecting or striking;
- coil/pack cable using the department's specified method.

## Level 4 — Assisted PA build

With employer/site instruction, an experienced hand may:

- position cabinets for assembly;
- assist a technician with array build logistics;
- hold/pass hardware/tools;
- connect loudspeakers if specifically directed and allowed by that employer;
- support the lift sequence without assuming rigging authority.

## Hard boundary

The ground hand does **not independently own**:

- console operation;
- microphone placement decisions;
- signal patching;
- DSP;
- system tuning;
- RF coordination;
- amplifier/controller programming;
- array design/geometry;
- loudspeaker rigging engineering;
- network configuration;
- troubleshooting decisions.

---

# Bottom line

A useful audio ground hand is not a junior FOH engineer. The role is to make the audio department faster and safer by moving, staging, carrying, routing, protecting, and striking gear correctly while preserving the technical system exactly as the audio crew intends it.

The strongest transferable rule is:

> **Support the physical workflow; preserve the technical setup.**

When the task shifts from moving/placing/protecting equipment into deciding how the system is connected, configured, tuned, routed, rigged, or operated, the worker has crossed from general-hand support into specialist audio work.
