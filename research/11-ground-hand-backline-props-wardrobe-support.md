# Research Package 11 — Ground-Hand Support for Backline, Props, Wardrobe, and General Production

**Research date:** 2026-08-15

## Scope

This package examines what a **general stagehand / ground hand** actually does to support backline, props, wardrobe, and general production-support functions at U.S. arenas, theaters, festivals, and corporate events.

The scope is deliberately **not** training a worker to become a backline technician, props technician, wardrobe technician/dresser, or production coordinator. The focus is how an ordinary hand supports those departments safely and professionally without disturbing performer-owned, show-critical, or department-controlled items.

---

# Executive conclusion

The strongest cross-source pattern is:

> **Move and stage what you are assigned; preserve the department's setup; do not adjust, reposition, alter, or handle show-critical/personal items without explicit direction.**

Backline, props, and wardrobe should **not** be taught as one generic category of miscellaneous gear. They differ materially:

- **Backline** often includes performer-owned or artist-specific instruments, amps, pedals, stands, drum hardware, and other equipment where settings and exact placement may matter.
- **Props** are often cue-dependent, preset-dependent, fragile, unique, or irreplaceable. Their location can be part of the show.
- **Wardrobe/costume** is a specialist craft involving fit, continuity, maintenance, laundry, repairs, quick changes, and performer interaction.
- **General production support** is broader and usually includes lower-risk logistics such as tables, chairs, signage, work lights, cases, carts, and support materials under production direction.

The curriculum implication is that the ground hand needs **handling discipline and permission awareness**, not specialist knowledge.

# 1. Backline: what the general hand actually supports

## Specialist boundary is real

PRG's current Crew Portal lists **Backline Technician** as a specialist role requiring broad backline knowledge and artist-facing experience. PRG describes progressively experienced backline technicians with at least approximately 3, 5, or 8 years of backline-event experience; the most experienced tier includes instructing other backline technicians. PRG separately identifies drum- and guitar-technician knowledge.

**Source:** PRG Crew Portal — Backline Technician  
https://crew.prg.com/en/

This is strong evidence that a ground hand moving backline cases is **not equivalent to a backline technician**.

## Ground-hand tasks supported by broader stagehand practice

General stagehands routinely unload, push, sort, stage, and reload road cases for departments. In a backline context, reasonable support tasks under the backline lead's direction include:

- moving backline road cases from truck/dock to assigned staging areas;
- placing cases where the backline technician requests them;
- helping move drum risers or rolling risers;
- carrying or positioning amp cabinets, stands, or hardware when explicitly assigned;
- helping place heavier equipment that requires a team carry;
- returning empty cases or lids to designated locations;
- keeping backline lanes and backstage work areas clear;
- striking and re-casing equipment only in the order and method directed by the backline technician or lead.

These tasks are consistent with general stagehand labor but should remain directed because backline equipment may be performer-specific or preset-specific.

## What is off-limits without direction

A ground hand should **not independently**:

- tune an instrument;
- change amplifier controls or channel/settings;
- move microphone positions already set around an instrument;
- alter pedalboards, effects settings, cable routing, patching, or power arrangement;
- reposition drum hardware after the backline technician has set it;
- alter keyboard/synth settings;
- move an artist's instrument simply because it appears to be in the way;
- open personal instrument cases or remove instruments without being told to do so.

These boundaries are an **analysis of the specialist-role evidence and live-production chain of command**, rather than a single universal national rulebook. Employer/tour procedures can be stricter.

## Why performer interaction matters

PRG specifically says a Backline Technician should have experience **dealing with artists**. That matters because backline is not merely heavy equipment; it may involve personal preferences, performer comfort, exact placement, instrument condition, and show continuity.

**Source:** PRG Crew Portal — Backline Technician  
https://crew.prg.com/en/

### Curriculum interpretation

Teach the ground hand:

> If it is an instrument or artist-specific setup, treat it as controlled gear. Move it only when assigned, use the indicated case/handle, and do not adjust anything unless the backline lead specifically directs you.

# 2. Props: support is common, but presets are show-critical

## Props is a distinct craft/department

IATSE touring documentation lists a **Head Prop-Person** separately from carpentry, electrics/audio/video, and wardrobe. Current touring examples also call substantial local props crews for take-in, performance, and put-out.

Examples:

### Spamalot National Tour

IATSE lists:

- Head Prop-Person
- 5 props workers for take-in
- 4 for performance
- 5 for put-out

**Source:** IATSE, Spamalot National Tour  
https://iatse.net/shows/spamalot-national-tour/

### Beauty and the Beast North American Tour

IATSE lists:

- Head Prop-Person
- 6 props workers for take-in
- 3 for performance
- 6 for put-out

**Source:** IATSE, Beauty and the Beast North American Tour  
https://iatse.net/shows/beauty-and-the-beast-north-american-tour/

### The Notebook Tour

IATSE lists:

- Head Prop-Person
- 6 props workers for take-in
- 2 for performance
- 6 for put-out

**Source:** IATSE, The Notebook Tour  
https://iatse.net/shows/the-notebook/

These examples establish that local hands may work props calls under a traveling Head Prop-Person without themselves owning the department.

## What props crews actually supervise

A current touring Props Assistant posting for *Operation Mincemeat* describes duties including:

- setting up and loading in the touring production with the Prop Head;
- supervising installation, operation, and disassembly of props;
- teaching local workers their tracks;
- supervising local crews during load-in/load-out;
- maintaining props and spares.

**Source:** Playbill, Props Assistant — Operation Mincemeat Tour  
https://playbill.com/job/props-assistant-operation-mincemeat-tour/42db6305-b5c8-4dbf-91b7-13af5f8d780d

This is direct evidence for the ground-hand model: **local hands handle and place props under the props department's supervision**.

## Real ground-hand support tasks

Depending on the show, general/local hands assigned to props may:

- unload and stage prop trunks/cases;
- carry or roll large props into the venue;
- move furniture or set-dressing items under direction;
- place items at locations identified by the props lead;
- help with backstage organization;
- assist in striking and re-packing props;
- carry or move show props as part of an assigned performance track after being trained on that track.

A current Performing Stagehand posting for an Off-Broadway show explicitly includes moving and setting props between scenes, operating curtains/scenic elements, and assisting show-specific backstage effects.

**Source:** Playbill, Performing Stagehand — Holiday Cover  
https://playbill.com/job/performing-stagehand-holiday-cover/03794855-5b25-4cc7-9572-a06747dfba46

This should **not** be generalized into "any stagehand may move any prop." That posting describes a specifically trained show position.

## What should remain off-limits without direction

Ground hands should not independently:

- move a prop from a preset position;
- substitute one prop for another;
- repair, modify, paint, cut, glue, or alter a prop;
- decide how a prop should be secured or operated;
- open/handle delicate or specialized props just to inspect them;
- re-pack show-critical pieces into a case unless the props lead has shown the correct method;
- treat stage weapons, pyrotechnic props, chemicals, animals, or other regulated/special hazards as ordinary props.

The final bullet is intentionally a **boundary statement**: those categories require separate safety procedures and qualified/specially assigned personnel.

## Why props need more handling discipline than ordinary freight

Professional props positions include fabrication, repair, maintenance, set dressing, storage, and delivery. A current Oregon Shakespeare Festival Props Artisan description includes building/repairing show props, transferring them to rehearsal/performance spaces, dressing scenery during load-ins, and selecting construction methods.

**Source:** Playbill, Props Artisan  
https://playbill.com/job/props-artisan/6deba66c-8660-4d36-a5b0-b0bc9561cc7c

A current Pacific Northwest Ballet Properties Assistant posting likewise describes substantial physical handling but as part of a dedicated properties job under an IATSE Local 15 agreement.

**Source:** Playbill, Properties Assistant  
https://playbill.com/job/properties-assistant/9c4c34f9-8330-482a-bf59-f0d576505aca

### Curriculum interpretation

The transferable rule should be:

> A prop's exact location can be part of the show. Never "clean up," consolidate, or relocate a prop preset unless the props lead tells you to.

# 3. Wardrobe: a separate specialist craft, not generic soft goods

## IATSE recognizes wardrobe separately

IATSE's Pink Contract touring guidance states that a touring member must be represented by the craft they travel under; it explicitly gives **wardrobe** and **props** as separate examples.

**Source:** IATSE, An Introduction to Pink Contract Touring  
https://iatse.net/about/stagecraft/touring-2/

Touring Yellow Card data similarly separates Head Wardrobe and local wardrobe crews.

Examples:

- *Spamalot*: Head Wardrobe plus 9 local wardrobe workers for take-in/performance/put-out.
- *Beauty and the Beast*: Head Wardrobe plus 10 local wardrobe workers for take-in/performance/put-out.
- *The Notebook*: Head Wardrobe plus 5 local wardrobe workers; one wardrobe worker is specifically assigned laundry.

**Sources:**  
https://iatse.net/shows/spamalot-national-tour/  
https://iatse.net/shows/beauty-and-the-beast-north-american-tour/  
https://iatse.net/shows/the-notebook/

## Wardrobe work is more specialized than simply carrying clothing

A current Wardrobe Dayworker posting requires intermediate sewing knowledge and assigns:

- washing and drying costumes;
- sorting and distributing show costumes;
- repairing garments, accessories, and shoes;
- consulting on complex repairs;
- reporting costuming issues.

**Source:** Playbill, Part Time Wardrobe Dayworker  
https://playbill.com/job/part-time-wardrobe-dayworker/ca1a6c30-2154-41f4-ae1f-1112b909d185

A current touring Wardrobe & Hair Supervisor posting emphasizes costume maintenance, backstage operations, performer readiness, and preserving the production's artistic look.

**Source:** Playbill, Wardrobe & Hair Supervisor  
https://playbill.com/job/wardrobe-hair-supervisor/d569fe64-ef51-45c4-8619-0ffcd07e081c

## Ground-hand support around wardrobe

A general hand who is **not assigned to wardrobe** may still be directed to:

- move closed wardrobe trunks/racks from truck to the assigned wardrobe area;
- help roll empty racks or cases;
- move tables, mirrors, chairs, work lights, or other support items as directed;
- help position trunks in a dressing/backstage space without opening or reorganizing them;
- return packed trunks to the loading area during strike.

These are logistics tasks, not wardrobe work.

## What should be off-limits without permission

A general ground hand should not independently:

- remove costumes from garment bags/trunks;
- rearrange costume presets;
- move labeled performer clothing;
- fold, wash, steam, iron, repair, alter, or treat costumes;
- place costumes on an unapproved surface;
- use a costume rack as a general storage rack;
- touch shoes, wigs, accessories, jewelry, or performer-specific costume items just because they are nearby.

These restrictions are **curriculum analysis derived from the dedicated-craft evidence**, not a single national wardrobe regulation. Specific productions may allow broader local assistance after wardrobe personnel train the assigned workers.

### Curriculum interpretation

Teach:

> Closed trunks and racks can be general freight when the wardrobe lead assigns you to move them. The contents are department-controlled items. Do not open, reorganize, clean, repair, or relocate costume pieces unless wardrobe specifically instructs you.

# 4. General production support

Not all support work belongs to a specialist department. General stagehands may also be assigned to move or set:

- tables;
- chairs;
- signage;
- stanchions;
- work lights;
- pipe-and-drape components;
- production office furniture;
- backstage support supplies;
- utility carts;
- trash/recycling equipment;
- hospitality or credentialing support furniture when assigned;
- general road cases and production trunks.

The same rule still applies: **location and ownership matter**. A stagehand should not decide that a production item is "in the way" and relocate it without checking with the appropriate lead.

This is consistent with the broader Crew Blueprint beginner-course framing that stagehands support multiple departments, move production equipment, and should avoid freelancing or touching department gear without direction.

# 5. Common mistakes and why they matter

## Backline mistakes

Common problems include:

- dropping or tipping instrument/amp cases;
- grabbing delicate equipment instead of designated handles/case structure;
- stacking cases contrary to labels or obvious case geometry;
- moving a tuned/preset instrument setup after the backline technician has placed it;
- altering amp, pedal, keyboard, or drum settings;
- disconnecting cables because they appear unused;
- placing drinks or loose objects on instrument/amp cases or musician equipment.

The general handling principles are analysis; exact backline handling procedures are **tour- and artist-specific**.

## Props mistakes

Common problems include:

- moving a preset because it looks cluttered;
- placing a show prop in the wrong wing or wrong side of stage;
- stacking other gear on prop trunks or delicate pieces;
- failing to protect decorative/scenic finishes;
- re-packing a custom object incorrectly;
- assuming two similar-looking props are interchangeable;
- borrowing a prop or production furniture item for another task without permission.

## Wardrobe mistakes

Common problems include:

- placing costumes on the floor or dirty work surfaces;
- crushing garment bags under cases/equipment;
- rolling racks carelessly into scenery or doorways;
- moving a costume preset to "organize" the area;
- mixing labeled performer items;
- placing tape, tools, drinks, or dirty gloves on wardrobe trunks/racks;
- opening a trunk or garment bag without being assigned.

Again, these are **best-practice curriculum deductions from the specialist-craft evidence**, not universal contractual language.

# 6. Etiquette and communication norms

The strongest professional norm is **permission before intervention**.

Useful ground-hand communication includes:

- "Where do you want these backline cases staged?"
- "Do you want this trunk opened or left closed?"
- "Is this ready to move?"
- "Which side of stage does this prop case go to?"
- "Do you want me to carry the item or the case?"
- "This is labeled for [performer/department]. Do you want it moved?"
- "I haven't handled this item before—show me where you want me to grab it."
- "Is this preset, or can I move it?"

These phrases are **Crew Blueprint teaching recommendations**, not standardized national callouts.

## Why deference is professional rather than passive

A dedicated backline technician may have years of artist-facing experience. A props head may be managing exact presets and show tracks. Wardrobe may be maintaining performer-specific continuity and quick-change sequences. In that environment, "helping" by changing something without permission can create more work than doing nothing.

A strong general hand is therefore proactive about **asking and moving**, but conservative about **adjusting and deciding**.

---

# 7. Cross-context differences

## Arena/concert/festival

Backline is usually more prominent. General hands may move large quantities of backline cases, rolling risers, amps, drums, keyboards, and support equipment under touring backline direction. Artist-owned gear and exact stage placement make permission especially important.

## Theater/touring musical

Props and wardrobe are more structurally distinct departments. IATSE Yellow Cards routinely call separate props and wardrobe crews under traveling department heads. Local stagehands can be assigned into those calls, but the department head/traveling staff establish the workflow.

## Corporate events

Backline may be minimal or absent except where live bands perform. General production-support handling—tables, chairs, signs, lecterns, scenic décor, work lights, backstage furniture, and client-facing items—may be more common. Branded scenic/prop elements can still be fragile or placement-specific.

## Employer/production variability

There is no single national rule defining exactly which general stagehands may handle backline, props, or wardrobe. Union agreements, tour yellow cards, venue jurisdiction, employer staffing models, artist preferences, and department-head instructions determine the practical boundary.

# 8. Evidence-based ground-hand task matrix

| Area | Appropriate support under direction | Do not independently do |
|---|---|---|
| Backline | Move/stage cases, risers, amps/hardware, team carries, strike/re-case as directed | Tune, change settings, alter pedal/amp/keyboard/drum setup, move artist instruments after preset |
| Props | Move trunks, carry/roll pieces, place where directed, trained show tracks | Change presets, substitute, repair/modify, operate specialized props without assignment |
| Wardrobe | Move closed trunks/racks and support furniture | Open trunks, rearrange presets, clean/repair/steam/alter costumes, handle performer items without permission |
| Production support | Tables, chairs, signs, work lights, carts, general support cases | Relocate department/client-critical items without confirming ownership/placement |

# 9. Curriculum recommendations

## Teach an "ownership sensitivity" hierarchy

A useful Crew Blueprint framework is:

**General production item → department-owned gear → show-critical preset → performer-owned/personal item**

As sensitivity rises, the hand should require increasingly explicit direction before touching, moving, opening, or altering the item.

This is a **Crew Blueprint synthesis model**, not an industry-standard published hierarchy.

## Teach the difference between moving and changing

The most transferable rule across backline, props, and wardrobe is:

> **Moving an assigned item is labor. Changing an assigned item's setup, contents, preset, settings, condition, or location logic is department work.**

A ground hand can become very valuable by learning how to move sensitive items carefully while resisting the urge to improve, reorganize, troubleshoot, or adjust them.

# Bottom line

A general hand can be extremely useful to backline, props, wardrobe, and production support without pretending to own any of those crafts.

The professional model is:

**Identify the responsible department → ask where/how the item should move → handle it carefully → preserve presets/settings/contents → report problems instead of improvising.**

For Crew Blueprint, the defining beginner skill is not knowing how to tune a guitar, repair a prop, or maintain a costume. It is knowing when an item is **sensitive, show-critical, department-controlled, or performer-owned—and treating that status as part of the job.**
