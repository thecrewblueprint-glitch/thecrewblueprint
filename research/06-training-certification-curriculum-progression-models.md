# Research Package 06 — Training and Certification Curriculum Progression Models in Live Events

**Research date:** 2026-08-15

## Research question

How do existing live-events and entertainment-technology training/certification systems structure learning from beginner to advanced, particularly across:

1. ETCP (Entertainment Technician Certification Program)
2. IATSE local apprenticeship structures
3. Tech Crew HQ (Australia)
4. ESTA/PLASA training and certification offerings

This package focuses on:

- how each system defines beginner vs. intermediate vs. advanced;
- whether lower tiers must be completed before higher tiers unlock;
- whether advancement is course-based, hours-based, exam-based, experience-based, or some combination;
- how general/foundational learning is separated from department-specific specialization.

---

# Executive conclusion

There is **no single dominant curriculum architecture** across the industry. The programs studied represent four distinct models:

1. **ETCP = experienced-worker certification model.** It does not train workers from beginner to advanced and does not require a lower certificate before a higher one. Readiness is established primarily through documented professional experience and an eligibility point system, followed by a rigorous discipline-specific exam.

2. **IATSE local apprenticeships = developmental workforce model.** These are the clearest U.S. examples of staged growth from new worker/apprentice toward journeyman or full-member status. Requirements vary substantially by local and can include aptitude tests, classes, logged hours, minimum service periods, written/practical exams, and demonstrated competence.

3. **Tech Crew HQ = beginner/foundational learning model.** Its public material explicitly describes the platform as starting-level/foundational education for backstage crews. It offers a broad foundation plus introductory department-specific modules, but there is no publicly documented locked beginner→intermediate→advanced progression.

4. **PLASA rigging pathway = staged professional-development model.** The Trainee Rigger Scheme gives new entrants a logbook and experience pathway; the National Rigging Certificate then assesses experienced practitioners against a professional standard. This is the closest example found of a clear **entry pathway → logged practical experience → advanced professional assessment** architecture.

The strongest curriculum lesson for The Crew Blueprint is therefore **not** to copy any one program wholesale. A robust progression would combine:

**Foundational orientation → department awareness → supervised practical competency → documented experience → independent-role competency → advanced professional assessment/credentialing where one exists.**

# 1. ETCP — Entertainment Technician Certification Program

## What ETCP actually is

ETCP is an ESTA-run personnel certification program for experienced entertainment technicians. It currently certifies:

- Rigger — Arena
- Rigger — Theatre
- Entertainment Electrician
- Portable Power Distribution Technician (PPDT)

ETCP states that its certifications focus on disciplines that directly affect the health and safety of crews, performers, and audiences.

**Source:** ETCP home/about  
https://etcp.esta.org/  
https://etcp.esta.org/etcp/about.html

## Important curriculum-design finding: ETCP is not a beginner-to-advanced course ladder

ETCP explicitly targets **experienced entertainment technicians**. Candidates qualify to sit for the exams through a points system that emphasizes work experience, although formal education, apprenticeships, and internships can contribute.

Current eligibility thresholds published by ETCP are:

- Arena Rigger: 30 points
- Theatre Rigger: 30 points
- Entertainment Electrician: 30 points
- Portable Power Distribution Technician: 20 points

**Source:** ETCP General Testing Info  
https://etcp.esta.org/certify/certify.html

This means ETCP's model is:

**accumulate professional experience → become eligible → sit for discipline-specific assessment → certify**

not:

**take Level 1 → unlock Level 2 → unlock Level 3.**

## Arena Rigger vs. Theatre Rigger are parallel scopes, not junior/senior tiers

A critical terminology point: **Arena Rigger and Theatre Rigger are not lower and higher levels of one rigging certification.** They are parallel certifications covering different systems and work contexts.

ETCP describes Arena Rigging as rigging that typically uses chain hoists and truss systems to temporarily suspend objects from overhead structures across arenas, theatrical venues, convention spaces, and similar environments.

Theatre Rigging covers traditional theatrical systems such as counterweight, mechanical, and hydraulic systems that are commonly permanently installed in theatres.

A worker may pursue either or both certifications.

**Source:** ETCP FAQ — difference between rigging examinations  
https://etcp.esta.org/etcp/faq.html

## How ETCP defines advanced readiness

Readiness is primarily defined through:

- documented professional experience;
- points from relevant apprenticeships/internships;
- points from formal education where allowed;
- discipline-specific examination performance.

For the Entertainment Electrician credential, ETCP states that the examination is intended to validate the competency of roughly the **upper third** of entertainment electricians.

**Source:** ETCP Entertainment Electrician  
https://etcp.esta.org/certify/certify_electrical.html

For the Portable Power Distribution Technician credential, ETCP states that the certification targets the **top two-thirds** of workers using that technology across corporate, trade show, outdoor event, theatrical, and film/TV environments.

**Source:** ETCP Portable Power Distribution Technician  
https://etcp.esta.org/certify/certify_ppdt.html

## Exam content is organized around professional bodies of knowledge

ETCP's exams are built from job analyses and discipline content outlines rather than course sequences.

For Arena Rigging, major exam domains include planning/engineering and practical rigging knowledge. Theatre Rigging similarly uses a job-analysis-derived content outline oriented around theatre-specific systems.

**Sources:**  
ETCP Arena Rigging Exam Content  
https://etcp.esta.org/certify/examination_rigger_arena.html  
ETCP Theatre Rigging Exam Content  
https://etcp.esta.org/certify/examination_rigger_theatre.html

## Does ETCP require lower tiers first?

**No.**

There is no ETCP beginner certification that must be completed before Arena Rigger, Theatre Rigger, Entertainment Electrician, or PPDT. Eligibility is based on experience/points, not possession of a lower ETCP credential.

A candidate can also hold multiple ETCP certifications where their experience supports it.

## General vs. department-specific structure

ETCP is overwhelmingly **department/scope specific**.

It does not provide a general stagehand/foundational certificate before specialization. Its credentials begin after substantial professional development has already occurred elsewhere.

## Continuing development after certification

ETCP certification is valid for five years. Recertification requires professional experience and/or continuing education credits. ETCP explicitly allows cross-training—for example, a rigger may receive renewal credit for relevant electrical coursework and vice versa.

**Source:** ETCP Recertification  
https://etcp.esta.org/recertify/recertify.html

This is useful structurally: even advanced certification is treated as a **continuing professional-development state**, not a permanent endpoint.

# 2. IATSE Local Apprenticeship Structures

## Central finding

IATSE does not appear to impose one universal apprenticeship curriculum across all stagehand locals. Individual locals establish their own systems based on jurisdiction, contracts, crafts, hiring-hall rules, and membership structures.

As a result, apprentice→journeyman progression is **real but highly local-specific**.

The examples below demonstrate several distinct models.

---

## IATSE Local One — New York City

Local One currently operates a formal apprenticeship program lasting approximately **2–3 years**.

The program is explicitly designed to train candidates from the ground up and does not require prior theatre or television experience.

Applicants:

1. register;
2. take an aptitude assessment;
3. are ranked by score;
4. top candidates receive full-time paid placements;
5. complete 2–3 years of apprenticeship;
6. graduate into full Local One membership.

The entrance test focuses on aptitude rather than existing industry knowledge and includes basic mathematics, spatial reasoning, mechanical reasoning, and verbal comprehension.

Apprentices may work in theatres, television studios, lighting and sound shops, scenic fabrication facilities, and other Local One workplaces. The program states that apprentices develop skills across lighting, sound, rigging, carpentry, and emerging technologies.

**Source:** IATSE Local One Apprenticeship Program  
https://www.iatselocaloneapprenticeship.com/

### Curriculum architecture

**Beginner definition:** aptitude and trainability; prior industry knowledge not required.  
**Intermediate definition:** paid apprentice developing practical competence over 2–3 years.  
**Advanced endpoint:** successful completion leading to full membership.

### Gate structure

Yes—there are strong gates:

**competitive entrance assessment → placement → multi-year apprenticeship → completion/full membership.**

### General vs. specialist content

The model is broad stagecraft exposure rather than forcing immediate specialization. Apprentices develop across multiple disciplines while working in a specific assigned environment.

---

## IATSE Local 470 — Northeastern Wisconsin

Local 470 publishes unusually concrete hours-based progression information.

At specified venues, permit workers receive the **Apprentice rate after 1,000 reported hours** of Local 470 work experience and the **Journeyman rate after 2,000 reported hours**.

**Source:** IATSE Local 470 Hours Report  
https://www.ia470.com/education/stringer.htm

Local 470 also maintains a Stagehand Primer/study guide intended to help apprentices study to become Journeymen Stagehands.

**Source:** Local 470 Education  
https://www.ia470.com/education.html

### Curriculum architecture

**Beginner:** permit/new worker.  
**Intermediate:** apprentice-level worker after significant logged work exposure.  
**Advanced:** journeyman rate/status after additional documented hours and local qualification processes.

### Gate structure

This is one of the clearest examples of **experience hours functioning as an advancement gate**.

### Department-specific example: Wardrobe

Local 470's wardrobe pathway demonstrates a combined hours + time + testing model:

- at least 100 hours of wardrobe/hair/makeup work before applying;
- at least 6 months in apprenticeship;
- written test within one year;
- practical test within two years;
- minimum score of 80% on both written and practical exams.

**Source:** IATSE Local 470 Wardrobe Department  
https://www.ia470.com/wardrobe/

This is a strong example of **documented exposure + minimum development period + knowledge exam + practical exam**.

---

## IATSE Local 28 — Portland, Oregon

Local 28 uses a membership progression of **Junior member → Journeyman**.

For Stagehand Craft Junior eligibility, an applicant must have worked at least **320 hours during the previous consecutive 12 months** under Local 28 contracts. Applicants are also evaluated for residence, work hours, and a Stagecraft test.

A worker must successfully complete Junior-member requirements before becoming eligible for Journeyman membership.

**Source:** IATSE Local 28 — Becoming a Member  
https://www.iatse28.org/?page=Becoming20a20member20of20Local2028&zone=%2Funionactive%2Fview_page.cfm

Local 28 also publishes separate study guides for C List, B List, and A List testing, indicating an additional graded qualification/list structure.

**Source:** IATSE Local 28 Useful Links  
https://www.iatse28.org/?page=Useful20Links&zone=%2Funionactive%2Fview_page.cfm

### Curriculum architecture

Local 28 demonstrates that a local may combine:

- work-hour eligibility;
- membership tier progression;
- stagecraft testing;
- graded referral/list qualification.

This is substantially more structured than a simple seniority system.

---

## IATSE Local 38 — Detroit

Local 38 states that its apprenticeship program is designed for qualified applicants seeking to become **journeyperson members**.

The program consists of a series of **basic classes and two master classes**.

**Source:** IATSE Local 38 — How To Apply  
https://www.iatse38.org/mobile/index.cfm?page=How20To20Join&zone=%2Funionactive%2Fview_page.cfm

### Curriculum architecture

This provides a particularly explicit course-tier concept:

**basic classes → master classes → journeyperson pathway.**

The public page does not provide enough detail to reconstruct the exact subject sequence, so this research should not infer one.

---

## IATSE Local 84 — Hartford-area example

Local 84's published work rules describe an **Apprenticeship Classification** for workers accepted into its formal training program.

Workers in this classification receive an entry-level examination assessing new-apprentice skills and are explicitly described as **“in-training”** while working toward full Journeyman classification.

**Source:** IATSE Local 84 Work Rules  
https://www.iatse84.org/?page=Work20Rules&zone=%2Funionactive%2Fview_page.cfm

This is another clear example of apprentice status being recognized as a distinct intermediate qualification above non-certified extra/hand status.

---

## IATSE Local 190 — Wichita example

Local 190's 2024 apprenticeship training program provides an especially useful curriculum-design example.

It identifies:

- **Core 8 classes** containing information useful to every hand regardless of department;
- a **Run Crew Core** allowing customization around individual interests while introducing general running-crew concepts;
- a Stage Employee Apprenticeship Qualification Test;
- the ability for experienced workers to test out rather than repeat material they can already demonstrate.

The program states that its testing criteria were based on the USITT BACKstage exam for high-school technical theatre students.

**Source:** IATSE Local 190 Apprenticeship Training Program  
https://www.iatse190.org/post/iatse-190-apprenticeship-training-program

### Curriculum architecture

This is the strongest U.S. example found of explicitly separating:

**general/core knowledge → customizable run-crew learning → qualification testing.**

It also recognizes prior learning through testing rather than assuming every worker must consume the same sequence.

---

# IATSE synthesis

Across the locals studied, “beginner,” “intermediate,” and “advanced” are defined through combinations of:

- aptitude/entrance testing;
- logged hours;
- time in apprenticeship;
- classroom/core coursework;
- practical work experience;
- written testing;
- practical testing;
- list or referral status;
- eventual journeyman/full-member classification.

The most important finding is **variation**. There is no single IATSE-wide stagehand curriculum ladder that The Crew Blueprint can simply copy.

However, a recurring developmental pattern is visible:

**new/permit worker → apprentice/junior/in-training worker → tested or experienced journeyman/full member.**

# 3. Tech Crew HQ — Australia

## Positioning

Tech Crew HQ is an Australian online training platform for backstage and technical crews. Its public material describes its purpose as providing **starting-level knowledge** for learners and building a foundation for future careers in live events, theatre, and the arts.

It targets high-school age learners and above, as well as schools, churches, TAFE, community theatres, and arts organizations.

**Sources:**  
Tech Crew HQ home  
https://www.techcrewhq.com/  
Tech Crew HQ Teacher Info  
https://www.techcrewhq.com/teacherinfo.html  
Tech Crew HQ About  
https://www.techcrewhq.com/abouttechcrew.html

## Publicly visible course structure

Tech Crew HQ currently advertises:

- Backstage Essentials
- Tech Crew HQ Online (broad all-in-one course)
- Live Event Safety
- Stage Lighting Introduction
- Sound Essentials
- Vision Systems Introduction
- Video for Live Events
- Stage Management
- in-person workshops

Backstage Essentials is described as a **solid foundation** for stage, audio, and lighting crew.

The full Tech Crew HQ Online course covers safety, lighting, audio, vision, stage management, careers, and more.

Individual courses then introduce specific disciplines.

**Source:** Tech Crew HQ home/catalog  
https://www.techcrewhq.com/

## How it defines beginner/intermediate/advanced

Publicly, Tech Crew HQ is strongest at the **beginner/foundational** end.

The platform says it provides a starting-level knowledge base and foundational skills. Individual department courses are described as “Introduction,” “Essentials,” or “basics.”

The public course catalog does **not** document a formal intermediate or advanced certification tier equivalent to apprentice/journeyman or ETCP.

## Does lower-tier completion unlock higher tiers?

No public evidence was found that learners must complete Backstage Essentials or the broad Tech Crew HQ course before accessing Stage Lighting Introduction, Sound Essentials, Live Event Safety, Stage Management, or other modules.

The FAQ indicates courses are purchased individually and learners can begin the course of their choice.

**Source:** Tech Crew HQ FAQ  
https://www.techcrewhq.com/faqs.html

Therefore, the public model appears to be **modular rather than prerequisite-gated**.

## General vs. department-specific content

This is the strongest direct analog to The Crew Blueprint's proposed architecture.

Tech Crew HQ visibly separates:

### General/foundational

- Backstage Essentials
- broad Tech Crew HQ Online course
- Live Event Safety
- careers/general backstage context

### Department-specific introductions

- Lighting
- Sound
- Vision/video
- Stage management

Practical exercises, quizzes, games, and activities are integrated into the online learning.

## Relationship to formal Australian qualifications

Tech Crew HQ explicitly states that its courses **complement**, but are not the same as, Certificate III in Live Production and Services or VET Entertainment Industry coursework. It describes itself as functioning somewhat like an unofficial textbook with access to industry knowledge.

**Source:** Tech Crew HQ FAQ  
https://www.techcrewhq.com/faqs.html

## Curriculum lesson

Tech Crew HQ supports a model of:

**broad backstage foundation + safety + introductory department modules + practical activities**

but it does not publicly demonstrate a complete competency ladder through advanced professional practice.

# 4. ESTA / PLASA Training and Certification

## Important organizational distinction

ESTA and PLASA are related historically within the entertainment-technology standards/training ecosystem, but their current public programs should not be treated as one identical certification system.

In the U.S., **ESTA operates ETCP**, which is addressed above.

PLASA's most clearly tiered skills pathway is currently its **entertainment rigging pathway** in the UK.

---

## PLASA Trainee Rigger Scheme

The Trainee Rigger Scheme is explicitly designed for **new entrants seeking a career in entertainment rigging**.

Trainees receive:

- a Trainee Rigger ID card;
- a logbook listing specific rigging tasks in which they should build experience;
- experience working alongside NRC-qualified riggers who sign off logged experience;
- a pathway toward readiness for the National Rigging Certificate Level 2 assessment.

Applicants must be 18 or over and must complete an approved industry-related health-and-safety course before applying.

**Source:** PLASA Trainee Rigger Scheme  
https://www.plasa.org/qualification/trainee-rigger-scheme/

## Critical progression feature

The Trainee Rigger Scheme itself is **optional** and is not a mandatory prerequisite for the National Rigging Certificate.

This is important: PLASA provides a recommended developmental pathway without making the lower tier the only gate into advanced assessment.

Experienced workers can therefore demonstrate readiness through their existing professional background rather than being forced through beginner material.

## National Rigging Certificate (NRC)

PLASA describes the NRC as the industry-standard certification for rigging professionals and a benchmark for employers and venues.

Candidates must document experience through employment records/witness testimony before assessment. Successful candidates receive an ID skills card and certificate, with continuing evidence of safe professional practice required for renewal.

**Source:** PLASA Rigging & Production  
https://www.plasa.org/rigging-production-training-certification/

## National Event Lifting Training (NELT)

PLASA also offers National Event Lifting Training, a three-day program for production technicians who select and use lifting equipment commonly found in events and entertainment.

The course is aimed at technicians who conduct lifting operations from the ground and emphasizes role/responsibility awareness and working appropriately alongside qualified riggers.

**Sources:**  
PLASA Skills  
https://www.plasa.org/skills-rigging-production/  
PLASA Rigging & Production  
https://www.plasa.org/rigging-production-training-certification/

## PLASA progression model

The clearest conceptual sequence is:

**basic safety prerequisite → trainee status/logbook → supervised logged experience → NRC professional assessment**

with NELT operating as a role-specific technical course for production technicians involved in event lifting.

## General vs. department-specific structure

PLASA's most formalized progression is **department-specific**, especially rigging and lifting.

Its broader professional-development offerings cover topics such as business skills, HR, health and safety, and related professional skills, but there is no publicly visible unified “Stagehand Level 1 → Level 2 → Level 3” system across all entertainment departments.

**Source:** PLASA Skills  
https://www.plasa.org/skills-rigging-production/

# 5. ESTA-recognized training ecosystem

Beyond ETCP certification itself, ESTA/ETCP recognizes external training programs and providers that align with ETCP content outlines.

Recognized courses can count toward renewal education credits. ETCP requires recognized training-provider applications to identify:

- intended audience;
- program objectives;
- schedule;
- course outline;
- instructors;
- relationship to ETCP content domains.

**Source:** ETCP Training and Recognition  
https://etcp.esta.org/training/training.html

An example is the Academy of Production Technology's entertainment electrical training. Its course content begins with foundational electrical topics and extends into advanced entertainment power/control systems, and the program is recognized for ETCP renewal credit.

**Source:** ETCP Recognized Electrical Training — Academy of Production Technology  
https://etcp.esta.org/training/bios/electrical_academyproductiontechnology.html

This demonstrates another useful structural idea: **one course can deliberately accommodate both newer learners and experienced professionals**, while the professional certification remains separate.

# What these systems imply for The Crew Blueprint

## 1. Do not confuse training with certification

The strongest industry systems separate these concepts:

- **Training** develops competence.
- **Logged work experience** demonstrates exposure and application.
- **Assessment** verifies knowledge/skill.
- **Certification** recognizes professional competence.

ETCP is the clearest example: certification happens after experience; it is not the learning pathway itself.

## 2. Use foundational content before specialization

Tech Crew HQ and Local 190 both support the value of a shared core before workers branch into specialist interests.

A Crew Blueprint architecture could therefore use:

**Foundation Core**

- stagehand/worksite fundamentals;
- safety;
- communication;
- venue awareness;
- equipment/material interaction;
- basic department awareness;

followed by:

**Department Pathways**

- audio;
- lighting/electrics;
- video;
- rigging;
- staging/carpentry;
- stage management;
- later public-safety/event-operations paths where appropriate.

## 3. Progress by demonstrated capability, not course consumption alone

IATSE and PLASA examples repeatedly use real-world experience, logged hours, supervisor sign-off, or practical testing.

A credible training architecture should therefore avoid implying that watching online lessons alone makes someone an independent technician.

The learning system can distinguish:

- **knowledge complete**;
- **practice required**;
- **supervised competency demonstrated**;
- **independent-role ready**;
- **lead-level ready**;
- **external certification eligible**, where applicable.

## 4. Allow experienced workers to bypass redundant beginner content

Local 190's test-out model and PLASA's optional Trainee Rigger Scheme both support recognition of prior learning.

The Crew Blueprint should eventually consider assessment-based placement so an experienced worker does not have to complete every beginner module merely to reach advanced content.

## 5. Advanced tracks should branch, not form one universal ladder

ETCP demonstrates that advanced competence is discipline-specific. Arena and Theatre Rigger are parallel scopes; electrical and portable power are separate certifications.

Therefore a worker should not be forced through a single sequence such as:

**general hand → audio → lighting → rigging → leadership.**

A more defensible structure is:

**Foundation Core → choose pathway → supervised competency → independent technician → lead/advanced specialization → external professional credential where relevant.**

## 6. Experience thresholds can be meaningful without pretending they are universal

Local 470's 1,000/2,000-hour thresholds are useful examples of experience gates, but they are local-specific and should not be copied as universal U.S. standards.

The Crew Blueprint can use experience bands as guidance while clearly labeling them as **developmental estimates**, unless a specific employer, union, or certifying body defines an actual threshold.

# Recommended Crew Blueprint curriculum model derived from the evidence

## Layer 0 — Orientation / Entry Readiness

Purpose: prepare a person who may have little or no professional experience.

Topics:

- industry structure;
- worksite expectations;
- basic safety;
- communication;
- PPE;
- venue awareness;
- material/public-area interaction;
- load-in/show/load-out context.

Comparable evidence: Tech Crew HQ foundational courses; IATSE new-worker/core material.

## Layer 1 — General Stagecraft Foundation

Purpose: build broad competence useful across departments.

Topics:

- common equipment recognition;
- cable/material handling;
- basic staging and department interfaces;
- worksite hazard recognition;
- crew structure;
- basic running-crew awareness.

Comparable evidence: Local 190 Core 8; Local One broad apprenticeship exposure.

## Layer 2 — Department Introduction

Purpose: provide awareness plus supervised-entry knowledge in a chosen pathway.

Examples:

- Audio Fundamentals
- Lighting/Electrics Fundamentals
- Video Fundamentals
- Rigging Awareness / Ground Support
- Staging/Carpentry Fundamentals
- Stage Management Fundamentals

Comparable evidence: Tech Crew HQ department introductions.

## Layer 3 — Supervised Practical Competency

Purpose: move from “knows concepts” to “can perform defined tasks under supervision.”

Evidence mechanisms could include:

- employer/mentor sign-off;
- practical checklists;
- logged work experience;
- scenario assessments;
- supervised demonstrations.

Comparable evidence: PLASA trainee logbook; IATSE apprenticeship/OJT models.

## Layer 4 — Independent Technician / Journeyman-Equivalent Competency

Purpose: identify the point where a worker can independently own a defined operational task or system.

Assessment should emphasize:

- practical capability;
- troubleshooting;
- safety judgment;
- repeatability;
- communication;
- ability to work without continuous instruction.

Comparable evidence: IATSE journeyman classifications; PLASA NRC readiness.

## Layer 5 — Lead / Advanced Specialist

Purpose: develop leadership, advanced systems knowledge, planning, troubleshooting, and mentoring.

This level should branch heavily by department.

## Layer 6 — External Professional Certification

Where legitimate external certifications exist, The Crew Blueprint should point outward rather than pretending to replace them.

Examples:

- ETCP Arena Rigger
- ETCP Theatre Rigger
- ETCP Entertainment Electrician
- ETCP Portable Power Distribution Technician

The Crew Blueprint can prepare learners conceptually for those destinations while clearly stating that only the external certifying organization awards the credential.

---

# Bottom line

1. **ETCP does not provide a beginner-to-advanced curriculum ladder.** It certifies experienced practitioners through experience-based eligibility and high-level discipline exams.
2. **IATSE apprenticeships are the strongest U.S. evidence for true developmental progression**, but structures vary dramatically by local.
3. **Tech Crew HQ is the closest direct online-learning analog**: broad foundation plus introductory department modules, but no publicly documented formal advanced tier ladder.
4. **PLASA's rigging pathway is the clearest staged professional model**: safety prerequisite → trainee/logbook → supervised experience → professional assessment.
5. The recurring industry pattern is **foundation → supervised practice → documented experience → assessment → independent/advanced status**, not “watch course 1, unlock course 2, become certified.”
6. The Crew Blueprint should therefore separate **knowledge progression, practical competency progression, career progression, and external certification eligibility** as four related but distinct dimensions.
