**Research date:** 2026-08-17  
**Queue item:** CA-003  
**Purpose:** Re-research the correct second-course progression for backline, props, wardrobe, and related production-support work after basic ground-hand awareness.  
**Decision to resolve:** **What is the correct Course 2 mental model for ownership and accountability when handling backline, props, wardrobe, and show-critical production items?**

> **Status note:** This is a fresh CA-003 research pass created after the research queue was deliberately restarted. It does not overwrite the earlier ground-hand or Course 2 research. Treat this package as the newer evidence review for the restarted queue.

---

## Executive conclusion

The most defensible Course 2 mental model is:

> **Identify whose item it is and what state it is supposed to be in → know exactly what action you are authorized to perform → preserve placement, condition, configuration, and documentation → confirm handoff or return → stop and report any mismatch rather than improvising.**

The core progression beyond Course 1 is not “touch more expensive gear.” It is learning **controlled custody**.

A Course 2 learner should understand that show-critical objects exist inside an accountability system. The same physical action—moving a case, stand, garment, prop, instrument, pedal, accessory, or scenic dressing—can be routine, prohibited, or highly consequential depending on ownership, preset state, performer dependence, rental status, department instructions, and timing.

The curriculum should therefore teach a hierarchy of permission:

**identify → verify authority → move/set/check only as assigned → preserve state → document/report → hand off**

It should not imply that a junior worker may independently tune, repair, alter, patch, program, substitute, fit, launder, redesign, or technically configure an item merely because they are trusted to carry, preset, inventory, or return it.

# 1. Evidence base and role structure

Current IATSE touring yellow-card data provides strong evidence that Props and Wardrobe remain distinct departments with traveling heads and local take-in, performance, and put-out crews. Current touring examples such as *The Notebook*, *Stereophonic*, *The Outsiders*, *Suffs*, *Moulin Rouge*, and others separately list Head Prop-Person and Head Wardrobe positions and local crew counts. This supports a curriculum model in which a local/junior worker can perform assigned labor inside a department without inheriting the department head's authority.

# 2. The central Course 2 concept: controlled custody

“Chain of custody” is not a single standardized entertainment-industry term with one universal procedure across backline, props, and wardrobe. Crew Blueprint should therefore present it as a **curriculum model for accountability**, not falsely claim one industry-wide formal protocol.

The underlying practices are well supported:

- props are inventoried, tracked, mapped, preset, used, reset, and returned;
- costumes are checked in/out, tracked, prepared, maintained, and returned;
- rentals are transferred to a responsible representative and remain accountable until pickup/return;
- show-critical equipment is prepared and maintained as a controlled system.

PRG's current rental terms require a customer representative to be present to sign for delivered equipment and to remain with equipment at show close until pickup. The customer is responsible for repair costs for damage during the rental period. This is direct evidence that custody, handoff, condition, and return matter operationally.

Source: PRG — Terms & Conditions: https://orders.prg.com/main/terms

Theatre programs likewise formalize tracking. UAF defines **tracking** as monitoring the location, use, and movement of costumes or props throughout rehearsal or performance. Howard University's theatre handbook describes prop plots, marked locations, assigned placement responsibility, pre-setting, collection after use, return of borrowed property, and retention of source/contact records for loaned property.

Sources:
- University of Alaska Fairbanks — Stage Manager's Handbook: https://www.uaf.edu/theatrefilm/stage-managers-handbook.php
- Howard University — Theatre student handbook: https://finearts.howard.edu/sites/finearts.howard.edu/files/2023-02/Student-Handbook_2014-2015-FINAL.pdf

### Crew Blueprint teaching model

Treat custody as five questions:

1. **Identity:** What exactly is this item?
2. **Ownership:** Who owns or controls it—artist, rental house, department, venue, production, or another party?
3. **State:** What condition/configuration/location is expected right now?
4. **Authority:** What am I actually assigned to do with it?
5. **Handoff:** Who receives responsibility next, and has the handoff/return been confirmed?

This model is a curriculum synthesis from the documented tracking, preset, rental, and departmental practices above.

# 3. Move, set, check, modify, and operate are different permissions

Course 2 should explicitly separate these verbs.

| Action | Beginner meaning | Course 2 boundary |
|---|---|---|
| **Move** | Transport an item from one approved place to another | Does not imply permission to alter setup or contents |
| **Set / preset** | Put the specified item in the specified location/orientation/state | Follow the established plot, mark, list, or department instruction |
| **Check** | Verify identity, location, visible condition, completeness, or expected state | Report discrepancy; do not automatically correct technical/configuration problems |
| **Modify** | Change construction, adjustment, fit, electronics, programming, tuning, repair, contents, or intended configuration | Specialist/authorized work unless specifically trained and assigned |
| **Operate** | Use the item for its performance/technical function | Separate authorization; custody or setup permission does not grant operating authority |

This distinction is essential because institutional role descriptions assign modifications, repairs, design decisions, and maintenance to designated prop, wardrobe, technical, or supervisory roles even while run crews perform presets and tracking.

Sources:
- Ithaca College technician guidelines
- SUNY New Paltz Production Handbook
- University of Montana Theatre & Dance handbook
- East Texas Baptist University Theatre Student Handbook

# 4. What “preset” should mean

UAF's current Stage Manager's Handbook defines **preset** as the arrangement of props, set pieces, and equipment before a rehearsal or performance begins. It defines tracking as monitoring location, use, and movement throughout rehearsal/performance.

Source: https://www.uaf.edu/theatrefilm/stage-managers-handbook.php

For Course 2, expand that definition operationally:

> A preset is a known starting state that must be reproducible.

A preset can include:
- exact item identity;
- exact location or marked position;
- orientation;
- sequence/order;
- required accessories or paired components;
- visible condition;
- readiness state;
- timing—when it must be ready;
- responsibility—who checks or releases it.

KU Theatre's Deck/Props description supports this operational model: preshow duties include presetting props/set and preparing assigned items; during the show, crew track or move items as assigned; postshow duties include cleaning/reset and communicating notes to stage management.

Source: University of Kansas Theatre — Practicum Job Descriptions: https://kutheatre.ku.edu/practicum-job-descriptions

Howard University's handbook adds prop plots, taped locations, individual placement responsibility, collection after use, and reporting readiness to stage management.

**Curriculum implication:** “It is basically in the right area” is not the same as “preset.”

# 5. Backline: assistant-level handling without artist-tech authority

Backline commonly includes instruments, amplifiers, keyboards, stands, pedals, drum hardware, cases, power supplies, accessories, and related performer systems. The key Course 2 lesson is that these often function as **matched systems**, not interchangeable loose objects.

Professional backline guidance emphasizes equipment readiness, inspection between rentals, fault tagging/isolation, maintenance of pedals/stands/moving parts, amplifier/cabinet compatibility checks, and keeping keyboard power supplies and pedals as controlled systems. This supports teaching junior workers that cases, accessories, power supplies, pedals, stands, and associated components may have a specific relationship and should not be casually swapped.

Source: The Backline Company — How to Choose a Backline Rental Partner: https://www.thebacklinecompany.com/the-handbook/how-to-choose-a-backline-rental-partner

### Appropriate Course 2 abilities

A learner may reasonably learn to:
- identify common backline categories;
- read labels, case IDs, rental tags, and stage-placement instructions;
- move and stage equipment using approved handling points and cases;
- keep matched accessories with their assigned system;
- confirm that expected components appear present;
- recognize obvious external damage, missing components, wrong cases, or mismatches;
- place stands, cases, amps, keyboards, hardware, and accessories where directed;
- preserve knobs, switches, pedal positions, cable associations, or other settings when instructed not to disturb them;
- report faults or changes immediately.

### Keep outside Course 2 authority

Unless specifically trained, assigned, and supervised for the production, Course 2 should not imply authority to:
- tune or intonate instruments;
- change performer-specific instrument setup;
- alter pedalboards or effects programming;
- repair electronics or instruments;
- change amplifier/keyboard programming or saved configurations;
- substitute power supplies, pedals, stands, cables, hardware, or instruments because they “look equivalent”;
- independently patch or repatch performer systems;
- decide that a fault has been repaired and return an item to service.

The correct response to a discrepancy is **preserve evidence/state, identify the item, report the problem, and wait for authorized direction**.

# 6. Props: identity, location, sequence, and reset

Props practice provides some of the strongest direct evidence for the Course 2 accountability model.

Ithaca College requires the props function to organize backstage prop spaces/tables, create tracking lists for prop movement, prepare all show props, run them as necessary, and put them away. University of Kansas assigns Deck/Props crews to preset, track/move assigned props during the show, reset afterward, and report notes. SUNY Oswego describes running crews tracking props at all times, developing a props map, setting preshow positions, and ensuring props are ready on cue.

Sources:
- Ithaca College — Designer and Technician Guidelines
- University of Kansas Theatre — Practicum Job Descriptions
- SUNY Oswego — Theatre Student Handbook: https://www.oswego.edu/theatre/node/806

### Course 2 learner responsibilities under direction

Teach learners to:
- match item to prop list/plot/label;
- place it at its assigned location;
- keep tables and storage orderly;
- verify expected items before a cue or show;
- track movement through the performance when assigned;
- return/reset after use;
- report damage, contamination, loss, or unauthorized movement;
- preserve borrowed/rented items for return.

### Boundary

A junior worker should not infer that reset authority includes design authority. University of Montana explicitly states that prop decisions remain under scenic/design/directorial supervision and that final appearance decisions are not independently controlled by the Props Master. This makes the boundary even clearer for an assistant/run-crew learner.

Source: University of Montana Theatre & Dance handbook: https://www.umt.edu/theatre-dance/links/20-21-handbook.pdf

Course 2 should therefore keep independent fabrication, aesthetic substitution, structural alteration, repair strategy, or redesign outside scope unless specifically assigned by the responsible department.

# 7. Wardrobe: tracking and care are not the same as alteration authority

Wardrobe evidence strongly supports structured check-in/out and supervisory control.

SUNY New Paltz requires wardrobe paperwork including dressing lists and costume check-in/out; supervisors oversee beginning/end-of-performance costume checks, quick-change rehearsals, maintenance, cleaning, repair, and crew training. Its wardrobe day-crew and run-crew descriptions show that laundering, pressing, steaming, repairs, dressing assistance, and quick changes are trained/assigned functions rather than casual help.

Source: https://www.newpaltz.edu/fpa/theatre/productions/production-handbook/

University of Michigan describes wardrobe supervision as organizing paperwork and training the run crew; costume construction/alteration is handled by a specialized costume-shop team. It also notes that quick-change costumes may be constructed differently from ordinary garments.

Source: https://smtd.umich.edu/the-costume-shop-the-uprod-theatrical-shops-at-work/

### Course 2 awareness/assistant level

A learner can reasonably understand:
- garment/costume identity and assigned performer/character;
- rack, hanger, bag, shoe, accessory, and dressing-location organization;
- check-in/check-out and missing-item awareness;
- keeping costume pieces together and protected;
- preset and return discipline;
- communicating visible damage or contamination;
- maintaining privacy and keeping backstage/dressing areas orderly;
- that quick changes are rehearsed workflows, not improvised assistance.

### Keep specialist work bounded

Do not imply independent authority for:
- fittings;
- alterations;
- repair decisions;
- laundering, dry-cleaning, steaming, pressing, or chemical/heat treatment without department instruction/training;
- deciding whether a garment is safe/ready after damage;
- performer dressing or quick-change assistance without assignment and rehearsal;
- hair/makeup work;
- changing costume design or substituting items.

Barnard's wardrobe-crew description demonstrates that dressing, upkeep, repair, cleaning, and quick changes can be legitimate crew duties—but explicitly within an assigned run-crew structure under supervisors. Course 2 should teach awareness of these workflows without treating them as universal permissions.

Source: Barnard College — Wardrobe Crew: https://barnard.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/wardrobe_crew_0.pdf

# 8. Ownership changes handling expectations

Course 2 should distinguish at least five ownership/control categories.

## Performer-owned / personal

Treat as **highest-permission sensitivity**. Do not assume production access equals personal-property access. Move or handle only when specifically assigned. Preserve exact condition/configuration. Do not explore contents of personal cases/bags or use personal equipment.

This is primarily a Crew Blueprint professional-conduct recommendation derived from the broader custody and departmental-control evidence; there is no single universal live-event rule that covers every artist/tour.

## Rented

Treat as **documented temporary custody**. Keep identifying labels/cases/accessories together, preserve return condition, report damage/loss, and follow designated handoff/return procedures. PRG's terms directly demonstrate customer responsibility for damage and signed delivery/pickup custody.

## Department-owned

Follow department instructions and internal organization. Department ownership does not make the item general-use gear.

## Show-critical production item

Treat the expected state as part of the show. Do not change placement, configuration, sequence, or contents because another arrangement appears more convenient.

## General production item

May have broader handling permission, but assignment and safety still control. “General” does not mean “free to modify.”

# 9. Labels, inventory, cases, and documentation

Course 2 should teach documentation as an operational control rather than clerical busywork.

Useful beginner practices:
- read before moving;
- match item ID to case/location/list;
- do not peel, cover, rewrite, or improvise labels without direction;
- keep accessories with the system they belong to;
- distinguish empty case from missing item;
- note visible damage before/at handoff when the workflow calls for it;
- report mismatches immediately;
- use the production's actual inventory/checklist system rather than inventing a parallel one;
- confirm return/handoff when required.

Theatre props documentation supports plots, tracking lists, assigned placement, readiness reports, and return records. Rental practice supports signed transfer and damage accountability.

---

# 10. Work-state vocabulary

These terms are not universally standardized across every tour or employer. Crew Blueprint should teach them as **conceptual states** and tell learners to confirm local meaning.

- **Do not touch:** no handling authority.
- **Move only:** transport without changing configuration/content/settings.
- **Set / preset:** place into an established starting state.
- **Ready:** expected preparation has been completed according to the responsible department's process.
- **Checked:** a designated check has occurred; who is authorized to declare this varies.
- **In use / live:** preserve state and do not disturb unless assigned.
- **Strike / return:** remove from performance position and move into the approved storage/case/return workflow.
- **Hold / quarantine / fault:** do not return to service until the responsible department resolves the issue.

The curriculum must avoid implying that saying “checked” or “ready” makes a junior worker the final technical authority.

# 11. Troubleshooting = discrepancy control, not improvisation

For these departments, beginner troubleshooting should be taught as **state reconciliation**:

1. **Stop unnecessary movement.**
2. **Identify the item.** Label, case, list, performer, location, department.
3. **Compare expected vs. actual.** Missing? wrong location? wrong case? incomplete? visibly damaged? altered? settings changed?
4. **Preserve the current state.** Do not erase evidence by randomly changing things.
5. **Notify the responsible lead/department.** Give concise facts.
6. **Act only on authorized correction.**
7. **Reconfirm the state/handoff.**

Examples:

| Discrepancy | Course 2 response |
|---|---|
| Missing item | Check assigned list/location, report; do not silently substitute |
| Wrong case | Stop, verify labels/IDs, report mismatch |
| Visible damage | Isolate from unnecessary handling and report |
| Incomplete preset | Identify missing/incorrect element and escalate before declaring ready |
| Settings/configuration changed | Do not “put it back” from memory unless specifically authorized |
| Unknown ownership | Treat as restricted until ownership/department is confirmed |
| Rental item separated from accessories | Keep components controlled, identify mismatch, report |
| Wardrobe item damaged/soiled | Protect item and report; do not choose a cleaning/repair method independently |

# 12. Load-in, show call, changeovers, and load-out

Accountability changes with phase.

## Load-in

Priority is **identity + destination + system integrity**. Items are entering custody and being distributed. Wrong cases, missing accessories, or undocumented damage should be caught early.

## Show call

Priority becomes **preset + timing + state preservation**. A small movement or configuration change can affect a cue or performer.

## Changeover

Priority is **fast but controlled transition**. Speed does not erase ownership or preset rules. One item's strike state may be another item's setup state. Explicit handoffs matter more when multiple crews/artists overlap.

## Load-out

Priority becomes **reconciliation + correct case/owner + return condition**. Do not treat the end of the show as permission to throw similar-looking items into whichever case is nearby.

# 13. Backstage privacy and restricted information

Course 2 should include a professional-conduct boundary around backstage information and personal spaces.

Learners should understand:
- credentials grant only the access required for work;
- dressing rooms and personal artist areas deserve heightened privacy;
- personal property should not be searched, photographed, used, or discussed casually;
- schedules, set lists, technical documents, credentials, access details, and production information should be used for work purposes and shared only as required by the production;
- backstage access is not permission to create or publish content;
- if unsure whether information/property is restricted, ask the lead rather than assume.

Specific confidentiality rules vary significantly by artist, employer, venue, tour, contract, and production. Crew Blueprint should therefore teach the **default professional principle**—minimum necessary access and disclosure—while directing learners to follow local policy.

# 14. Safety and material handling

OSHA's general materials-handling rules require aisles and passageways to remain clear and storage to be stable and secure against sliding/collapse. OSHA warehouse guidance similarly emphasizes stable storage, safe racking, clear areas, and controlling material-handling hazards.

Sources:
- OSHA 29 CFR 1910.176: https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.176
- OSHA 29 CFR 1926.250: https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.250
- OSHA Warehousing — Hazards and Solutions: https://www.osha.gov/warehousing/hazards-solutions

Curriculum application:
- expensive/show-critical status never justifies unsafe lifting;
- cases/racks/garment equipment must not block egress or travel paths;
- awkward/heavy items require appropriate assistance/equipment;
- stacked/stored items must remain stable;
- do not rush a changeover by creating a trip, crush, or unstable-storage hazard.

# 15. Recommended Course 2 learning architecture

## Module 1 — Ownership, Permission & Custody

Learner outcomes:
- classify ownership/control category;
- distinguish move/set/check/modify/operate;
- understand controlled custody and handoff;
- interpret labels, lists, cases, and basic inventory documentation;
- apply heightened care to artist-owned and rental property.

### Retrieval scenarios

Use short decisions such as:
- “You were told to move the keyboard case. May you open it and set the keyboard?”
- “A pedal is in the wrong case. Do you swap it based on appearance or stop and verify?”
- “A rental item arrives with visible damage. What should happen before it disappears into the build?”

Correct reasoning should emphasize **scope of permission and preservation of state**.

## Module 2 — Presets, Tracking & Show-Critical State

Learner outcomes:
- define preset and tracking;
- follow a plot/list/mark;
- understand exact location/orientation/order/timing;
- perform assigned checks without claiming final authority;
- reset/return items after use;
- understand show-call and changeover accountability.

Use visual prop-table, backline-stage, and wardrobe-rack exercises where learners identify discrepancies against a reference state.

## Module 3 — Discrepancies, Damage & Escalation

Learner outcomes:
- detect missing, damaged, wrong-location, wrong-case, incomplete, or altered conditions;
- preserve state;
- report concise facts;
- avoid unauthorized repair/substitution;
- complete controlled handoff/return;
- apply privacy/confidentiality boundaries.

Use branching scenarios rather than “how to repair” tasks.

# 16. Assessment boundary

Course completion may demonstrate that the learner can:
- identify ownership/accountability risk;
- interpret basic preset/tracking documentation;
- distinguish levels of permission;
- recognize discrepancies;
- select safe escalation behavior;
- preserve custody and return discipline in simulated scenarios.

It must **not** claim that the learner is qualified as:
- backline technician or artist tech;
- instrument technician;
- props master/lead;
- prop fabricator/repair specialist;
- wardrobe supervisor;
- dresser/quick-change specialist without production-specific training;
- costume technician, stitcher, tailor, laundry specialist, hair/makeup worker;
- department head or final checker/acceptance authority.

Recommended completion language:

> **This course teaches awareness and supervised production-support practices for ownership, presets, tracking, handling, and discrepancy reporting. It does not replace employer, department, artist, rental-provider, or production-specific training and does not authorize specialist technical, repair, alteration, performer-support, or supervisory work.**

---

# 17. What varies by production

Do not universalize:
- who owns backline and who is allowed to touch it;
- whether local hands may unpack/set instruments;
- who performs backline patching or tuning;
- prop-table layouts and check procedures;
- terminology for ready/checked/clear/hold states;
- wardrobe check-in/out paperwork;
- who may assist performers with dressing;
- cleaning/laundry practices;
- rental documentation systems;
- whether a changeover uses dedicated artist techs, local hands, touring crew, or mixed teams;
- privacy/confidentiality rules beyond the general professional baseline.

Teach learners to ask: **“What does this production's responsible department require?”**

# 18. Final answer to the research decision

## What is the correct Course 2 mental model for ownership and accountability?

> **Every show-critical item has an identity, an owner/controller, an expected state, an allowed action, and a next handoff. Your job is to know all five before you change anything.**

The learner should progress from Course 1's “handle carefully and ask before touching sensitive items” to a more operational model:

**IDENTIFY → VERIFY OWNERSHIP / DEPARTMENT → CONFIRM PERMISSION → PRESERVE EXPECTED STATE → MOVE / PRESET / CHECK AS ASSIGNED → REPORT DISCREPANCIES → CONFIRM HANDOFF / RETURN**

The single most important boundary is:

> **Permission to handle an item is not permission to modify, configure, repair, operate, or make specialist decisions about it.**

This mental model transfers across backline, props, wardrobe, rentals, artist property, and other show-critical production assets without pretending those departments are identical.

## Evidence-quality note

The strongest direct evidence in this package concerns theatrical props/wardrobe role separation, preset/tracking practice, rental custody, and general materials handling. Backline practice is less standardized in publicly available primary documentation, so the curriculum recommendations intentionally stay conservative: identify, protect, preserve configuration, report discrepancies, and defer technical/artist-specific changes to authorized personnel.
