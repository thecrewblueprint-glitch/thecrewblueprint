# 31 — Predictive Hazard Recognition: Supervisor Version

**Research Queue ID:** CB-008  
**Status:** Complete research packet  
**Research date:** 2026-08-17  
**Depends on:** CB-001 through CB-007  
**Purpose:** Define the supervisor-level hazard-recognition model that should sit above department-specific technical leadership without presenting Crew Blueprint as a substitute for OSHA-required employer training, competent-person designation, or professional safety credentials.

---

## Decision question

**What changes when hazard recognition moves from worker awareness to supervisor responsibility?**

## Bottom-line finding

OSHA’s current Recommended Practices support a clear progression:

**worker:** recognize hazards encountered in assigned work and report/respond appropriately;  
**supervisor:** proactively identify hazards before and during work, understand controls, respond correctly to worker reports, investigate incidents/near misses, and verify that controls remain effective.

OSHA explicitly states that employers, managers, and supervisors are responsible for worker safety and may need specific training in hazard recognition, hierarchy of controls, incident response, and root-cause investigation.

Sources:

https://www.osha.gov/safety-management/education-training  
https://www.osha.gov/safety-management/hazard-identification

The appropriate Crew Blueprint teaching model is therefore:

> **Predictive hazard recognition means looking forward through the work sequence and asking what new hazard will exist after the next action, change, interface, or environmental shift—not merely spotting what is already visibly wrong.**

---

# 1. Supervisor-level difference

A worker can be competent at noticing immediate hazards and still not be ready to supervise.

The supervisor must additionally consider:

- the upcoming work sequence;
- simultaneous department activity;
- changing site conditions;
- staffing and fatigue;
- equipment movement;
- temporary/incomplete systems;
- handoffs between crews;
- nonroutine tasks;
- weather/environmental change;
- emergency scenarios;
- whether existing controls will remain effective after the job changes.

OSHA describes hazard identification as a proactive, ongoing process and specifically advises evaluating hazards before changes to operations, workflows, equipment, materials, or organization.

Source: OSHA — Hazard Identification and Assessment  
https://www.osha.gov/safety-management/hazard-identification

---

# 2. Recommended prediction model

Crew Blueprint should use a simple supervisor reasoning loop:

## Step 1 — Define the next work state

Ask:

- What is about to move, change, open, close, energize, become elevated, become occupied, or become accessible?
- Which crews will enter or leave the area?
- What temporary condition will exist during the transition?

## Step 2 — Identify exposure

Ask:

- Who could be affected?
- Is the exposure limited to the assigned crew, or does it affect adjacent departments, venue staff, contractors, performers, or the public?

## Step 3 — Characterize consequence and likelihood

OSHA recommends considering severity and likelihood when prioritizing corrective action.

The purpose here is not to produce a pseudo-engineering risk score. It is to help supervisors distinguish trivial inconvenience from conditions capable of serious harm.

## Step 4 — Select/confirm controls

Use the hierarchy-of-controls concept and existing approved procedures. The supervisor should confirm that required controls actually exist and are usable before the work proceeds.

## Step 5 — Reassess after change

A control that worked ten minutes ago may be inadequate after equipment moves, weather changes, staffing changes, or another department starts work.

This is the core predictive habit.

---

# 3. OSHA support for proactive supervision

OSHA’s Recommended Practices state that effective hazard identification includes:

- collecting existing hazard information;
- regular workplace inspections;
- incident and near-miss investigation;
- identifying hazards in emergencies and nonroutine work;
- evaluating severity and likelihood;
- prioritizing corrective action.

OSHA also recommends follow-up to confirm that controls are installed, used correctly, and remain effective.

Sources:

https://www.osha.gov/safety-management/hazard-identification  
https://www.osha.gov/safety-management/hazard-prevention

This supports a supervisor course that emphasizes **anticipation + control verification + reassessment**, rather than memorized hazard lists.

---

# 4. Live-event application domains

The supervisor version should repeatedly practice cross-department situations such as:

- load-in traffic intersecting with pedestrian work;
- incomplete staging or open edges during build transitions;
- simultaneous overhead and floor work;
- cable paths changing as departments come online;
- weather changes at outdoor events;
- low visibility or fatigue during load-out;
- temporary removal of barriers or access controls;
- changes to crew size or worker capability;
- new equipment or nonroutine procedures;
- emergency egress becoming constrained by work activity;
- production changes that create new technical dependencies.

The course should focus on recognizing and escalating these conditions, not on providing technical instructions for hazardous specialist work.

---

# 5. Near misses and weak signals

OSHA specifically recommends investigating close calls/near misses because they reveal hazards and program shortcomings before a more serious event occurs.

Supervisor training should therefore teach:

- treat near misses as data;
- avoid blaming the nearest worker as the end of the analysis;
- ask what system condition allowed the event;
- check whether tools, time, staffing, training, supervision, procedures, and work organization contributed;
- communicate lessons back into future planning.

Source: OSHA — Hazard Identification and Assessment  
https://www.osha.gov/safety-management/hazard-identification

# 6. Recommended CB-008 curriculum architecture

## Module A — From hazard spotting to hazard forecasting

- visible versus emerging hazards;
- work-state transitions;
- nonroutine work;
- simultaneous operations.

## Module B — Exposure and risk prioritization

- who can be affected;
- severity/likelihood reasoning;
- worst-first prioritization without ignoring recognized serious hazards;
- escalation boundaries.

## Module C — Control verification

- hierarchy of controls;
- confirm controls exist before work;
- verify workers understand controls;
- follow up after implementation;
- recognize degraded/defeated controls.

## Module D — Change management

- schedule changes;
- staffing changes;
- equipment/process changes;
- environmental/weather changes;
- cross-department changes;
- re-brief/reassess triggers.

## Module E — Near misses, incidents, and learning loops

- objective reporting;
- root-cause thinking;
- corrective action;
- communicate lessons;
- prevent recurrence.

---

# 7. Assessment model

Use short evolving scenarios where the work state changes over time.

A high-quality answer should identify:

1. the new hazard created by the change;
2. who may be exposed;
3. whether the existing control remains adequate;
4. whether work pauses or continues;
5. who must be informed;
6. what should be verified before resuming.

This tests prediction rather than recall.

---

# 8. Hard boundaries

Completion must not be represented as:

- OSHA certification;
- employer safety-program compliance;
- competent-person designation;
- qualified-person status;
- professional safety certification;
- authority to supervise technical work outside the learner’s actual craft competence.

Supervisor safety training must sit **on top of**, not replace, underlying department competence and site/employer-specific training.

## Curriculum decision

**Supervisor hazard training should teach anticipation of the next work state, not merely recognition of the current one.**

The learner should leave able to reason: **what changes next, who becomes exposed, which control might fail, and what verification is required before the work proceeds?**
