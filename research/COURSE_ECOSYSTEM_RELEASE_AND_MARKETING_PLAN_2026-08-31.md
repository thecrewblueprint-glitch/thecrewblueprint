## Operating model

The real domain is production. Course development, evidence reconciliation, owner review, validator hardening, and release assembly happen in the repository first. A release moves to production only after its bounded content and learner routes pass the relevant checks.

Do not use the production domain as a scratchpad. Do not use an unfinished research branch as a marketing destination.

## Build rule

Build every course whose evidence supports a bounded learner claim. When evidence does not support a practical or authority claim, keep the course moving at the awareness, recognition, support, or systems layer and record the exact gate. Do not manufacture operational authority to make the catalog look complete.

Intentional external gates remain external, including generic physical rigging authorization, live-power practical authorization, structural acceptance, powered-equipment operation, manufacturer-specific service work, and other specialist tasks that require employer, manufacturer, venue, qualified-person, or in-person control.

## Release gates

Every staged release should pass, as applicable:

1. source lineage and support-edge check;
2. dangerous-authority boundary review;
3. five-question assessment review for generated tiered courses;
4. course-consent coverage;
5. noindex owner-review state before promotion;
6. internal-link validation;
7. mobile layout and navigation check;
8. visual/media registration where technical media carries safety meaning;
9. owner review of public claims and positioning;
10. production promotion as a distinct step from development merge.

## Social-media launch system

Marketing should track releases rather than advertise an unfinished catalog.

### Content pillars

1. **First-call readiness** — short posts that answer practical beginner questions without pretending to certify the viewer.
2. **Field-skill recognition** — visual recognition, sequence, and stop/ask decisions pulled from released Field Skills.
3. **Department map** — explain what Lighting, Audio, Video, Staging, Backline, Props, Wardrobe, Production, and other crews actually own.
4. **Crew language** — plain-language terminology and handoff concepts.
5. **Career ladder** — show how Fundamentals → Field Skills → Department Support → Systems → Lead/Supervisor/Career paths connect.
6. **Build transparency** — show evidence, source review, practitioner review, and the distinction between education and employer authorization.

### Channel roles

- **Instagram / TikTok / Shorts:** 15–45 second recognition, sequencing, terminology, stop/ask, and behind-the-course clips.
- **YouTube:** 3–6 minute released skill demonstrations, course walkthroughs, department explainers, and longer career-path content.
- **LinkedIn:** workforce-development positioning, curriculum transparency, employer/production-company value, release notes, and partnership outreach.
- **Website:** canonical destination for released courses and source context.

### Release campaign pattern

For each staged release that reaches production:

- T-7: problem post — what new workers usually do not get taught;
- T-5: vocabulary/recognition post;
- T-3: one stop-and-ask decision post;
- T-1: course preview with exact release date;
- Launch: one direct course link plus a clear education-not-authorization statement;
- T+2: quiz/poll from the course;
- T+5: practitioner or learner feedback prompt;
- T+7: next-path post that moves the learner to the following release.

## Claims discipline

Do not market The Crew Blueprint as employer authorization, OSHA certification, union qualification, manufacturer certification, rigging qualification, electrical qualification, structural approval, or guaranteed job placement. Market the product around preparation, vocabulary, systems understanding, safer decision boundaries, observed-practice structure, and career development.
