# MDQ-011 — Warehouse / Rental-Shop Operations

## Evidence decision

Rental-shop and warehouse work is not merely backstage storage. Current entertainment-production employers treat it as a structured operational lane involving:

- receiving and returns;
- picking, scanning, staging, and order preparation;
- inventory control;
- equipment cleaning and organization;
- quality-control checks;
- basic maintenance/repair within role scope;
- loading/unloading and transport handoff;
- department-specific prep;
- documentation and asset-management systems;
- progression into more technical shop roles and field work.

This is a legitimate Crew Blueprint career/competency domain, not just a sidebar to live-show labor.

## Canonical warehouse workflow map

A transferable shop lifecycle is:

> **Return / Receive → Identify → Scan / Record → Sort → QC / Condition Disposition → Clean / Maintain / Route Repair → Re-shelve → Pull for Job → Prep / Configure by Department → Verify Order → Stage → Transport Handoff → Return Cycle**

This is a cross-source workflow synthesis, not one employer's universal procedure.

## Competency families

### General warehouse support

- identify common production-equipment and case categories;
- follow location/bin/rack/order identifiers;
- pick and stage assigned equipment;
- keep aisles/work areas orderly;
- handle returns through the controlling intake process;
- preserve labels/barcodes/serial identities;
- report obvious damage/missing items rather than silently re-shelving questionable gear.

### Asset/inventory workflow

- understand that scan/booking systems establish custody/location/order status;
- distinguish physical presence from system-record status;
- reconcile mismatches through the assigned process rather than inventing an inventory correction.

### QC / prep boundary

A general learner can understand:

- visual/condition inspection concepts;
- completeness checks;
- cleanliness;
- documentation;
- department handoff.

Exact technical testing, repair, firmware/software configuration, electrical safety testing, rigging inspection, battery/service work, or manufacturer-specific acceptance belongs to appropriately trained shop/department roles.

### Career progression

Research supports a pathway such as:

> warehouse support → department prep/QC → repair/specialist shop technician → field technician/system support → department leadership/operations

This is a career map, not a guaranteed ladder.

## Authority boundaries

Warehouse exposure does not authorize:

- powered industrial-truck operation without employer training/authorization;
- electrical/rigging inspection signoff;
- repair beyond assigned competency;
- overriding inventory/maintenance dispositions;
- shipping regulated/hazardous goods without applicable training/process;
- accepting a department-specific technical system merely because it has been physically staged.

**MDQ-011 state:** `researched / draftable for role architecture and general shop workflow; technical sub-role packets still needed`.

# MDQ-012 — Logistics / Truck / Case / Cargo Architecture

## Evidence decision

Live-event logistics is a distinct operational domain linking warehouse, transportation, dock, truck, venue, department, and show workflows. Stagehands commonly interact with this domain, but final transport/cargo/vehicle authority may belong to drivers, truck loaders, logistics leads, warehouse staff, employers, carriers, or regulated roles.

Crew Blueprint should teach **interfaces and role architecture first**, not make general learners responsible for transport decisions they do not own.

## Current industry evidence

### IATSE International — Touring / local conditions

IATSE's touring guidance states that local conditions can determine who unloads trucks, whether stagehands/teamsters/third parties perform the work, how local house heads operate, and whether more pushers are needed because of a venue's physical loading conditions.

Source:  
https://iatse.net/about/stagecraft/touring-2/

This is strong evidence that logistics workflow is not nationally uniform.

### IATSE Local 101 — work assignment architecture

Local 101 explicitly lists specialized positions such as truck loader and forklift among assignments made by the Job Steward/Leadman according to show needs and worker abilities.

Source:  
https://iatse101.net/hiring-hall-referral-work-rules/

### IATSE Local 193 — Trucks and Pushers

Local 193 maintains dedicated educational material for truck loaders and pushers, demonstrating that these can be distinct work assignments with local practices and venue-specific conditions.

Source:  
https://www.iatselocal193.com/trucks-and-pushers

The detailed local handling tips are **local practice evidence**, not a universal Crew Blueprint operating procedure.

### Current Encore warehouse logistics evidence

Encore warehouse roles include pick/scan/stage, loading/unloading vans/trucks/tractor-trailers, shipment preparation, transportation, scanning/tracking, and inventory/QC.

Source:  
https://jobs.encoreglobal.com/en/job/orlando/sr-warehouse-technician-warehouse-ops-orlando-fl/6228/99122439776

## Canonical role/interface map

Potential roles/interfaces include:

- warehouse picker/stager;
- department prep technician;
- warehouse/logistics supervisor;
- pusher/general material-movement crew;
- truck loader;
- forklift/PIT operator;
- driver/carrier;
- department head/road crew receiving gear;
- venue dock/operations staff;
- production/logistics management.

Titles and ownership vary by employer, union local, venue, carrier, and production.

## Transferable workflow map

> **Inventory / Job Pull → Department Prep → Stage for Transport → Load Handoff → Transport → Venue/Dock Receipt → Push/Route to Department → Show Workflow → Strike/Return Flow → Truck/Transport Handoff → Warehouse Return / QC**

This is a logistics lifecycle model, not a truck-pack tutorial.

## Learner-safe competency families

- read case/job/destination labels;
- understand that truck position/order can be production-critical even if the learner does not own the pack plan;
- identify the person controlling the dock/truck flow;
- preserve chain-of-custody/scan/label information;
- route cases to assigned destinations;
- recognize damaged/unstable/misidentified items and escalate;
- understand that warehouse, transport, venue, and department handoffs are separate responsibility points;
- understand that “I can move this case” does not mean “I own transport acceptance.”

## External/controlled boundaries

Do not make general course completion equivalent to authority for:

- commercial vehicle operation;
- forklift/PIT operation;
- regulated cargo acceptance;
- hazardous-material shipping;
- load securement responsibility;
- final truck-pack design/acceptance;
- vehicle positioning/dock control;
- carrier compliance decisions.

**MDQ-012 state:** `researched role/interface baseline; deeper logistics/business/regulatory research remains`.

# MDQ-013 — Production Communications / Radio / Intercom / Readback

## Evidence decision

Production communication has two distinct layers that should not be conflated:

1. **human communication discipline** — listen, confirm, report, use concise language, know who owns the instruction;
2. **communications systems** — radios, wired/wireless partyline, matrix intercom, point-to-point paths, IFB, interfaces, IP/networked systems, channel/key assignments.

Stagehand Fundamentals already covers the first layer. MDQ-013 establishes the systems/context layer and its relationship to Audio, Stage Management, show control, broadcast, and department communication.

## Manufacturer/industry technical evidence

### Clear-Com Intercom Design Guide

Clear-Com identifies common production intercom architectures including:

- wired analog partyline;
- wired network partyline;
- wireless partyline;
- matrix systems;
- interfaces;
- virtual communications;
- IFB/cueing subsystems;
- point-to-point and group/partyline communication models.

Source:  
https://clearcom.com/downloadcenter/technicaldocs/IntercomDesignGuide/Intercom_Design_Guide_%28e-Book%29.pdf

### Clear-Com production applications

Current Clear-Com material shows that live/broadcast communication may combine matrix, wired partyline, wireless users, panels, radios/phones and IP interfaces, and that communications are mission-critical enough to warrant redundancy planning.

Sources:  
https://www.clearcom.com/Resource-Library/Videos/Details/intercom-plan-c-backing-up-the-back-up  
https://clearcom.com/Markets/Broadcast-Production

Manufacturer examples establish real architectures, not universal system design rules.

## Competency ladder

### General user / Stagehand Core interface

A general learner should understand:

- headset/radio/intercom is an assigned production tool, not casual chat;
- identify the assigned channel/key/path and who needs to hear the message;
- listen before transmitting where the system/workflow requires it;
- keep routine messages concise and relevant;
- acknowledge critical instructions according to the production's communication convention;
- avoid changing channels/keys/configuration without assignment;
- protect show-critical communications from unnecessary traffic;
- report failed/unclear comms rather than silently assuming an instruction;
- know that wired/wireless/intercom/radio systems can differ substantially.

Exact phrases, call signs, radio codes, cue words, and channel plans are production-specific unless a controlling source says otherwise.

### Department support / systems understanding

Deeper learning can cover conceptually:

- partyline vs point-to-point;
- matrix routing;
- panels/beltpacks/wireless users;
- IFB/talent cueing;
- interfaces between intercom/radio/phone/IP systems;
- wired vs wireless system tradeoffs;
- redundancy and failure domains;
- documentation/channel-plan concepts.

### Specialist/engineering layer

Configuration, RF planning, network design, matrix programming, system integration, fault diagnosis, and show-critical comms design belong to department/specialist paths and manufacturer/employer training.

## Cross-domain home

MDQ-013 is the transferable communications foundation. Applications then extend into:

- Audio — MDQ-037;
- Stage Management — MDQ-090;
- show control — MDQ-100;
- broadcast/camera — MDQ-045;
- RF — MDQ-036;
- production management/venue ops.

**MDQ-013 state:** `researched conceptual foundation / draftable for general-user system literacy; specialist configuration research remains`.

# MDQ-014 — Outdoor / Weather / Public-Area Transfer

## Evidence decision

Outdoor and public-area event work is not merely an indoor stagehand course with “weather” added. It changes:

- environmental exposure;
- terrain and material movement;
- temporary infrastructure;
- evacuation/shelter planning;
- public/pedestrian interfaces;
- visibility and route control;
- heat/cold/wind/precipitation conditions;
- lightning/severe-weather decision authority;
- communications and emergency planning.

The learner's role is normally to **recognize conditions, follow the event/employer plan, communicate/escalate, and move to designated safety when instructed**—not independently create or override the weather/emergency plan.

## Current OSHA evidence

### Heat — 2026 National Emphasis Program

OSHA updated its National Emphasis Program for indoor/outdoor heat-related hazards on April 10, 2026. OSHA identifies risk factors including strenuous activity, hot environments, lack of acclimatization, and heat-retaining clothing.

Sources:  
https://www.osha.gov/heat-exposure  
https://www.osha.gov/news/newsreleases/osha-national-news-release/20260410

OSHA heat-planning guidance emphasizes employer planning, designated oversight, acclimatization/new-worker considerations, monitoring conditions, controls, first aid/emergency response and training.

Source:  
https://www.osha.gov/heat-exposure/planning

### Emergency preparedness

OSHA recommends advance emergency planning so workers understand roles, routes, exits, chain of command, and actions during emergencies.

Source:  
https://www.osha.gov/emergency-preparedness/getting-started

## National Weather Service event evidence

### Event Ready planning

NWS event-planning guidance identifies weather hazards including lightning, flooding, high wind, thunderstorms/tornadoes, heat, fog, and winter weather, and asks organizers to define shelter/evacuation options, alert methods and time needed to move people.

Source:  
https://www.weather.gov/media/crh/eventready/Event_Ready_Guide.pdf

### Lightning

NWS recommends organized outdoor activities have a lightning-safety plan identifying:

- when activity stops;
- where people go for safety;
- when activity may resume;
- who monitors weather and makes decisions.

NWS states that no place outdoors is safe during a thunderstorm; substantial buildings or appropriate hard-topped vehicles are the preferred safe locations.

Source:  
https://www.weather.gov/safety/lightning-sports

### Event communications / weather watch

NWS event-preparedness resources emphasize centralized communication, weather monitoring, event plans, and an empowered weather decision process.

Sources:  
https://www.weather.gov/byz/event_preparedness  
https://www.weather.gov/lwx/WeatherReadyPavilion

## Transferable early-career competencies

### Outdoor jobsite orientation

- identify weather/shelter/evacuation information provided for the event;
- know who communicates weather/emergency decisions;
- identify stable work/egress/public routes;
- notice terrain, mud/wet surfaces, slopes, wind exposure and changing visibility;
- protect assigned materials/equipment only within the event's plan and authority structure;
- do not remain in a hazardous area merely to finish a task after an evacuation/stop order.

### Heat/environment awareness

- recognize that workload, clothing/PPE, acclimatization and environmental conditions change risk;
- know the employer/site heat plan and access to water/rest/shade or other controls;
- report symptoms/unsafe conditions through the controlling process;
- understand that new/returning workers can require acclimatization controls.

### Public-area interface

- preserve exits/egress and accessible routes;
- manage only assigned cable/protector/barricade/public-route tasks;
- avoid creating trip/obstruction hazards;
- recognize that crowd/security decisions belong to designated event/security/venue authorities;
- communicate changing hazards rather than improvising public-control measures.

## Authority boundaries

A Stagehand/general-hand course should not position the learner as the person who independently decides:

- whether an event is evacuated/cancelled/resumed;
- weather thresholds;
- structural wind limits;
- lightning instrumentation thresholds;
- temporary-structure engineering decisions;
- crowd/security emergency decisions;
- medical diagnosis/emergency command.

Those decisions belong to the event's designated responsible roles and controlling plans.

## Sector-transfer map

Outdoor/public-area evidence should later be applied across:

- festivals;
- amphitheaters;
- stadium fields;
- temporary outdoor stages;
- fairs/community events;
- public plazas/streets;
- corporate/outdoor activations;
- load-in/out exposed to weather even when the performance itself is indoors.

**MDQ-014 state:** `researched/draftable for awareness, plan-following, and escalation; site-specific emergency/engineering authority remains external`.

# Cross-packet synthesis

MDQ-011–014 completes the immediate Stagehand-adjacent universe by adding four systems around the worker:

> **Shop creates/preps the gear → logistics moves it → communications coordinate the people → outdoor/public-area conditions change how the plan must be executed.**

These domains should be available for later synthesis regardless of final course format.

## Shared curriculum principles

1. **Custody matters.** Scan/label/assignment records communicate ownership and status.
2. **Handoffs matter.** Shop, transport, dock, department and worker responsibilities are not interchangeable.
3. **Communications are operational infrastructure.** Human communication behavior and technical comms systems need separate but connected instruction.
4. **Environment changes the plan.** Outdoor/public-area work must follow the event's designated safety and emergency authority.
5. **Exposure is not authorization.** Working around forklifts, trucks, intercom matrices, weather monitoring, rigging or power does not make a beginner the operator/decision-maker.
