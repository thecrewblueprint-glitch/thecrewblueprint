# 22 — Staging & Carpentry Course 2: Build & Load Coordination

**Research Queue ID:** CA-002  
**Status:** Complete research packet  
**Research date:** 2026-08-17  
**Purpose:** Establish the evidence-backed instructional architecture for the second Crew Blueprint Staging & Carpentry pathway course, progressing from ground-hand/scenic handling into supervised staging-team participation, load-in coordination, modular deck assembly awareness, and safe worksite decision-making without implying structural authority, engineering qualification, or independent carpenter status.

---

## Decision question

**What is the correct competency progression from scenic handling in Course 1 to membership on a staging/carpentry team?**

## Bottom-line finding

The correct progression is not simply “carry scenic pieces → build stages.” The learner must cross three distinct competency thresholds:

1. **Vocabulary & Equipment Recognition** — understand what the major staging/carpentry components are, how they function in the build, and which parts of the system are safety-critical.
2. **Load-In Workflow & Team Coordination** — understand how materials are received, staged, sequenced, handled, and routed through a build under a lead’s direction.
3. **Supervised Modular Assembly & Inspection Awareness** — participate in repeatable manufacturer-defined assembly tasks after instruction, recognize obvious incomplete/unsafe conditions, and stop/escalate when the build departs from the plan.

The central teaching model should be:

> **Know the system, follow the build plan, perform only the task you were shown, and never confuse assembly labor with structural authority.**

This model is supported by the existing Crew Blueprint Staging/Carpentry ground-hand research, current manufacturer documentation from Wenger/StageTek and Bil-Jax, OSHA material-handling and walking-working-surface requirements, ESTA’s current ANSI E1.21 temporary-structure standard, and current IATSE touring crew structures showing carpentry as a distinct department with traveling heads and local take-in/put-out labor.

# 1. Relationship to Staging & Carpentry Course 1

Research Package 10 established the appropriate Course 1 boundary:

- move and stage deck sections, risers, stairs, guardrails, scenic pieces, and related hardware;
- assist with modular staging under direction;
- follow layouts or placement instructions at the level assigned;
- protect scenic finishes and equipment;
- recognize obvious hazards and incomplete conditions;
- do not make structural, load, hardware-substitution, or final-acceptance decisions.

Course 2 should preserve that boundary while increasing the learner’s usefulness inside the department.

The learner should finish Course 2 able to:

> **Join a staging/carpentry crew as a junior team member, identify common components and their function, understand the flow of a load-in and strike, stage materials efficiently, participate in repeatable modular assembly after instruction, recognize obvious missing or incorrect conditions, communicate status clearly, and escalate anything involving structural configuration, load, code, design, modification, or final safety acceptance.**

Course completion should not imply the learner is a scenic carpenter, head carpenter, technical director, structural designer, competent person, engineer, or qualified staging supervisor.

---

# 2. Why the progression must be workflow-based rather than tool-based

A weak staging course would organize learning around a list of tools and hardware. The evidence supports a stronger model: **teach the production workflow first, then place tools and components inside that workflow.**

Current manufacturer staging systems are modular but product-specific. Wenger StageTek uses its own deck, leg, bracing, connector, guardrail, stair, storage-cart, and accessory logic. Bil-Jax AS2100 and ST8100 use different deck, rail, leg, bracing, and locking methods. Even within one manufacturer, product families can differ materially.

Therefore the transferable skill is not memorizing one universal deck-locking sequence. It is learning to ask:

1. What system is this?
2. What does the build plan require?
3. Which component am I handling?
4. What is its function?
5. What has the lead shown me to do?
6. What condition should exist when this step is complete?
7. What requires escalation rather than improvisation?

That reasoning pattern transfers between systems even when the hardware does not.

# 3. Recommended Course 2 architecture

The research queue proposed three modules:

1. Vocabulary & Equipment
2. Load-In Workflow
3. Deck Assembly

That structure is sound, but the module framing should be made more precise:

## Module 1 — Staging Systems, Components & Build Vocabulary

Purpose: move the learner from “I recognize a deck” to “I understand how the major parts of a modular staging system relate to one another.”

## Module 2 — Load-In Workflow, Material Staging & Crew Coordination

Purpose: teach the learner how a staging/carpentry department receives, sorts, stages, moves, sequences, and communicates through a build.

## Module 3 — Supervised Modular Assembly, Inspection Awareness & Escalation

Purpose: teach repeatable assembly reasoning and quality/safety checks without turning the learner into a structural decision-maker.

The course should end with an integrated build-sequence scenario rather than a purely vocabulary-based exam.

# 4. Module 1 — Staging Systems, Components & Build Vocabulary

## Recommended outcome

The learner should be able to identify common staging/carpentry components, explain their basic purpose, and distinguish between **handling/assembly functions** and **design/structural functions**.

## 4.1 Platform/deck

Teach the deck as the walking/working surface element of a modular stage or riser system.

The learner should understand:

- decks come in different sizes and ratings;
- deck construction varies by manufacturer and environment;
- deck orientation may matter;
- deck surfaces may be indoor, outdoor, textured, laminated, painted, carpeted, or otherwise finished;
- the deck is only one part of the support system.

Wenger StageTek documentation shows multiple standard deck dimensions, fixed and adjustable legs, accessories, braces, rails, stairs, and carts. Its outdoor staging product uses weather-capable aluminum deck construction and product-specific connector/accessory systems.

### Source

https://shop.wengercorp.com/education/stagetekr-staging.html

https://shop.wengercorp.com/education/stagetek-outdoor-staging.html

## 4.2 Legs, frames, support elements, and leveling systems

The learner should recognize that legs and support frames are not interchangeable generic pieces.

Teach:

- fixed-height legs;
- adjustable/telescoping legs;
- base pads/feet;
- build-up frames or support frames where applicable;
- center/support legs where specified;
- manufacturer-specific leveling features;
- bracing and stabilizing elements.

The learner should understand that a stage can look level or solid while still being incorrectly configured.

**Boundary:** Course 2 may teach the learner how to recognize and install the specific component they have been shown under direction. It should not teach them to invent a leveling workaround, omit supports, change leg geometry, or decide what bracing is necessary.

## 4.3 Connectors, clamps, locks, and pins

Different systems use different attachment methods. Therefore teach categories rather than a universal sequence:

- deck-to-deck connectors;
- leg-to-deck connections;
- leg-to-leg or frame connectors;
- braces;
- pins/clips;
- rail clamps/locks;
- stair/ramp attachment hardware.

The critical transferable habit is:

> **Do not substitute “this looks similar” for “this is the correct component for this system.”**

Bil-Jax’s AS2100 ramp instructions, for example, use a defined guardrail T-Lock seating and fastening process. Wenger StageTek uses its own no-tool clamping and connector systems. That difference is exactly why Course 2 should emphasize system recognition.

## 4.4 Guardrails, handrails, chair stops, and edge protection

Teach these as safety-critical components rather than finishing accessories.

The learner should understand:

- rail systems are product-specific;
- rails may have load/height requirements;
- stairs/ramps may have separate handrail systems;
- missing or incomplete edge protection is an escalation condition;
- the learner does not independently decide code applicability.

Wenger’s StageTek guardrails are documented as 42 inches high and designed to meet specified IBC loading requirements. Bil-Jax provides system-specific rail installation guidance. OSHA’s walking-working-surface rules impose fall-protection duties in applicable work contexts, while OSHA explicitly treats the exposed perimeter of an entertainment stage differently in general-industry rule language. This is a strong reason **not** to teach one simplistic federal “stage rail height rule.”

Instead teach:

> Follow the approved build plan, manufacturer documentation, site rules, and lead/competent-person direction. If a rail, stair, access point, or edge condition does not match the plan, stop and report it.

## 4.5 Stairs, ramps, and access systems

Teach the learner to recognize access components as part of the complete staging system.

Course 2 should cover:

- fixed versus adjustable stair assemblies at a conceptual level;
- hook/bracket/attachment concepts;
- handrails;
- transition/filler components;
- ramps as engineered/system-specific assemblies rather than improvised pathways.

Do not teach learners to independently design accessibility ramps or determine legal/code compliance.

## 4.6 Risers and tiered systems

Teach risers as configurations of staging components with different use cases:

- performer risers;
- drum risers;
- seated risers;
- camera/speaker platforms;
- multi-level staging.

Wenger documents StageTek for flat staging, multi-level stages, seated risers, runways, speaker/camera platforms, corporate events, and displays.

The curriculum implication is that **the same component family can support very different load/use cases**, which reinforces why the build plan—not intuition—governs configuration.

### Source

https://shop.wengercorp.com/education/stagetekr-staging.html

## 4.7 Scenic pieces and finished production assets

Course 1 introduced safe scenic handling. Course 2 should add ownership and install-context awareness:

- finished scenic pieces may contain painted or fragile surfaces;
- some pieces contain integrated hardware, automation interfaces, or attachment points;
- scenic units may need a particular orientation, sequence, or destination;
- moving a unit is different from modifying or repairing it.

The learner should understand:

> Handle what you are assigned; do not drill, cut, fasten, alter, repair, or re-engineer scenic units unless specifically trained and authorized for that task.

## 4.8 Tools and hardware recognition

Course 2 may introduce common staging/carpentry tools in context, but tool operation should remain competency-gated.

Appropriate recognition-level content:

- adjustable/open-end/socket wrenches;
- ratchets;
- hex keys;
- hammers/mallets;
- tape measures;
- levels;
- approved fasteners/hardware for the current system;
- carts/dollies/material-handling aids.

Power saws, woodworking machinery, drilling, fabrication, fastening equipment, and repair operations should not become automatic Course 2 competencies merely because they are common in carpentry.

OSHA’s woodworking and power-tool guidance emphasizes guards, safe condition, correct equipment selection, worker training, and trained/authorized operation.

# 5. Module 2 — Load-In Workflow, Material Staging & Crew Coordination

## Recommended outcome

The learner should understand how a staging/carpentry crew turns an arriving load of components into an organized build without unnecessary rehandling, congestion, damage, or freelancing.

## 5.1 The correct load-in mental model

Do not teach one universal truck-unload order. The current Crew Blueprint repository already corrected that misconception elsewhere: trailer unloading proceeds according to the actual pack and physical access, not a universal category sequence.

For staging/carpentry, teach the department-level workflow as:

**receive → identify → route → stage → verify → assemble → inspect/report → clear/reset**

This is deliberately a **workflow model**, not a fixed physical order.

## 5.2 Receive and identify

The junior crew member should learn to:

- confirm which pieces belong to the staging/carpentry department;
- read labels, markings, carts, or build references where used;
- distinguish complete assemblies from loose components;
- avoid separating matched hardware from the system it belongs to;
- ask before opening, unbundling, or redistributing items.

## 5.3 Route and stage materials

OSHA’s material-handling staging guidance is directly useful here. Poor staging can cause unnecessary carrying, awkward lifting, and repeated handling; planned staging can keep materials accessible and reduce movement.

Course 2 should teach:

- clear the intended route before moving large components;
- keep travel paths and emergency access clear;
- place material where the next build step can reach it;
- avoid creating unstable stacks or leaning heavy components insecurely;
- keep like components organized when the build plan calls for it;
- preserve access to hardware and tools needed next;
- avoid staging material where lifts, forklifts, carts, or other crews need to travel.

### Source

OSHA Materials Handling — Staging & Housekeeping  
https://www.osha.gov/etools/electrical-contractors/materials-handling/staging

## 5.4 Team carry and set-down communication

Large decks and rails can be awkward and heavy. Wenger’s current product information lists examples of StageTek components in weights that commonly justify coordinated team handling rather than solo improvisation.

Course 2 should reinforce:

- designate who is leading the move;
- communicate before lift/set-down;
- protect hands at mating edges and pinch points;
- keep the route visible;
- stop if one person loses grip or footing;
- use carts/material-handling aids when assigned and appropriate.

This should be framed as general safe-handling behavior rather than a manufacturer-independent lifting formula.

### Source

https://shop.wengercorp.com/education/stagetekr-staging.html

## 5.5 Build-zone organization

The learner should understand that staging/carpentry work creates its own temporary hazards:

- open edges;
- partially supported decks;
- loose pins/fasteners;
- protruding legs or braces;
- stacked rails;
- incomplete stairs;
- trip hazards from tools and hardware;
- movement conflicts with other departments.

Teach the junior crew member to keep work zones orderly and communicate incomplete conditions rather than assuming everyone can see them.

## 5.6 Reading the build plan at a junior level

Course 2 should introduce basic plan literacy without presenting the learner as a technical director or designer.

Appropriate concepts:

- overall footprint;
- stage/riser dimensions;
- orientation (upstage/downstage, stage left/right where applicable);
- deck count/layout;
- height changes;
- stairs/ramps/access locations;
- rail locations;
- component callouts;
- work sequence notes.

Do not require structural calculations, engineering interpretation, code analysis, or independent change approval.

## 5.7 Chain of command

Current IATSE touring yellow-card information consistently identifies a Head Carpenter and separates carpentry from other departments. Current tours also show substantial local carpentry take-in and put-out crews working under traveling department leadership.

Examples:

- *The Notebook* lists a Head Carpenter and local carpentry crews for take-in/performance/put-out.
- *Suffs* lists a Head Carpenter and separate carpentry staffing, including deck/fly functions in remarks.
- *Spamalot* lists a Head Carpenter and local carpentry staffing with specialized fly/weight-loader roles.

This is strong evidence for the course’s hierarchy model:

> A junior staging/carpentry worker participates in a department workflow; they do not become the department authority simply because they are physically assembling the system.

# 6. Module 3 — Supervised Modular Assembly, Inspection Awareness & Escalation

## Recommended outcome

The learner should be able to participate in a repeatable modular build after being shown the current system, verify obvious completion cues, and stop when the build departs from instruction.

The course should teach **assembly reasoning**, not a universal product recipe.

## 6.1 The supervised assembly loop

Use this repeatable instructional model:

1. **Identify the system and current build step.**
2. **Confirm the correct component/hardware.**
3. **Watch or review the approved method.**
4. **Perform the assigned physical task.**
5. **Check the visible completion cue.**
6. **Do not force, substitute, or redesign.**
7. **Report completion or discrepancy to the lead.**

This is a safer and more transferable skill than memorizing one manufacturer’s connection sequence.

## 6.2 Visible completion cues

Course 2 can teach learners to recognize obvious visual/tactile cues such as:

- component fully seated where designed;
- pin/clip/lock present where the plan calls for one;
- hardware visibly engaged rather than partially inserted;
- guardrail/stair component attached in the intended location;
- no obvious missing support element;
- deck surfaces aligned as instructed;
- no loose component sitting where it can become a trip or falling-object hazard.

The course must explicitly state that a visible cue is **not structural certification**.

## 6.3 Do not force mismatched hardware

A common novice failure mode is assuming resistance means “hit it harder.” Course 2 should teach the opposite:

> If the component does not fit or lock as demonstrated, stop and verify the part, orientation, connection, and build plan.

Because different staging systems use different connectors, forcing a mismatched component can damage the equipment or create an incomplete connection.

## 6.4 Bracing and support systems

Teach bracing as a structural/safety-critical element whose presence and configuration are governed by the system documentation and build plan.

Wenger’s StageTek materials explicitly identify stabilizing braces and publish separate general bracing guidelines for flat and tiered systems. This supports teaching the learner to recognize bracing and follow instructions, but not to calculate or improvise it.

### Source

https://www.wengercorp.com/literature/

## 6.5 Uneven ground and outdoor staging

Outdoor staging requires additional awareness of ground conditions and weather.

Wenger’s outdoor StageTek documentation discusses use over uneven terrain, product-specific connectors/accessories, and soft-ground considerations. ESTA’s current ANSI E1.21-2024 establishes design/performance requirements for temporary structures used in outdoor entertainment production and ties the standard to structural reliability, safety, use, and maintenance.

Course 2 should teach only the **awareness and escalation layer**:

- ground condition matters;
- settlement or instability matters;
- weather can change the safety context;
- temporary outdoor structures require an approved plan and responsible oversight;
- the junior worker should not improvise anchoring, ballast, leveling, bracing, or structural modifications.

## 6.6 Walking-working surface and load awareness

OSHA requires employers in applicable contexts to ensure walking-working surfaces can support intended loads. OSHA construction rules similarly require strength and structural integrity before employees are allowed to work on a surface.

This does **not** mean Course 2 should teach a junior worker to calculate capacity.

Teach instead:

- stages/platforms have load limits and intended configurations;
- concentrated loads can matter;
- unsupported or incorrectly supported surfaces are not acceptable simply because they “feel solid”;
- workers should not place heavy equipment wherever convenient without direction;
- any uncertainty about capacity or configuration must be escalated.

## 6.7 Inspection awareness versus final acceptance

Course 2 should distinguish three levels:

### Level A — Worker self-check

“Did I perform the assigned step the way I was shown?”

### Level B — Crew/lead verification

“Does this portion of the build match the plan and department procedure?”

### Level C — Structural/code/final acceptance

“Is the completed system approved for use under the applicable design, manufacturer guidance, site rules, code, engineering, and responsible authority?”

Course 2 teaches Level A and awareness of Level B. It must not represent the learner as qualified to provide Level C approval.

# 7. Carpentry skill boundary

The word “carpentry” can create an instructional trap. A staging/carpentry department may perform tasks ranging from modular deck assembly to skilled scenic fabrication, cutting, repair, machining, and show-specific installation.

Course 2 should **not** collapse all of these into one beginner competency.

## Appropriate Course 2 exposure

- measuring/reading dimensions at a basic level;
- identifying common fasteners and hardware;
- recognizing common hand tools;
- moving and staging lumber/scenic/materials when assigned;
- participating in modular assembly;
- holding/positioning material under direction;
- basic install support after demonstration;
- housekeeping and hardware organization.

## Requires separate practical training/authorization

- powered saw operation;
- woodworking machinery;
- nail/staple guns;
- structural cutting/drilling;
- fabrication;
- custom scenic modification;
- repair of structural components;
- changing load-bearing hardware;
- welding;
- independent construction from drawings/specifications.

OSHA’s woodworking guidance emphasizes machine guarding, correct equipment selection, worker training, and trained/authorized operation. That supports keeping machine/tool operation behind a separate practical competency gate.

# 9. Recommended integrated scenario

Use a scenario-based final rather than asking the learner to recite hardware names.

Example scenario:

A junior worker joins a staging crew assembling a multi-level modular platform. Several deck sizes, two leg heights, rails, stairs, and braces are staged nearby. One connector does not seat as expected, a rail is missing from the next planned edge, and a deck is being staged where another crew needs a travel path.

Ask the learner to identify:

- what they can handle independently;
- what they should verify with the build plan;
- what should be moved for workflow/housekeeping reasons;
- what condition requires stop-and-ask;
- who should make the structural/configuration decision;
- what they should report after completing their assigned step.

The correct instructional emphasis is **decision discipline**, not “finish the stage yourself.”

# 10. Assessment architecture

Course 2 assessment should measure four capabilities:

## 10.1 Recognition

Can the learner identify major components and their purpose?

## 10.2 Workflow reasoning

Can the learner place materials and tasks into a sensible department workflow without inventing a universal sequence?

## 10.3 Boundary judgment

Can the learner distinguish an assigned physical task from a structural/configuration decision?

## 10.4 Error detection and escalation

Can the learner notice missing, mismatched, incomplete, unstable-looking, or plan-inconsistent conditions and choose to stop/report rather than improvise?

Recommended assessment mix:

- component-identification retrieval questions;
- “what would you do next?” scenarios;
- build-plan reading exercises;
- photo/diagram discrepancy checks;
- short integrated scenario at the end of each module;
- final cumulative scenario.

Avoid making successful quiz completion equivalent to hands-on competency.

# 12. Evidence on current temporary-structure standards

ESTA’s Technical Standards Program currently lists **ANSI E1.21-2024, Entertainment Technology — Temporary Structures Used for Technical Production of Outdoor Entertainment Events** as the current edition. ESTA states that it establishes minimum design/performance parameters for the design, manufacturing, use, and maintenance of temporary ground-supported structures used in outdoor entertainment production, and notes correlation with the 2024 International Fire Code and 2024 International Building Code.

This is important for Crew Blueprint because it confirms that outdoor temporary-structure safety is a formal engineering/operations discipline. It does **not** mean every modular indoor deck system falls directly under E1.21.

Curriculum rule:

> Cite E1.21 for the existence and seriousness of temporary outdoor structure design/use/maintenance requirements; do not misrepresent it as a universal “deck assembly standard” for every stage platform.

### Source

https://tsp.esta.org/tsp/documents/published_docs.php

# 13. OSHA interpretation cautions

Crew Blueprint should not overgeneralize OSHA standards across every live-event staging context.

Important distinctions:

- OSHA general-industry walking-working-surface rules contain an explicit exception for exposed entertainment-stage perimeters in 29 CFR 1910.28(a)(2)(iii).
- Construction rules use different fall-protection thresholds and applicability tests.
- Temporary elevated work platforms can fall within scaffold rules depending on their purpose and context.
- State Plans may impose requirements that differ from or exceed federal OSHA.

Therefore Course 2 should teach **hazard awareness and compliance escalation**, not a simplified universal rule such as “all stages over X height require the same rail.”

# 14. Evidence-strength notes

## Strong direct evidence

Supported directly by official/manufacturer/union documentation:

- modular staging systems use product-specific decks, legs, braces, connectors, rails, stairs, and accessories;
- manufacturers publish separate owner/assembly/bracing guidance;
- guardrail and stair systems are treated as dedicated system components;
- staging components can be heavy enough to justify coordinated material handling;
- OSHA recognizes hazards from poor material staging, walking-working surfaces, power tools, and woodworking machinery;
- IATSE touring structures distinguish Head Carpenter roles from local carpentry crews;
- ANSI E1.21-2024 governs a defined class of outdoor entertainment temporary structures.

## Strong curriculum inference

Derived from multiple sources rather than stated as a single national rule:

- junior staging-team competency should progress from recognition → workflow → supervised assembly → inspection awareness;
- manufacturer-specific assembly examples should teach reasoning rather than one universal hardware sequence;
- tool recognition should precede separate practical authorization for higher-risk power-tool/fabrication tasks;
- integrated scenario assessment is more appropriate than vocabulary-only testing for this competency level.

## Must remain qualified

Do not present as universal:

- exact crew titles below Head Carpenter;
- exact rail thresholds for every event/site;
- one manufacturer’s brace/connector/rail method;
- one fixed load-in or strike order;
- one universal deck weight, height, or load rating;
- one universal definition of “carpenter,” “deck carpenter,” “staging tech,” or “stagehand.”

# 16. Recommended handoff to curriculum writer

The curriculum writer should preserve five rules:

1. **Teach system logic, not one manufacturer’s recipe.**
2. **Teach workflow before tool operation.**
3. **Teach assembly participation without structural authority.**
4. **Use stop-and-escalate scenarios for mismatched, incomplete, unstable-looking, or plan-inconsistent conditions.**
5. **Do not represent course completion as qualification to design, engineer, approve, fabricate, rig, or independently sign off on staging systems.**

# Final research decision

**CA-002 is sufficiently supported to move into curriculum development.**

The three-module structure should be retained but refined to:

1. **Staging Systems, Components & Build Vocabulary**
2. **Load-In Workflow, Material Staging & Crew Coordination**
3. **Supervised Modular Assembly, Inspection Awareness & Escalation**

The essential progression is:

**recognize → route/stage → follow plan → assemble under direction → self-check → report/escalate**

That progression gives a beginner more responsibility without falsely converting them into a structural decision-maker or independent carpenter.
