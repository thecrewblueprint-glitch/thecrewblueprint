# Research Package 18 — Stagehand Fundamentals: Load-In and Load-Out Validation

**Research date:** 2026-08-16  
**Purpose:** Source-validation packet for the existing *Stagehand Fundamentals* course. This packet validates operational claims about load-in/load-out, manual material handling, docks/trailers, powered industrial truck awareness, and the limits of evidence around beginner assignment patterns.

---

# Executive Summary

The course's description of load-in and load-out is strongly supported by current employer, municipal, union, and OSHA sources.

Current stagehand postings consistently describe **load-in** as unloading trucks, moving road cases and technical equipment, assembling stage/riser/scenic/technical systems, distributing equipment, and running power/data/audio/production cable as assigned. Current IATSE touring materials also distinguish **take-in**, **performance**, and **put-out** staffing and identify separate truck-loader/pusher requirements, confirming that load-in, show, and load-out are genuinely different operational phases.

Current sources also strongly support **load-out** as dismantling/striking, packing, moving, and reloading equipment for transport. However, the stronger course claim that load-out is *generally more physically demanding and higher-risk than load-in* should be framed as a **credible operational inference, not a universal documented rule**. OSHA and NIOSH support the underlying risk factors—fatigue, overexertion, manual handling, reduced visibility, congestion, and night/extended-shift work—but I did not find a national U.S. standard or major employer source that formally ranks load-out as categorically more hazardous than load-in.

Likewise, IATSE touring cards provide excellent evidence that many productions use **more workers for take-in/put-out than performance**, but they do not establish a nationwide rule that *brand-new* stagehands are normally assigned load-in/load-out before show call. That claim should remain qualified.

# 3. Is Load-Out More Physically Demanding / Higher-Risk Than Load-In?

## Finding: the underlying risk factors are real; the blanket ranking is not directly standardized

The course currently frames load-out as faster, more chaotic, physically intense, and more vulnerable to fatigue-related mistakes. The **risk factors are well supported**, but the statement should not be presented as a national industry rule that load-out is always more dangerous than load-in.

### Fatigue and overexertion

OSHA's material-handling guidance identifies strains/sprains, heavy/bulky loads, improper lifting, pinch points, and falling improperly stored materials as core manual-handling hazards.

**Source:** OSHA — Materials Handling and Storage, OSHA 2236  
https://obis.osha.gov/Publications/OSHA2236/osha2236.html

OSHA's ergonomics guidance says repeated lifting, forceful exertion, awkward posture, pushing, pulling, and carrying raise musculoskeletal risk. OSHA also recommends task rotation and rest to reduce over-fatigue.

**Sources:**

OSHA Technical Manual, Section VII, Chapter 1 — Ergonomics  
https://www.osha.gov/otm/section-7-ergonomics/chapter-1

OSHA — Ergonomics: Solutions to Control Hazards  
https://www.osha.gov/ergonomics/control-hazards

OSHA eTool — Materials Handling: Heavy Lifting  
https://www.osha.gov/etools/electrical-contractors/materials-handling/heavy

### Reduced visibility / nighttime work

OSHA's powered-industrial-truck guidance notes additional headlight/lighting concerns when operating at night, outdoors, or where additional lighting would improve visibility, and warns that transitions between bright and dark areas can impair visibility long enough to strike workers or objects.

**Source:** OSHA eTool — Powered Industrial Trucks: Traveling & Maneuvering  
https://www.osha.gov/etools/powered-industrial-trucks/operating-forklift/traveling-maneuvering

### Time pressure

Time pressure is common operationally, but I did not find an OSHA standard defining live-event load-out as inherently time-pressured or legally higher-risk than load-in. This part should remain industry-context language rather than a regulatory claim.

### Course wording recommendation

Prefer:

> **Load-out often combines repeated manual handling, fatigue, tight timelines, active truck/dock traffic, and sometimes lower-light conditions. These factors can increase risk if crews rush or lose situational awareness.**

Avoid:

> “Load-out is always more dangerous than load-in.”

### Claim status

**Partially supported / should be qualified.**

# 4. OSHA Material-Handling Guidance Relevant to Stagehands

## 29 CFR 1910.176 — Handling materials, general

This is the most directly relevant general-industry regulation for stagehand-like material movement and storage.

### 1910.176(a) — aisles, docks, passageways

Where mechanical handling equipment is used, OSHA requires sufficient safe clearances at aisles, loading docks, doorways, turns, and passageways. Aisles and passageways must be kept clear and in good repair, and permanent aisles/passageways must be appropriately marked.

**Source:** OSHA — 29 CFR 1910.176  
https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.176

### 1910.176(b) — stable stacking/storage

Stored materials must not create hazards. Containers/bundles stored in tiers must be stacked, blocked, interlocked, and limited in height so they remain stable and secure against sliding or collapse.

This directly supports training around stable road-case/equipment storage and not creating unstable stacks.

**Source:** OSHA — 29 CFR 1910.176(b)  
https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.176

### 1910.176(c) — housekeeping

Storage areas must be kept free of accumulations that create tripping, fire, explosion, or similar hazards.

This supports the course's emphasis on keeping backstage/dock/staging areas clear during load-in/load-out.

# 5. Manual Lifting, Carrying, Team Lifts, Pushing, and Pulling

## Important legal correction: OSHA has no universal federal maximum manual-lift weight

OSHA explicitly states that it **does not have a standard setting a single maximum amount a worker may lift or carry**. Instead, risk depends on frequency, twisting, vertical height, reach distance, hold duration, and other factors.

**Source:** OSHA Standard Interpretation — Safe weight limits when manually lifting  
https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/standardinterpretations/2013-06-04-0

This is important for Crew Blueprint: do not teach “OSHA says one person may lift up to X pounds” as a regulation.

## OSHA/NIOSH ergonomic guidance

OSHA's Technical Manual recommends designing handling work to minimize weight, range of motion, frequency, awkward postures, and travel distance, and to use carts/conveyors where possible.

**Source:** OSHA Technical Manual — Section VII, Chapter 1  
https://www.osha.gov/otm/section-7-ergonomics/chapter-1

OSHA's ergonomics controls page explicitly gives **two-person lifting of heavy loads** as an administrative/work-practice control.

**Source:** OSHA — Ergonomics: Solutions to Control Hazards  
https://www.osha.gov/ergonomics/control-hazards

OSHA's materials-handling heavy-lifting eTool recommends mechanical assistance, keeping loads in the body's “power zone,” avoiding twisting, keeping loads close, and using two or more people for heavier loads in the specific scenario described by that guidance.

**Source:** OSHA eTool — Materials Handling: Heavy Lifting  
https://www.osha.gov/etools/electrical-contractors/materials-handling/heavy

### Curriculum implication

Teach:

- assess the load before lifting;
- use carts/pallet jacks/mechanical help where appropriate and authorized;
- team-lift awkward/heavy objects when needed;
- keep the load close;
- avoid twisting under load;
- maintain clear sightlines;
- stop and get help when a load cannot be safely controlled.

Do **not** present a fixed OSHA stagehand lifting limit.

# 6. Loading Docks, Trucks, Trailers, Forklifts, and Pallet-Jack Awareness

## Loading docks

OSHA identifies loading docks as hazardous environments for powered industrial trucks and workers nearby. Key hazards include falling from dock edges and slipping/skidding on wet or icy surfaces.

OSHA recommends maintaining safe distance from the dock edge, watching for tail swing, keeping working surfaces clean, and improving dock-edge visibility.

**Source:** OSHA eTool — Powered Industrial Trucks: Loading Docks  
https://www.osha.gov/etools/powered-industrial-trucks/workplace/loading-docks

## Forklift/pedestrian interaction

OSHA states that pedestrians can be struck by forklifts or falling loads and recommends:

- standing clear of operating lift trucks;
- recognizing blind spots and rear swing;
- using pedestrian walkways where available;
- never passing under elevated loads;
- separating pedestrian and forklift traffic where possible.

**Source:** OSHA eTool — Powered Industrial Trucks: Pedestrian Traffic  
https://www.osha.gov/etools/powered-industrial-trucks/workplace/pedestrian-traffic

OSHA's warehousing guidance also states that only trained/certified workers may operate forklifts and that operators must watch for pedestrians, maintain clearance from loading-dock edges, and follow safe stacking procedures.

**Source:** OSHA — Warehousing: Hazards and Solutions  
https://www.osha.gov/warehousing/hazards-solutions

## 29 CFR 1910.178 — Powered Industrial Trucks

The federal powered-industrial-truck standard governs forklift operation/training and is a key boundary for entry-level stagehands: being present around forklifts is not the same as being authorized to operate them.

**Source:** OSHA — 29 CFR 1910.178 / PIT overview  
https://www.osha.gov/powered-industrial-trucks

## Dockboards / bridge plates

OSHA requires dockboards/bridge plates used by powered industrial trucks to be secured before travel and driven over carefully/slowly within rated capacity.

**Source:** OSHA eTool — Traveling & Maneuvering, referencing 29 CFR 1910.178(n)(11)  
https://www.osha.gov/etools/powered-industrial-trucks/operating-forklift/traveling-maneuvering

### Stagehand-level interpretation

A general hand should understand:

- where the pedestrian path is;
- that forklift operators may have blind spots;
- not to walk behind or into the turning radius of moving equipment;
- not to stand under raised loads;
- not to block dock aisles or marked passages;
- not to operate forklifts without required training/authorization;
- to follow the truck loader/lead's directions around trailer and dock flow.

# 7. Case Stacking and Truck-Pack Awareness

## What OSHA supports directly

OSHA 1910.176(b) requires stable and secure tiered storage against sliding or collapse.

**Source:** 29 CFR 1910.176(b)  
https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.176

OSHA's material-handling publication also advises considering container condition, height, weight, stability, accessibility, and correct piling/cross-tiering when storing materials.

**Source:** OSHA 2236 — Materials Handling and Storage  
https://obis.osha.gov/Publications/OSHA2236/osha2236.html

## What OSHA does NOT prescribe

OSHA does **not** prescribe a universal live-event road-case truck-pack sequence such as “heaviest first, then department X, then department Y.” Truck packs are production-, trailer-, case-, and employer-specific.

### Curriculum implication

Teach beginners:

> **Truck loading is controlled work. Follow the truck loader/lead's pack plan. Do not improvise case placement or stacking.**

Teach stability principles and hazard awareness, but do not invent a universal road-case pack order.

# 8. Do New Stagehands Usually Work Load-In/Load-Out Before Show Call?

## Strongest direct evidence: staffing numbers differ materially by phase

Current IATSE touring yellow cards provide unusually strong evidence that productions often require **far more local labor for take-in and put-out than for performance**.

Examples:

### A Beautiful Noise Tour

- Carpentry: 14 take-in / 6 performance / 14 put-out
- Electrics: 20 take-in / 6 performance / 20 put-out
- Audio: 6 in / 2 show / 6 out (included in LX numbers)
- Pushers: 4 in / 8 out
- Truck loaders: 4 unload / 8 load

**Source:** IATSE — A Beautiful Noise Tour  
https://iatse.net/shows/a-beautiful-noise-tour/

### The Sound of Music

- Carpentry: 20 take-in / 8 performance / 20 put-out
- Electrics: 20 take-in / 6 performance / 20 put-out
- Audio: 6 in / 2 show / 6 out
- Pushers: 4 in / 4 out

**Source:** IATSE — The Sound of Music  
https://iatse.net/shows/the-sound-of-music/

### The Notebook Tour

- Carpentry: 16 take-in / 4 performance / 18 put-out
- Electrics: 22 take-in / 4 performance / 22 put-out
- Props: 6 take-in / 2 performance / 6 put-out

**Source:** IATSE — The Notebook Tour  
https://iatse.net/shows/the-notebook/

### Spamalot National Tour

- Carpentry: 14 take-in / 4 performance / 14 put-out
- Electrics: 24 take-in / 5 performance / 24 put-out
- Props: 5 take-in / 4 performance / 5 put-out

**Source:** IATSE — Spamalot National Tour  
https://iatse.net/shows/spamalot-national-tour/

## What this proves

It strongly supports the statement that **many productions require more local general labor during load-in/load-out than during the show**.

## What this does NOT prove

It does **not** prove that every beginner is formally restricted to load-in/load-out or that there is a national rule requiring trust-building before show-call assignment.

The staffing data supports a practical inference: because there are often more local positions during the in/out than during performance, entry-level workers may have more opportunities to enter through those phases.

## Evidence on trust / ability

The City of La Mirada classification provides a useful concrete progression example:

- Stagehand I handles truck loading/unloading, cable running, riser assembly, equipment setup, and basic production work.
- Stagehand II leads Stagehand I workers and handles more complex tasks such as chain-motor rigging and control-board operation.
- Stagehand III leads lower levels and handles safety assessments, programming, power/control tie-in, and client communication.

**Source:** City of La Mirada — Stagehand I/II/III  
https://www.governmentjobs.com/careers/cityoflamirada/classspecs/1563876

This is **one employer's tier structure, not a national stagehand standard**, but it directly illustrates the broader concept that greater responsibility is associated with demonstrated capability and progressively more complex assignments.

### Recommended course wording

> **Load-in and load-out often use larger local crews than the performance itself, making them common entry points for general stagehands. Show-call assignments vary by production and may depend on the worker's demonstrated ability, timing, department knowledge, and the local/venue assignment system.**

### Claim status

**Supported as a qualified practical pattern; not a universal rule.**

# 10. Glossary

**Load-in / Take-in** — Pre-performance movement and assembly of production equipment into the venue/site.

**Show call / Performance call** — Crew work required while the performance/event is actively running.

**Load-out / Put-out** — Post-performance strike, packing, movement, and reloading/removal of production equipment.

**Pusher** — Touring/union-call terminology used on some IATSE yellow cards for local labor assigned to equipment movement. The exact role can vary by production/local.

**Truck loader** — Worker assigned to control or execute trailer loading/unloading. IATSE touring cards may list dedicated truck-loader counts.

**Dockboard / bridge plate** — Surface bridging the gap between a dock and a vehicle/trailer; OSHA has specific powered-industrial-truck requirements for securing and traversing these devices.

**Powered industrial truck (PIT)** — OSHA category including forklifts and certain powered material-handling vehicles governed by 29 CFR 1910.178.

**Manual material handling** — Lifting, lowering, carrying, pushing, pulling, holding, or otherwise moving materials by human effort.

# Bottom Line for Stagehand Fundamentals

The course can confidently teach that **load-in builds the production and load-out strikes, packs, and returns it to transport**, and that both phases involve substantial manual material handling, truck/dock activity, case/equipment movement, and department support.

The strongest regulatory anchors are **29 CFR 1910.176** for clear passageways and stable storage, **29 CFR 1910.178** for powered industrial trucks, and OSHA/NIOSH ergonomics guidance for lifting, carrying, pushing, pulling, and fatigue-related controls.

Two claims should remain qualified:

1. **Load-out is not universally documented as categorically more hazardous than load-in.** Teach the specific risk factors—fatigue, repeated handling, congestion, night work, reduced visibility, and time pressure—rather than a universal ranking.
2. **New stagehands are not nationally required to start on load-in/load-out.** However, real IATSE touring staffing cards show that take-in/put-out frequently use much larger local crews than performance, which makes load-in/load-out a defensible and common entry point in practice.
