# Research Package 20 — Stagehand Fundamentals Gap Audit and Missing-Coverage Fill

**Research date:** 2026-08-16  
**Purpose:** Audit Research Packages 15–19 for weak, partial, or missing support and fill the most important remaining citation gaps for the existing *Stagehand Fundamentals* course.  
**Scope:** This package does **not** replace Packages 15–19. It supplements them where those packets explicitly qualified claims, relied on weaker secondary evidence, or left practical course language without a direct source.

> **Use note for Claude:** Treat this file as a gap-filling companion to Packages 15–19. Where this packet says a point remains local/employer-specific, preserve that qualification. Do not convert local IATSE rules, university theatre handbooks, or manufacturer practices into universal U.S. stagehand standards.

---

# Executive summary

Packages 15–19 already provide strong support for the central Stagehand Fundamentals claims. The remaining gaps were mostly not about the core job itself, but about **specific practical details** that the course currently presents as common practice:

1. Why crews wear **show blacks** and whether that convention is documented beyond job-posting dress codes.
2. Whether common beginner tools such as an **adjustable/crescent wrench, flashlight, multi-tool, pen/pencil, and work bag** appear in real stagehand/technical-theatre requirements.
3. Whether **over-under cable coiling** can be supported by a manufacturer/training source rather than only retailer/editorial guidance.
4. Whether **gaff tape, spike tape, and electrical tape** have distinct documented production uses.
5. Whether OSHA directly supports **near-miss reporting** and proactive hazard reporting.
6. Whether OSHA supports the course's practical preference for **pushing rather than pulling** carts/cases where feasible.
7. Whether the course's claim that load-out can become more hazardous because of **fatigue and reduced visibility** can be grounded more directly.
8. Whether there is stronger real-world evidence for **show-call assignment being tied to reliability/ability and prior load-in/load-out participation**.
9. Whether there is a real **first-30-days onboarding standard** for stagehands.
10. Whether exact headset/radio phrases such as **“copy,” “heard,” and “standing by”** are standardized.

The audit found strong sources for items 1–8. Items 9 and 10 remain **non-standardized** and should stay framed as employer/local/production-specific.

# 1. Gap from Packages 15–16: Why “show blacks” are used

## Previous status

Package 16 established that all-black stagehand attire is real employer practice but is not OSHA PPE. The weaker point was the **reason** for the convention.

## New evidence

Western Oregon University's Theatre Student Handbook requires black clothing for run crew and explicitly explains that crew members may work in front of the audience in near-blackout conditions; the clothing provides a uniform, discreet appearance.

**Source:** Western Oregon University — Theatre Student Handbook  
https://wou.edu/theatre-dance/theatre/theatre-student-handbook/

The University of Minnesota Duluth's shift-crew guidance says the default backstage dress code is all black because crew generally are not supposed to be noticed backstage/onstage by the audience.

**Source:** University of Minnesota Duluth — Shift Crew Practicum Information  
https://www.d.umn.edu/~mharvey/th1699informationshiftcrew.html

Radford University's technical-theatre guidance lists running-crew “blacks” as black shirt, pants, shoes, and socks and separately lists a flashlight with clip as a basic running-crew supply.

**Source:** Radford University — Theatre & Cinema, Current Students / Technical-Design Requirements  
https://www.radford.edu/visual-performing-arts/theatre-and-cinema-department/current-students/index.html

IATSE Local 19 provides especially useful professional-stagehand context: most load-ins/load-outs use work clothes, while many show calls use “show blacks” or dress blacks, with requirements varying by venue and call.

**Source:** IATSE Local 19 — Instructions to Referral Stagehands / Code of Conduct  
https://www.iatse19.org/index.php/about/code-of-conduct/

## Curriculum conclusion

**Now strongly supportable as industry practice, but not a universal standard:**

> “Show blacks” are black, low-visibility work clothes commonly required when stagehands/run crew may be visible during a performance. The purpose is to keep crew visually unobtrusive and professionally consistent. Exact clothing requirements vary by venue, employer, production, and call type.

Do **not** teach show blacks as OSHA PPE.

# 2. Gap from Package 19: Real stagehand tool lists

## Previous status

Package 19 validated general hand-tool safety but had weaker evidence for the course's particular beginner tool list.

## New evidence

IATSE Local 99's current referral rules include an official tools list and require new referrals to attend orientation/safety training before receiving work. The published list includes photo identification, pen/pencil, and a tool bag/backpack, followed by call-specific tools.

**Source:** IATSE Local 99 — Job Referral Procedure and Rules / Tools List  
https://iatse99.org/about/job-referral-rules/

Radford University lists a tape measure and **8-inch crescent wrench** for scene-shop technical students and **blacks plus a flashlight with clip** for running crews.

**Source:** Radford University — Theatre & Cinema Technical/Design Requirements  
https://www.radford.edu/visual-performing-arts/theatre-and-cinema-department/current-students/index.html

SUNY New Paltz's production handbook lists a **Maglite/flashlight** and **multi-tool** among backstage/production supplies and requires black garments for previews/performances.

**Source:** SUNY New Paltz — Theatre Production Handbook  
https://www.newpaltz.edu/fpa/theatre/productions/production-handbook/

IATSE Local 122's referral rules require workers to report with the appropriate tools for assigned jobs and direct referents to a Required Tool List; failure to arrive with required tools can result in removal from the call.

**Source:** IATSE Local 122 — Referral Hall Rules  
https://www.iatse122.org/resources/referral-hall-rules/

## Curriculum conclusion

The course can safely teach that **an adjustable/crescent wrench, flashlight, writing tool, and compact tool bag are common stagecraft supplies**, but exact required tools are call- and local-specific.

Recommended wording:

> Bring only the tools your employer/local expects you to provide, and check the call instructions. A small adjustable wrench, flashlight, pen/pencil, and compact tool bag appear on real stagecraft tool lists, but specialized tools depend on your assignment.

Do not imply that every new general hand is expected to own or use a multi-tool, knife, meter, drill, or specialized department tool.

# 3. Gap from Package 19: Primary-source over-under cable coiling support

## Previous status

Package 19 relied substantially on Sweetwater editorial/training material. That was useful but not as strong as a manufacturer/training source.

## New evidence

QSC's live-sound technical guidance states that repeated flexing and bending damages cables internally and recommends proper coiling without sharp bends to extend cable life. QSC specifically says it favors the **over/under technique**.

**Source:** QSC — How to Ready Your Gear for the First Gig Back  
https://blogs.qsc.com/live-sound/how-to-ready-your-gear-for-the-first-gig-back/

QSC's current training portal provides a step-by-step “How to properly roll a cable” lesson demonstrating alternating over/under loops and tying the finished coil.

**Source:** QSC Training — How to Properly Roll a Cable  
https://training.qsc.com/mod/book/tool/print/index.php?id=1715

## Curriculum conclusion

The over-under method is now supported by an **audio-manufacturer training source**, not just retailer guidance.

Teach it as a professional cable-care technique, not as an OSHA rule:

> Over-under coiling alternates the natural twist of the cable, helping it deploy cleanly and reducing kinks and repeated bend stress. Follow department/manufacturer instructions where specialty cable requires a different bend radius or storage method.

# 4. Gap from Package 19: Gaff tape, spike tape, and electrical tape are not interchangeable

## New evidence

Pro Tapes, an established entertainment-industry tape manufacturer, documents distinct uses:

### Gaffer tape

Pro Gaff is marketed for entertainment applications including cable/wire management, equipment-case labeling, temporary holding, stage marking, and trip-hazard management. It is matte, hand-tearable, and designed to remove more cleanly than typical duct tape.

**Source:** Pro Tapes — Pro Gaff  
https://www.protapes.com/pro-gaff-tape/

### Spike tape

Pro Spike is a narrow matte cloth tape specifically intended for stage spiking, marking, labeling, color coding, and temporary holding. Pro Tapes describes stage-management use for marking actor, set-piece, and prop positions.

**Sources:**
- Pro Tapes — Pro Spike  
  https://www.protapes.com/products/pro-spike-matte-cloth-gaffers-tape/
- Pro Tapes — Stage Managers Need Pro Spike Tape  
  https://www.protapes.com/blog/stage-managers-need-pro-spike-tape/

### Electrical tape

Pro Tapes' rigging/electrical product documentation distinguishes UL-listed vinyl electrical tape for wire insulating, splicing, and color coding from gaffer/spike products.

**Source:** Pro Tapes — Rigging and Electrical  
https://www.protapes.com/industries/arts-entertainment/rigging-and-electrical/

## Curriculum conclusion

The course can teach functional recognition:

- **Gaff tape:** temporary entertainment-production securing/marking/cable-management uses.
- **Spike tape:** narrow stage-position marks and color-coded presets.
- **Electrical tape:** electrical-insulation/splicing/color-coding product category when appropriately rated and used by the responsible department.

Important boundary:

> Knowing what a tape is commonly used for does not authorize a general hand to make electrical repairs, modify wiring, or tape over a hazard instead of reporting/correcting it properly.

# 5. Gap from Packages 16–17: Near-miss reporting and incident learning

## New OSHA evidence

OSHA's Recommended Practices for Safety and Health Programs explicitly tells employers to establish processes for workers to report:

- hazards;
- injuries/illnesses;
- close calls/near misses;
- safety and health concerns.

OSHA also says close calls and near misses should be investigated because they identify hazards that could cause future harm.

**Sources:**
- OSHA — Worker Participation  
  https://www.osha.gov/safety-management/worker-participation
- OSHA — Hazard Identification and Assessment  
  https://www.osha.gov/safety-management/hazard-identification
- OSHA — Incident Investigation  
  https://www.osha.gov/incident-investigation

OSHA's “10 Ways to Get Your Program Started” likewise recommends a reporting system for injuries, incidents, near misses, hazards, and safety concerns without fear of retaliation.

**Source:** OSHA — 10 Ways to Get Your Program Started  
https://www.osha.gov/safety-management/ten-easy-things

## Curriculum conclusion

The course can strongly support an entry-level **near-miss/close-call reporting** lesson.

Recommended framing:

> A near miss is useful safety information, not “nothing happened.” Report it through the site's established process so the underlying hazard can be corrected before someone is hurt.

Do not imply OSHA requires every employer to use one specific near-miss form or reporting app.

# 6. Gap from Packages 18–19: Pushing vs. pulling carts and road cases

## New OSHA evidence

OSHA's material-handling ergonomics guidance explicitly says to **avoid pulling when possible** because pushing generally requires less effort and lets workers use body weight to assist the movement. OSHA also warns that pulling carts can lead to impacts with the worker's shins/ankles.

OSHA recommends:

- transport devices such as hand trucks and pallet jacks;
- handles in the worker's power zone;
- keeping loads low enough to see over them;
- maintaining wheels and flooring;
- reducing load weight where required;
- using ramps/elevators/mechanical aids when terrain or stairs make manual movement difficult.

**Source:** OSHA — Materials Handling: Pushing, Pulling and Carrying  
https://www.osha.gov/etools/electrical-contractors/materials-handling/pushing

OSHA's related back-safety guidance similarly says to push rather than pull when possible and emphasizes cart maintenance, handle height, flooring, and mechanical assistance.

**Source:** OSHA — Ergonomic Solutions: Pushing and Pulling Carts  
https://www.osha.gov/etools/electrical-contractors/supplemental-information/solutions/tasks-back

## Curriculum conclusion

This fills a practical load-in claim:

> When conditions allow, pushing a case/cart is generally preferable to pulling because it provides better body mechanics and reduces the chance the cart will strike the worker's legs. However, workers should follow the route, team method, spotter instructions, ramp direction, and case-specific handling procedure established for the call.

Do not turn this into “never pull.” Some confined routes, guiding tasks, or team maneuvers require different positioning under a lead's direction.

# 7. Gap from Package 18: Why load-out can become riskier — fatigue and visibility

## Previous status

Package 18 correctly refused to state that load-out is universally more dangerous than load-in because no national standard ranks the phases that way.

## Stronger supporting evidence for the underlying factors

NIOSH's current 2026 fatigue guidance states that work-related fatigue is associated with night work, extended work hours, physically/mentally demanding tasks, stress, and hot environments. Fatigue can slow reaction time, reduce attention/concentration, impair short-term memory, and impair judgment.

**Source:** NIOSH/CDC — Fatigue and Work (2026)  
https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/fatigue/about/index.html

NIOSH also states that long hours and nonstandard schedules increase fatigue-related safety risk and that demanding tasks, night shifts, and insufficient recovery can increase errors and injuries.

**Source:** NIOSH — Working Hours and Fatigue: Meeting the Needs of American Workers and Employers  
https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/blogs/2023/fatigue.html

OSHA's manual-material-handling guidance directly states that **low visibility or poor lighting increases the chance of trips and falls** and recommends proper lighting for low-light work areas.

**Source:** OSHA — Materials Handling: Heavy Lifting  
https://www.osha.gov/etools/electrical-contractors/materials-handling/heavy

## Curriculum conclusion

The course should preserve the qualified wording:

> Load-out often occurs after the show, when workers may be tired, the schedule may be compressed, and work may occur in reduced-light conditions. Those factors can increase risk, so load-out should be treated as a controlled, sequenced operation rather than a race.

This is a **well-supported risk-factor synthesis**, not a national statistical finding that every load-out is more dangerous than every load-in.

# 8. Gap from Packages 15 and 18: Stronger evidence for show-call assignment and “building trust”

## IATSE Local 101 — unusually direct evidence

IATSE Local 101's current hiring-hall rules provide one of the clearest real examples of how show-call opportunities can relate to broader call participation and worker capability.

Its referral criteria include:

- capability / demonstrated job skills;
- industry and recent job-specific experience;
- reliability;
- status such as journeyman, apprentice, casual, or new hire.

The rules state that the Job Steward/Leadman makes assignments according to production needs and worker abilities.

Most importantly, Local 101's rules state that **show call is offered first to workers who are working both load-in and load-out**, unless specialized skill requires otherwise.

Local 101 also uses a **180-day probationary period for new hires** as an evaluation period for continued placement on the hire list.

**Source:** IATSE Local 101 — Hiring Hall Referral Work Rules  
https://iatse101.net/hiring-hall-referral-work-rules/

## Additional tier evidence

The City of La Mirada's Stagehand I/II/III classification shows a real progressive structure. Stagehand I performs general setup/operation tasks, while Stagehand II leads Stagehand I workers and takes on more complicated responsibilities such as chain-motor rigging, scenic-rail weight loading, control-board operation, facility opening/lockup, and other higher-responsibility tasks.

**Source:** City of La Mirada — Stagehand I/II/III Classification  
https://www.governmentjobs.com/careers/cityoflamirada/classspecs/1563876

## Curriculum conclusion

The original course statement can be strengthened but must remain qualified:

> New hands commonly build trust through reliable load-in/load-out participation and demonstrated capability. Some real hiring halls explicitly prioritize workers who handle both load-in and load-out when offering show calls. However, this is **not a universal U.S. rule**; show-call assignment varies by production, venue, union local, employer, skill requirements, and worker experience.

This is now better supported than the earlier generic statement, but Local 101's rule must remain identified as **local-specific evidence**.

# 9. Gap from Package 19: “First 30 days” onboarding

## Audit finding

There is still **no national U.S. first-30-days stagehand onboarding standard**.

However, real locals document structured new-referral/onboarding periods that can support the course's general idea of an early development period.

### IATSE Local 99

New applicants must complete mandatory Orientation and Safety training before receiving/performing work offers. New referrals must then work an entire call within 90 days of completing orientation or reapply.

**Source:** IATSE Local 99 — Job Referral Procedure and Rules  
https://iatse99.org/about/job-referral-rules/

### IATSE Local 101

New hires complete a **180-day probationary period** used to evaluate whether they may remain on the hiring list.

**Source:** IATSE Local 101 — Hiring Hall Referral Work Rules  
https://iatse101.net/hiring-hall-referral-work-rules/

### IATSE Local 122

New applicants enter a referral system with a **one-year probationary period** after first dispatch; the rules also require appropriate tools, professional conduct, and reporting to the job steward before call time.

**Source:** IATSE Local 122 — Referral Hall Rules  
https://www.iatse122.org/resources/referral-hall-rules/

## Curriculum conclusion

Keep the course's **“First 30 Days” plan as a Crew Blueprint development tool**, not an industry rule.

Recommended labeling:

> **Crew Blueprint 30-Day Development Plan** — a suggested learning sequence for a new hand, not a standardized employer/union probation or onboarding schedule.

Real-world onboarding periods vary from mandatory pre-dispatch orientation to 90-day, 180-day, or one-year local evaluation/probation structures.

# 10. Gap from Package 15: Radio/headset phrases and communication etiquette

## Audit finding

No reliable evidence was found for a universal U.S. stagehand radio vocabulary in which every crew must use the exact words **“copy,” “heard,” or “standing by.”** Those phrases should remain examples, not standards.

## What is strongly documented

Western Oregon University requires headset conversations during performances to remain focused on the work and prohibits extraneous conversation.

**Source:** Western Oregon University — Theatre Student Handbook  
https://wou.edu/theatre-dance/theatre/theatre-student-handbook/

University of Kansas theatre guidance similarly states that show communication channels are for business only and that problems should be immediately communicated to stage management, which determines next steps.

**Source:** University of Kansas — Theatre Practicum Job Descriptions  
https://kutheatre.ku.edu/practicum-job-descriptions

Gettysburg College's current theatre handbook states that headsets are used only for production-required communication such as calls, warnings, and cues, not chit-chat.

**Source:** Gettysburg College — Theatre Arts Production Handbook  
https://www.gettysburg.edu/academic-programs/theatre-arts/productions/student-handbook-departmental-productions

Arkansas State's theatre handbook uses a specific local convention: backstage calls over the paging system are acknowledged with “thank you” so stage management knows the call was heard and understood. This is useful evidence that acknowledgement practices exist, but also proves they are **production/local conventions rather than one national vocabulary**.

**Source:** Arkansas State University — Theatre Student Handbook  
https://kb.astate.edu/books/department-of-theatre/page/theatre-student-handbook

## Curriculum conclusion

Teach principles before phrases:

- keep radio/headset traffic brief and job-relevant;
- acknowledge important instructions so the sender knows they were received;
- report problems promptly;
- do not talk over cues or critical traffic;
- follow the vocabulary established by the production/venue.

The course may list “copy,” “heard,” “standing by,” or “thank you” as **examples of concise acknowledgement language**, but not as universal mandated callouts.

# 11. Gap from Packages 15/17: Arrival, check-in, and early-call behavior

The course contains a “first 15 minutes on site” concept. There is no national first-15-minutes standard, but real referral halls strongly support the underlying behaviors.

IATSE Local 122 requires referred workers to report to the Job Steward **15–20 minutes before the scheduled start**, arrive with appropriate tools, and follow site work rules.

**Source:** IATSE Local 122 — Referral Hall Rules  
https://www.iatse122.org/resources/referral-hall-rules/

IATSE Local 205's published hall regulations similarly direct referents to report to the Job Steward about **15 minutes before** start time and sign in upon arrival.

**Source:** IATSE Local 205 — Referral Hall Regulations  
https://old.iatse205.org/hiring-hall/hall-regulations/

IATSE Local 99 requires orientation/safety training for new referrals and distributes work through formal referral procedures.

**Source:** IATSE Local 99 — Job Referral Procedure and Rules  
https://iatse99.org/about/job-referral-rules/

## Curriculum conclusion

The lesson can safely teach:

> Arrive early enough to locate the correct entrance/check-in point, report to the assigned steward/lead, receive site instructions, and be ready to work at call time.

Do not teach “15 minutes early” as a universal industry law; it is a common local rule/example.

# 12. Gap from Package 17/18: Housekeeping and end-of-call cleanup

The existing course teaches trash/tape cleanup, sweeping, and restoring work areas.

East Texas Baptist University's theatre handbook explicitly states that after work calls, scenic/lighting/costume crews are responsible for removing debris/materials and thoroughly sweeping the rehearsal/performance floor.

**Source:** East Texas Baptist University — Theatre Student Handbook  
https://www.etbu.edu/academics/academic-schools/school-communication-performing-arts/department-music-and-theatre-arts/theatre-student-handbook

OSHA 29 CFR 1910.22 independently requires walking-working surfaces to be maintained in a clean, orderly, and sanitary condition and kept free of hazards.

**Source:** OSHA — 29 CFR 1910.22, General Requirements for Walking-Working Surfaces  
https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.22

## Curriculum conclusion

“Trash, tape, sweep, and final cleanup” is defensible as real stagecraft practice supported by both venue/theatre procedure and OSHA housekeeping principles. Exact end-of-call duties remain employer/venue specific.

# Remaining unresolved / intentionally non-universal claims

After this gap fill, the following should **still not** be presented as nationwide standards:

1. **Every beginner starts with load-in/load-out before show call.** There is strong evidence this happens in some systems, but assignment remains local/employer/production specific.
2. **Load-out is always more dangerous than load-in.** Fatigue, night work, reduced visibility, congestion, and time pressure are real risk factors, but there is no national stagehand injury dataset establishing a universal phase ranking.
3. **Every stagehand must carry one identical tool kit.** Real locals/venues publish tool lists, but they differ.
4. **Every stagehand call requires show blacks.** Load-in/out frequently use ordinary work clothes; show-call attire varies substantially by venue and client.
5. **“Copy,” “heard,” and “standing by” are mandatory standard responses.** Productions establish their own concise communication conventions.
6. **A 30-day probation or onboarding period is standard.** Real systems use widely different evaluation periods and processes.
7. **One OSHA rule universally prohibits untrained use of all fly systems, stage machinery, entertainment automation, and rigging systems.** Equipment-specific regulations, employer authorization, manufacturer instructions, collective-bargaining rules, and entertainment-industry standards govern the boundary.
