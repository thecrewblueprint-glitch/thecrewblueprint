# Research Package 05 — Stage Management and Cross-Cutting Production Leadership in U.S. Live Events

**Research date:** 2026-08-15

## Research questions

1. Is stage management a distinct discipline and career track in U.S. theatrical/live production, separate from general stagehand work and technical departments?
2. Are crew-lead, crew-chief, site-supervisor, production-coordinator, and production-manager roles reached through one fixed starting track, or can workers enter leadership/coordination from multiple backgrounds?

---

# Executive conclusion

## 1. Stage management is a distinct professional discipline

Yes. In U.S. professional theatre, stage management is clearly treated as its **own craft and career track**, not simply as senior stagehand work and not as a technical department such as audio, lighting, video, or rigging.

Actors' Equity Association (AEA) represents professional **actors and stage managers** in live theatre and explicitly describes stage managers as leaders and collaborative specialists. Equity's description assigns them responsibilities such as coordinating rehearsals and performances, maintaining the operational record of the production, leading technical rehearsals, supervising performance cueing, facilitating interdepartmental communication, and maintaining the director/producer's artistic intent after opening.

**Source:** Actors' Equity Association, "Actors’ Equity Association Celebrates Stage Manager Day"  
https://actorsequity.org/news/PR/2023/02/15/SMDay2023

IATSE's own Pink Contract touring guide reinforces the separation. It describes the stage-management team—usually a Production Stage Manager (PSM) plus assistants—as responsible for maintaining the artistic aspects of the show and coordinating with actors, musicians, head carpenter, technical department heads, company management, and general management. The same guide separately describes the Head Carpenter as the overall head of the touring technical crew. On an Equity tour, IATSE states that the stage managers are Equity members.

**Source:** IATSE, "An Introduction to Pink Contract Touring"  
https://iatse.net/about/stagecraft/touring-2/

This is strong evidence that a theatrical production contains at least two different leadership structures:

- **Stage management:** performance/rehearsal coordination and show calling.
- **Technical crew leadership:** technical execution, load-in/load-out, and department/crew operations.

They collaborate heavily but are not the same career track.

## 2. Production leadership is partly track-specific and partly cross-cutting

The honest answer is **both**.

Some leadership roles are clearly the ceiling of a specific craft track:

- Audio technician → A1 / audio lead / audio crew chief.
- Lighting technician/programmer → lighting lead / head electrician / lighting crew chief.
- Rigger → lead/head rigger.
- Carpenter → head carpenter.
- General stagehand → lead hand / crew chief.
- Stage manager → Production Stage Manager / senior stage-management leadership.

Those positions generally require deep credibility in the craft being led.

But broader coordination roles—especially **Production Coordinator, Assistant Production Manager, Production Manager, Technical/Production Services Manager, Director of Production, and some Event Operations management roles**—are demonstrably cross-cutting. Current job postings explicitly accept experience from multiple pathways, including stage management, technical direction, production management, stagehand work, design, venue operations, or broader theatrical/live-event production.

Therefore, production leadership should be modeled as a **cross-cutting management/coordination layer that multiple craft pathways can feed into**, while preserving craft-specific leadership ladders underneath it.

# Part I — Stage management as a distinct career discipline

## What stage managers actually do

Actors' Equity describes Stage Managers and Assistant Stage Managers as professionals who:

- coordinate and facilitate rehearsals, calls, and performances;
- assemble and maintain records needed for technical and artistic operation;
- lead technical rehearsals;
- supervise performance cueing;
- facilitate interdepartmental communication; and
- maintain the artistic vision after opening.

**Source:** Actors' Equity Association  
https://actorsequity.org/news/PR/2023/02/15/SMDay2023

A current 2026 Amore Opera posting gives a concrete operational example. Its Stage Manager is expected to run technical rehearsals and cue-to-cue sessions, maintain the prompt book, record blocking, call performances, and organize backstage operations. It asks for at least one year of stage-management experience and prefers opera/musical-theatre experience.

**Source:** Playbill, Amore Opera Stage Manager posting, 2026  
https://playbill.com/job/position-stage-manager-amore-opera/611f2b10-fab3-4598-8420-c36ce1aeeee7

A 2026 U.S. national-tour Production Stage Manager posting from Paquin Entertainment describes the PSM as part of the tour leadership team, balancing artistic integrity, operational leadership, and company coordination. It requires professional stage-management experience and strong cue-calling, communication, leadership, and touring skills.

**Source:** Playbill, Paquin Entertainment Group Production Stage Manager posting, 2026  
https://playbill.com/job/production-stage-manager/e48e5e8f-49a5-400c-bbce-dfb8c53c7d09

These responsibilities are qualitatively different from general stagehand work. A stage manager's defining competency is not primarily moving/building equipment or independently operating a technical system. It is **coordinating the production process and performance execution across people, departments, timing, communication, documentation, and artistic continuity**.

# Typical stage-management progression

There is no single legally mandated national ladder, but professional theatre commonly uses titles such as:

1. **Stage Management Intern / Apprentice / Production Assistant** — employer-specific early-career roles.
2. **Assistant Stage Manager (ASM)** — supports rehearsal/performance operations, backstage execution, paperwork, tracking, and assigned cueing/coordination.
3. **Stage Manager (SM)** — may lead or independently manage a production depending on scale.
4. **Production Stage Manager (PSM)** — senior stage-management authority on larger productions/tours, often supervising other stage managers/ASMs and coordinating the complete show-management function.

The Stage Managers' Association (SMA) job system explicitly receives postings ranging from internships through Production Stage Manager positions in theatre, opera, dance, touring, and related fields.

**Source:** Stage Managers' Association, Jobs  
https://www.stagemanagers.org/sma-jobs/

SMA also describes itself as the only U.S. professional organization devoted specifically to working stage managers and recognizes stage managers at all career levels.

**Source:** Stage Managers' Association  
https://www.stagemanagers.org/

# Union and professional-organization structure

## Actors' Equity Association

For professional U.S. theatre, AEA is the most important labor organization for stage managers. Equity states that it represents more than 51,000 actors and stage managers and governs members through representation that specifically includes stage managers.

**Source:** Actors' Equity Association, About Equity  
https://www.actorsequity.org/aboutequity

Equity agreements distinguish Stage Manager and Assistant Stage Manager rates. For example, its Single Engagement Agreement publishes separate minimum compensation for actors, ASMs, and SMs.

**Source:** Actors' Equity Association, Single Engagement Agreement  
https://www.actorsequity.org/resources/producers/agreements-and-forms/single-engagement-agreement

Equity's internal Casting Call system specifically allows employers to advertise stage-manager openings to Equity members.

**Source:** Actors' Equity Association, Auditions and Job Interviews  
https://www.actorsequity.org/resources/Producers/casting-call-how-to/

## Stage Managers' Association

SMA is not a union. It is a professional association devoted to stage management, advocacy, education, mentoring, networking, and professional development.

**Source:** Stage Managers' Association  
https://www.stagemanagers.org/

SMA's own resource page identifies Actors' Equity as the theatrical union for stage managers.

**Source:** SMA Advocacy & Resources  
https://www.stagemanagers.org/advocacy-resources/

## IATSE

There is **not a single U.S.-wide IATSE stage-manager local equivalent to Actors' Equity's role in professional theatre**. IATSE primarily represents stagecraft technicians and allied crafts. Its own touring guidance says that on an Equity tour, stage managers are members of Actors' Equity.

**Source:** IATSE Pink Contract Touring guide  
https://iatse.net/about/stagecraft/touring-2/

However, labor jurisdiction can vary by employer, production type, and region. IATSE locals can represent employees whose employer classification is "Stage Manager" in particular bargaining units. A non-U.S. example—useful only as evidence of jurisdictional variability—is IATSE Local 63's representation of a Stage Manager and Technical Coordinator at the Winnipeg Symphony Orchestra.

**Source:** IATSE, Winnipeg Symphony Orchestra workers join Local 63  
https://iatse.net/winnipeg-symphony-orchestra-workers-latest-to-join-iatse/

For Crew Blueprint, the safe generalization is:

> In U.S. professional theatre, stage management is primarily associated with Actors' Equity; IATSE should not be presented as the default stage-manager union, though particular employers or sectors can have different bargaining arrangements.

# Formal training and certification

## Training exists; a universal national certification does not

Stage management has extensive formal education and professional-development infrastructure, but there is **no single national personnel certification equivalent to ETCP rigging/electrical certification that is required to call oneself a professional stage manager**.

Examples of formal training infrastructure include:

- college/university BFA/MFA and technical-theatre programs;
- USITT stage-management education;
- mentorship programs;
- employer-specific training;
- professional observation and mentoring through SMA;
- on-the-job ASM/SM progression.

USITT maintains Stage Management as a distinct management specialization. Its current Management Commission includes specific representatives for Stage Management and Production Management as separate areas.

**Source:** USITT Commissions  
https://www.usitt.org/education-training/commissions

USITT's Stage Management Mentor Project pairs students with professional stage managers and assigns participants to work as stage managers or assistant stage managers at major conference events.

**Source:** USITT, 2026 Stage Management Mentor Project  
https://www.usitt.org/news/2026-stage-management-mentor-project-cohort-announced

USITT's Backstage Study Guide identifies stage-management competencies including terminology, safe practice, organizational strategy, communications, scheduling, blocking notation, calling a show, and event-safety strategies.

**Source:** USITT Backstage Resources & Study Guide  
https://www.usitt.org/education-training/backstage/backstage-resources-study-guide

USITT also offers specialized early-career Stage Management training, including current programs involving Cirque du Soleil and Oregon Shakespeare Festival contexts.

**Sources:**  
https://www.usitt.org/education-training/training-ground  
https://www.usitt.org/education-training/usitt-training-ground-at-the-oregon-shakespeare-festival

## Equity membership is not a skills certification

AEA membership is a union status, not a stage-management competency certification. Under Equity's permanent Open Access policy, a stage manager can become eligible by demonstrating professional paid stage-management work within Equity's jurisdiction.

**Source:** Actors' Equity, Open Access  
https://www.actorsequity.org/news/PR/2023/02/08/OpenAccessPermanently

Therefore Crew Blueprint should avoid presenting an Equity card as equivalent to an ETCP-style technical certification.

# Part II — Is production leadership track-specific or cross-cutting?

# A useful distinction: craft leadership vs. production leadership

The industry becomes much clearer if leadership is separated into two categories.

## A. Craft/department leadership

These roles normally require depth in the department being led.

Examples:

- Head Rigger
- Head Carpenter
- Audio Crew Chief / A1
- Head Electrician / Lighting Lead
- Video Lead / Video Crew Chief
- General Stagehand Crew Chief
- Production Stage Manager within the stage-management track

These are usually reached by progressing within that craft because the role is accountable for technical decisions, work quality, crew instruction, or department-specific execution.

IATSE's touring guide illustrates this directly: the Head Carpenter is the overall head of the touring technical show crew, while individual department heads maintain their own technical responsibilities, and stage management is a separate leadership function.

**Source:** IATSE Pink Contract Touring guide  
https://iatse.net/about/stagecraft/touring-2/

## B. Cross-cutting production/operations leadership

Production-management roles often sit above or across departments and can be entered from multiple backgrounds.

The strongest evidence comes from current job postings.

### The Public Theater — Production Manager

A current Production Manager posting requires at least five years of theatrical production experience with a strong background in **production management, stage management, and/or technical direction**. The role manages budgets, scheduling, build/load-in/tech/previews/load-out, production support, and communication across production departments.

**Source:** Playbill, The Public Theater Production Manager  
https://playbill.com/job/production-manager/f7e2fb27-7b32-401f-a7ad-255a9c125f5e

This is direct evidence that production management can recruit laterally from more than one discipline.

### New York City Children's Theater — Production Manager

A 2026 Production Manager posting states that experience as a **stage manager, theater designer, or stagehand** is preferred but not required. The role oversees technical/logistical production, scheduling, designers, technicians, load-ins, troubleshooting, and production calendars.

**Source:** Playbill, NYCT Production Manager, 2026  
https://playbill.com/job/production-manager/dd2e19f6-25c6-48c5-8803-e51178144727

This is especially useful for Crew Blueprint because it explicitly names three different starting points—stage management, design, and stagehand work—as relevant preparation for one coordination role.

### Another 2026 Production Manager posting

A current posting requires five years of professional experience in **production management, production stage management, technical direction, or a combination thereof**.

**Source:** Playbill Production Manager posting  
https://playbill.com/job/production-manager/7225d968-be1e-43fb-85d9-b659e7d6ea8c

Again, the employer treats multiple professional tracks as valid feeders into production management.

### Event Technical Services Manager

A current Event Technical Services Manager posting accepts demonstrated experience in **stage management or live-event production** and requires working knowledge of rigging, scenic construction, lighting, audio, backstage operations, and collaboration with touring productions, venue staff, and union stagehands.

**Source:** Playbill Event Technical Services Manager  
https://playbill.com/job/event-technical-services-manager/f9ae6897-5463-40d7-8806-3d871520cdcd

### Production/Technical Services Manager

Another current posting seeks five to seven years of experience in **performing-arts venue production, technical theatre operations, touring production management, or a combination of touring and venue-based production work**. The role requires broad knowledge across lighting, sound, rigging, stage operations, advancing, labor calls, and production scheduling.

**Source:** Playbill Production-Technical Services Manager  
https://playbill.com/job/production-technical-services-manager/e1a5731b-29df-4b27-a4d9-ba497e92ca68

### Associate Production Manager as an explicit transition role

Encore currently describes Associate Production Manager as an **introductory role into Production Management**. The position manages technical and logistical elements of smaller productions or portions of larger productions, monitors execution against plans, resolves/escalates operational problems, and works with Production Managers, Senior Production Managers, and Producers.

**Source:** Encore, Associate Production Manager, 2026  
https://jobs.encoreglobal.com/es/trabajo/-/-/6228/93189806240

This demonstrates that production management can itself become a separate ladder after a worker crosses into it.

# Real-world evidence that workers move across disciplines

Career biographies also show that real professionals frequently accumulate experience across more than one production discipline before moving into leadership.

IATSE International Vice President Damian Petti's published biography states that he worked as a **technical director, production stage manager, sound technician, lighting designer, and motion-picture grip** before later holding senior labor leadership positions.

**Source:** IATSE, Damian Petti biography  
https://iatse.net/leadership/damian-petti-2/

This should not be treated as proof that every worker follows such a path, but it is concrete evidence that cross-discipline movement is real rather than merely theoretical.

# What does NOT transfer automatically

Cross-cutting leadership does not mean all technical roles are interchangeable.

A strong stage manager does not automatically qualify as a head rigger or A1. A strong stagehand does not automatically qualify as a Production Stage Manager. A lighting technician does not automatically become competent to supervise audio engineering.

What transfers strongly into broader coordination roles includes:

- scheduling and sequencing;
- communication across departments;
- interpreting production needs;
- crew coordination;
- safety and escalation judgment;
- budgeting/resource awareness;
- documentation;
- logistics;
- advancing and planning;
- conflict resolution;
- understanding load-in, show, and load-out timelines;
- ability to communicate with designers, technicians, venue staff, vendors, and management.

What usually remains craft-specific includes:

- advanced rigging judgment;
- electrical qualification;
- audio system engineering;
- lighting programming/design competency;
- video engineering;
- structural/scenic technical authority;
- cue-calling and stage-management documentation conventions.

Therefore the leadership layer is **cross-cutting in management competency, not a waiver of craft competence**.

# Recommended Crew Blueprint career architecture

A more accurate model than one single ladder is a **matrix with vertical craft ladders and horizontal leadership transitions**.

## Craft tracks

### General hand

Stagehand → Proven Hand → Lead Hand/Crew Lead → Crew Chief

### Audio

Audio Assistant/A2 → independent audio technician/A1 or specialist branch → Audio Lead/Crew Chief

### Lighting/electrics

Lighting/Electrics technician → operator/programmer/electrician → Lead/Head Electrician/Lighting Crew Chief

### Video

Video technician → independent operator/engineer → Video Lead/Crew Chief

### Rigging

Ground Rigger → Up/High Rigger → Lead/Head Rigger

### Carpentry/staging

Carpenter/Stage Builder → senior/independent technician → Assistant/Lead/Head Carpenter or staging lead

### Stage management

Stage-management assistant/intern → ASM → SM → PSM

## Cross-cutting coordination track

Workers from several of those tracks may later transition toward:

Production Assistant / Production Coordinator → Assistant/Associate Production Manager → Production Manager → Senior Production Manager / Director of Production / Event Operations leadership

The exact titles vary significantly by employer, region, production type, and whether the work is theatre, concert touring, corporate AV, festival production, or venue operations.

---

# Important distinction between Crew Chief and Production Manager

Crew Chief should **not** be treated as simply a junior Production Manager in every context.

A Crew Chief is often responsible for the execution and supervision of a particular labor crew or technical department. Their authority is operational and close to the work.

A Production Manager typically coordinates **multiple departments, budgets, schedules, vendors, logistics, technical requirements, production calendars, staffing, and cross-functional execution**.

A worker can move from Crew Chief into Production Management, but Production Management can also be entered from stage management, technical direction, venue production, design, or other production-coordination backgrounds, as the job postings above demonstrate.

Likewise, a Production Stage Manager is not merely the stage-management version of a general technical Crew Chief. The PSM is responsible for stage-management leadership and performance/rehearsal coordination, while the technical leadership chain may separately include a Head Carpenter, department heads, crew chiefs, and production management.

---

# How this varies by production sector

## Broadway/regional theatre

The separation is usually clearest:

- AEA Stage Management team
- IATSE technical departments
- department heads/head carpenter
- production management
- company/general management

## Touring theatre

Stage management and technical touring leadership remain distinct but collaborate continuously. IATSE's Pink Contract guide explicitly separates PSM/stage-management responsibility from Head Carpenter/technical-crew authority.

## Concert/festival production

The title "stage manager" can sometimes mean something different from theatrical AEA stage management. Festival/concert stage managers may coordinate stage schedules, artist changeovers, local crews, backline, production traffic, and show timing rather than maintain a theatrical prompt book or manage actors through rehearsals.

Therefore Crew Blueprint should identify **theatrical stage management** and **concert/festival stage management** as related but not identical occupational contexts.

## Corporate AV / meetings and events

The formal theatrical ASM→SM→PSM ladder is less dominant. Production Coordinator, Technical Director, Producer, Show Caller, Event Manager, Operations Manager, and Production Manager may perform overlapping coordination functions depending on company structure.

This is a major area where title language becomes employer-specific.

---

# Bottom line

1. **Stage management is unquestionably a distinct career discipline in U.S. professional theatre.** It has its own union representation through Actors' Equity, professional association through SMA, dedicated job market, training infrastructure, specialist terminology, and internal progression.

2. **Stage management is separate from stagehand and technical-department progression.** It collaborates with those tracks rather than simply sitting above them.

3. **There is no universal national stage-manager certification comparable to ETCP.** AEA membership is union membership, not a competency credential. Training occurs through theatre programs, mentorship, USITT/SMA professional development, and work experience.

4. **Craft leadership is usually track-specific.** Head Rigger, Audio Lead, Head Carpenter, and similar roles normally require deep craft competence.

5. **Production management/coordination is genuinely cross-cutting.** Current employers explicitly recruit candidates from stage management, production management, technical direction, stagehand work, design, venue operations, touring production, and combinations of these backgrounds.

6. **The industry does not support one universal ladder.** The most accurate model is multiple craft ladders feeding into an optional cross-cutting production/operations leadership layer.

7. **For Crew Blueprint:** represent leadership as both vertical and horizontal. Workers can become senior within their craft, or—after building broad production, communication, logistics, and management competence—transition into coordination and production-management roles that sit across crafts.
