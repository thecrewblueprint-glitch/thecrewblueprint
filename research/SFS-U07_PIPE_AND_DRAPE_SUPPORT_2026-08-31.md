# SFS-U07 — Pipe-and-Drape Support

**Prepared:** 2026-08-31  
**Status:** Source packet complete enough for bounded initial drafting; exact-system/manufacturer review required  
**Domain:** Stagehand/general-hand practical evidence  
**Course-format decision:** Deferred

## Research question

What can The Crew Blueprint responsibly teach a new stagehand about pipe-and-drape component recognition, handling, staging and lead-directed setup support without converting one manufacturer's assembly sequence into a universal rule or implying structural/public-safety acceptance authority?

## Bottom-line finding

Pipe-and-drape is ordinary early-career event/stagehand material in many corporate, convention, venue and event environments. A beginner can be taught to recognize the component families, protect drape, stage components safely, confirm the exact system and layout, work under the designated lead/manufacturer plan, keep public/egress routes clear, and stop when the base/upright/support combination, height, load, stability or site condition is unfamiliar.

The exact assembly sequence and allowable configuration are **system-specific**. Generic training should therefore teach recognition, preparation, handling, boundaries and verification—not one universal structural recipe.

## Evidence

### 1. Pipe-and-drape uses recognizable component families

Georgia Expo's current setup guidance shows a common system built from weighted bases with pins, uprights, drape supports/crossbars and drape. This validates the component-family model while remaining manufacturer-specific.

### 2. Generic component recognition does not establish universal interchangeability

Manufacturer systems can differ in:

- base size/weight;
- pin/base connection;
- upright diameter and height range;
- telescoping locks;
- drape-support/crossbar attachment;
- spans and allowable configurations;
- drape weight;
- stabilization/ballast requirements;
- public-contact/site restrictions.

Curriculum implication:

Teach the learner to identify **the actual system** and follow the responsible lead/manufacturer plan. Do not imply that visibly similar uprights, bases or crossbars are automatically compatible.

### 3. Drape care and flame-retardancy remain separate evidence layers

Pipe-and-drape work often includes fabric handling. Rose Brand evidence supports protecting theatrical/event drape from contamination/crushing and treating flame-retardancy documentation as a jurisdiction/authority issue rather than a beginner visual judgment.

### 4. Public-route and stability consequences raise the acceptance boundary

A freestanding divider can affect walking routes, audience/public areas and emergency access. The general Stagehand curriculum already has evidence that aisles/passages/egress and public-route conditions require protection and escalation.

Curriculum implication:

A new hand may carry, stage and assemble assigned components under direction. The learner should not independently decide that a completed divider is stable, appropriately ballasted, code-compliant or acceptable for public occupancy when that acceptance belongs to a lead, venue, manufacturer, competent person or AHJ.

## Transferable learner outcome

A bounded learner can be prepared to:

1. recognize common pipe-and-drape component categories;
2. identify the manufacturer/system/model where possible;
3. confirm the layout, destination and responsible lead;
4. stage bases/uprights/supports/drape without blocking egress or active routes;
5. protect drape from unnecessary contamination or crushing;
6. inspect for obvious damaged/missing/mismatched components and report them;
7. support assembly using the exact system instructions/lead direction;
8. verify the task back to the responsible lead rather than self-accepting the final installation;
9. stop if height, span, ballast/stability, public contact, outdoor/weather exposure, damaged components or an unfamiliar system creates uncertainty.

## What the evidence does not support as universal

Do not teach:

- one base weight for all systems;
- one span/height limit;
- one ballast formula;
- one universal order of assembly;
- one universal drape fullness/orientation rule;
- mixing brands/components without manufacturer approval;
- improvised ballast;
- structural/public-safety acceptance by the learner;
- outdoor/wind suitability from generic indoor instructions.

## Visual evidence needs

Future learner material should use reviewed visuals for:

- base/pin/upright/crossbar/drape component recognition;
- matched versus visibly mismatched/damaged components;
- staging that keeps routes clear;
- clean/protected drape handling;
- decision tree: ordinary directed support vs stop/escalate for stability/height/outdoor/public-route issues.

Product-specific diagrams should identify manufacturer/model/source and must not be generalized beyond that system.
