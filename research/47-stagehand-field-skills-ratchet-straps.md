# Research Package 47 — Stagehand Field Skills: Ratchet Straps

**RQ item:** RQ-SFS-02  
**Research status:** Source packet complete; practitioner review required  
**Prepared:** 2026-08-30  
**Use:** Evidence package for entry-level assistance with ratchet straps. It does not establish cargo-securement authority.

## Decision question

What can Crew Blueprint safely teach a stagehand about inspecting, threading, tensioning, locking, releasing, and storing a ratchet strap while keeping anchor selection, routing, suitability, final tension, and acceptance under the responsible lead?

## Bottom-line finding

A beginner can be taught the parts and controlled mechanical sequence of a ratchet strap. The course must not teach “a ratchet strap” as a single universal capacity or assume that knowing the mechanism qualifies someone to design a securement system.

Kinedyne provides manufacturer education on ratchet-strap components, selecting straps by working load limit, and threading webbing. Federal cargo-securement rules add legally important controls when the strap is used on a commercial motor vehicle: securement devices must be in proper working order, web tiedowns must conform to the incorporated standard, knots are prohibited, tiedowns must be secured against release, and edge protection is required where cargo could abrade or cut the tiedown.

For event work outside a regulated commercial-motor-vehicle application, the specific law may differ, but the manufacturer's label, instructions, condition limits, end fittings, and intended use still control.

## Scope distinction

This packet covers the hand skill of operating a ratchet strap **after a responsible lead has selected the strap, anchors, route, edge protection, and desired tension**.

It does not teach:

- flatbed load-plan design;
- calculating the number or aggregate working load limit of tiedowns;
- choosing an anchor point from appearance alone;
- substituting a cargo tiedown for lifting, fall protection, towing, rigging, or personnel restraint;
- securing a commercial load without driver/qualified load-lead acceptance;
- repairing webbing, stitching, end fittings, or a ratchet mechanism.

Flatbed cargo securement is addressed separately in Package 48.

## Source findings

### 1. Ratchet straps are assemblies, not generic rope

The working assembly includes webbing, fixed and adjustable ends, a ratchet/tensioning mechanism, mandrel or spool, handle, release/pawl system, stitching, label, and end fittings. Product dimensions, fittings, construction, and working load limits vary.

Kinedyne identifies ratchet straps as cargo-control products and emphasizes selection by the labeled safe working load limit.

Sources:

- https://kinedyne.com/ratchet-tie-down-winch-straps/
- https://kinedyne.com/ratchet-buckles/
- https://kinedyne.com/flatbed-trailer-products/ratchet-straps/

### 2. Threading and operation must match the actual mechanism

Kinedyne's education library includes a specific demonstration for threading webbing through ratchet straps and other buckles. Because mechanisms and release designs vary, Crew Blueprint should build the hand sequence around the actual strap used in the video and state that the manufacturer instructions control.

Source: https://kinedyne.com/201-cargo-securement-education/

A manufacturer's general sequence commonly involves opening the mechanism, feeding the adjustable webbing through the mandrel slot in the specified direction, pulling most slack through by hand, ratcheting to the assigned tension, and closing the handle into its locked position. Release requires controlling stored tension, operating the model's release, opening the mechanism fully, and removing webbing without placing hands in pinch points.

That sequence must be demonstrated and approved on the exact mechanism rather than taught solely from text.

### 3. Condition and labeling matter

For commercial-motor-vehicle cargo securement, 49 CFR 393.104 prohibits damaged or weakened tiedowns and components that adversely affect performance or reduce working load limit. It also requires vehicle structures and anchor points used in securement to be strong enough and undamaged.

Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-393/subpart-I/section-393.104

The entry-level course should therefore teach a conservative pre-use escalation screen:

- missing or unreadable identification/working-load label;
- cuts, tears, holes, broken or visibly damaged fibers;
- heavy abrasion, edge wear, crushing, knots, burns, melting, chemical attack, or contamination;
- pulled or damaged stitching;
- bent, cracked, spread, corroded, or distorted end fittings;
- a ratchet that is bent, cracked, heavily corroded, jammed, missing parts, or fails to lock/release normally;
- incompatible, damaged, or uncertain anchor points.

The learner removes the item from the immediate task and gives it to the responsible lead. The learner does not decide to repair, shorten, relabel, or return it to service.

### 4. Knots, release security, and edge protection are not optional details

For CMV cargo tiedowns, 49 CFR 393.104 states:

- tiedowns and securing devices must not contain knots;
- repaired tiedowns must meet the incorporated standard or manufacturer instructions;
- a tiedown must be attached and secured so it cannot loosen, unfasten, open, or release in transit;
- edge protection must be used when cargo could abrade or cut the tiedown, and the protection must resist abrasion, cutting, and crushing.

Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-393/subpart-I/section-393.104

These are strong reasons to teach a beginner not to improvise routing or use a knot to “fix” excess webbing or a damaged strap.

## Appropriate learner outcome

After supervised instruction on a specific strap, the learner should be able to:

- name the major parts;
- locate and read the label without converting it into an independent load-plan decision;
- perform the conservative inspection screen;
- thread the webbing in the model-correct direction;
- remove slack before ratcheting;
- operate the handle while keeping fingers and body out of pinch/line-of-fire zones;
- stop at the tension directed by the lead;
- close and confirm the mechanism is locked;
- control the release and stored tension;
- remove, roll, and store the strap without knots or new damage;
- stop and ask when suitability, anchors, routing, capacity, edge protection, condition, or final tension is unclear.

## Recommended supervised sequence

### 1. Confirm the plan

The lead identifies:

- the exact strap;
- both approved connection points;
- route and orientation;
- required edge protection;
- desired tension and sequence relative to other straps;
- who performs the final inspection.

The learner repeats the plan before attaching anything.

### 2. Inspect the complete assembly

Lay out enough of the strap to see both ends, label, webbing, stitching, hardware, and mechanism. Escalate any listed defect. Do not use a strap whose identity, capacity, or condition is uncertain.

### 3. Attach only to assigned points

Connect the fixed and adjustable ends exactly where directed. Confirm each end fitting is seated in the manner shown by the lead/manufacturer. Do not hook to sheet metal, trim, handles, guardrails, case hardware, unknown structure, or another strap unless the responsible plan expressly calls for it.

### 4. Route and protect

Keep the webbing flat unless the product instructions say otherwise. Avoid knots and unintended twists. Install the specified edge protection. Keep the strap away from heat, chemicals, moving parts, doors, tires, and sharp edges.

### 5. Thread and remove slack

Open the ratchet to the demonstrated position. Feed the free end through the mandrel in the demonstrated direction. Pull most slack through by hand before operating the handle. Excess material on the mandrel can prevent correct operation; the acceptable wrap range is model-specific and must come from the instructor/manufacturer.

### 6. Tension and lock

Stand in the position directed by the lead, with stable footing and no person in the strap's line of action. Operate the handle smoothly. Stop at the directed tension; do not add a pipe, bar, tool, body weight, or second person to increase leverage unless the manufacturer and qualified plan expressly call for that method. Close the handle into the locked position and ask for inspection.

### 7. Release under control

Confirm the load cannot shift when tension comes off and that no person is in the release path. Hold the mechanism, operate the release as demonstrated, open it fully, and let tension off in a controlled manner. Do not stand where hardware or cargo can spring, swing, roll, or fall toward the body.

### 8. Store

Remove debris, roll or fold according to company practice, protect the label and mechanism, and place rejected equipment in the location designated by the lead.

## Common errors

| Error | Why it matters | Correct response |
|---|---|---|
| Choosing an anchor because it “looks strong” | Appearance does not establish capacity or suitability | Use only the lead-approved point |
| Ratcheting before most slack is removed | Can overfill or bind the mechanism | Release and rethread under direction |
| Webbing is knotted, twisted, or routed across a sharp edge | Can reduce or compromise performance | Stop; reroute/protect as directed |
| Continuing after the load or structure begins to deform | Tension may be excessive or the plan wrong | Stop immediately and notify the lead |
| Fingers enter the spool/pawl area | Pinch/crush risk | Reposition hands and use the demonstrated grip |
| Release begins before the load is stable | Stored force or cargo movement can injure crew | Obtain lead confirmation and clear the area |
| Missing label or visible damage is ignored | Suitability and capacity cannot be established | Remove from task and escalate |

## Visual and video plan

Create model-specific original assets:

1. labeled ratchet-strap assembly;
2. acceptable versus escalate inspection examples;
3. webbing entering the mandrel in the correct direction;
4. slack removal before ratcheting;
5. safe hand zones and pinch zones;
6. locked handle confirmation;
7. controlled release with a clear line of fire;
8. wrong-anchor, sharp-edge, knot, twist, and overfilled-mandrel scenarios.

Manufacturer videos may be linked as references, but the course must remain complete without them. Final course footage should use the actual strap and anchors chosen by the reviewing lead.

## Assessment and practical verification

Knowledge questions should test:

- missing label;
- damaged webbing;
- a lead changing the anchor plan;
- sharp-edge routing;
- a mechanism that will not lock;
- a load that moves during tensioning;
- pressure to add leverage.

Practical observation should require the learner to inspect, thread, remove slack, tension to a reviewer-defined stop point, lock, release, and store one exact strap.

Record:

- knowledge completed;
- practice observed on identified strap/model;
- lead authorization, if any, as a separate employer-controlled state.

## Authority boundary

The responsible lead selects the strap, anchors, route, edge protection, sequence, tension, and final acceptance. The learner operates only within that plan. For vehicle cargo, the driver/motor carrier or qualified load lead retains the applicable responsibility described in Package 48. Nothing in this packet authorizes lifting, rigging, fall protection, towing, recovery, or personnel restraint.

## Review required before curriculum handoff

- Experienced production/truck lead review of hand sequence.
- Driver or qualified cargo-securement reviewer for any vehicle example.
- Manufacturer-instruction match for the exact strap filmed.
- Owner review for event-work authenticity.
- Novice learner test focused on stop/ask judgment.
