# O01 — Arrival / Check-In / Reporting Point

## Evidence finding

Multiple current IATSE local sources explicitly require workers to arrive before call time, check in with the steward or lead, and report to the assigned department/head.

Examples:

- Local 193: arrive before official call time; check in with the job steward; check/sign out with the steward at the end.
- Local 25: be at the work site before call time; check in with the job steward; do not leave without checking with the steward.
- Local 98: immediately inform the Job Steward of your presence, complete required paperwork, ask who the department head is, and report to that person.
- Local 101: report first to the Job Steward/Leadman and then to the Department Head.

## Transferable instructional conclusion

It is defensible to teach:

> **Call time means ready to work through the controlling check-in process, not merely arriving at the property. Find the person/process controlling the call, check in, confirm your assigned group/department, and report where directed.**

## Do not universalize

Do not teach one mandatory number of minutes early nationally. Sources vary (e.g. 10 vs 15 minutes), and employer/CBA/venue rules may supersede local examples.

# O02 — Assignment Ownership / Who Directs the Work

## Evidence finding

Local 101 states that the Job Steward/Leadman makes work assignments according to production needs and worker ability; workers report to assigned department heads and remain in their assigned department unless the appropriate chain of command changes the assignment.

IATSE touring guidance distinguishes touring heads, house/local heads, stewards, local crews, and other responsible roles rather than implying that any person on a jobsite can direct any worker.

Existing Crew Blueprint Package 15 already identified a key correction: a touring worker is not automatically the direct supervisor of local labor; the controlling call structure matters.

## Transferable instructional conclusion

Teach:

- identify who owns your assignment;
- know your department/work group;
- if conflicting instructions arrive, route them through the established lead/steward/head rather than silently changing departments;
- do not self-assign technical or show-critical work merely because it is visible.

A useful Crew Blueprint framework is:

> **Who gave me the task? What am I doing? Where does it go? Who owns the result? What makes me stop and ask?**

The checklist is a Crew Blueprint teaching tool, not a union rule.

## Evidence state

`draftable`

# O03 — Task Completion / Report-Back / Handoff

## Evidence finding

Current local rules repeatedly support reporting to department heads/stewards, staying with assigned work, and obtaining release before leaving a department or call.

Local 193 specifically states that after performance duties, workers should make sure the department head has released them before walking away. Local 101 requires workers to remain available/present with the assigned department and regulates leaving departments or calls without notification.

Existing SFS-U04 and SFS-U05 evidence separately establishes work-area organization, reset, and responsible handoff.

## Transferable instructional conclusion

Teach that **physical motion completed** is not always the same as **assignment accepted as complete**.

A learner should be prepared to:

1. compare the result to the stated destination/condition;
2. report missing/damaged/unclear conditions;
3. return control to the responsible person when acceptance belongs to them;
4. reset borrowed tools/materials/work area as assigned;
5. report back rather than silently disappearing from the task.

## Evidence state

`draftable`

# O04 — Reassignment / Find the Next Legitimate Task

## Evidence finding

The exact phrase “find another task” is not a national standard, but the underlying work pattern is strongly supported:

- Local 101 expects workers to remain with their assigned department and do an equal share of work.
- Local 193 states that during load-out, a worker released from a department may become a pusher unless told otherwise, illustrating reassignment after one work package ends.
- Local 98 tells workers to assume an out call continues until the work is finished.

## Transferable instructional conclusion

The strongest defensible teaching statement is:

> **When your assigned work is actually complete, return to the established source of work — your lead, steward, department head, crew chief, or other call-specific process — and make yourself available for the next assignment.**

Do not teach learners to:

- wander into another department;
- invent unauthorized work;
- touch technical/show-critical systems because they appear idle;
- leave because the first task ended.

# O05 — Standby / Downtime / Break / Meal Distinctions

## Industry-practice evidence

Local 193 explicitly warns workers not to assume meals are provided, not to assume they will be cut for an hour, and not to assume a short or long workday. Its show-call guidance notes that an intermission does not guarantee a break and that show duties may continue.

Local 101 requires workers to inform the Department Head/Job Steward before coffee, restroom, lunch, or leaving a call. That is a **local work rule**, not a national legal rule.

## Federal wage/hour evidence

DOL Fact Sheet #22 distinguishes:

- **engaged to wait** — generally work time when the employee remains controlled/on duty;
- **waiting to be engaged** — may be non-work time depending on circumstances;
- short rest periods, usually 20 minutes or less — generally counted as work time under the FLSA;
- bona fide meal periods — generally need not be compensated if the employee is completely relieved of duty.

Source:  
https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/22-flsa-hours-worked

## Critical curriculum boundary

Crew Blueprint should **not** teach “downtime is your free break” or “all waiting is unpaid.”

Instead teach the operational distinction:

- **task complete / awaiting reassignment**;
- **production dependency or hold**;
- **standby / show waiting**;
- **authorized short break**;
- **meal period**;
- **actual release**.

What counts as paid time depends on employment status, facts, contract/CBA, employer practice, and jurisdiction. The site should provide federal educational context and direct learners to the controlling employer/local/venue/jurisdiction for their actual situation.

`draftable with legal disclaimer and jurisdiction/CBA qualifier`

# O06 — Show-Call / Changeover / Performance-Rhythm Behavior

## Evidence finding

Local 193 treats show calls as timing-sensitive work that can directly affect the performance. It describes pre-show preparation, cue/time calls, presets, work during intermission, post-show duties, and department-head release.

IATSE International touring material distinguishes loading in, running the show, and loading out as separate operational phases.

## Transferable beginner learning

Teach:

- show-call work is not simply load-in with an audience present;
- timing, cues, quiet/clear backstage behavior, presets, changeovers, performer routes, and show-critical boundaries can matter;
- be at the assigned place when required;
- remain available during waiting periods;
- do not touch or alter show-critical equipment/props/scenery without assignment;
- confirm release after assigned show duties.

## Do not universalize

- exact cue language across every production;
- exact show-call minimums/pay guarantees;
- universal intermission break practices;
- universal continuity/preset procedures;
- universal dress requirements beyond the controlling production/venue instructions.

## Evidence state

`draftable with production-specific qualifier`

# O07 — Department Release / Call Release / Leaving the Worksite

## Evidence finding

The difference between finishing one task and being released from the call is strongly supported.

- Local 25: do not leave the job without checking with the steward.
- Local 193: check/sign out with the steward; after show duties ensure the department head released you; during load-out, department release may lead to reassignment as a pusher.
- Local 101: workers must check in/sign out as required and must not leave a department or call without notifying the appropriate authority.

## Transferable instructional conclusion

Teach three distinct states:

1. **Task done** — one assignment is finished.
2. **Department/work-group released** — that group no longer needs the worker, but the call may continue or reassignment may occur.
3. **Call released** — the controlling authority has released the worker from work.

A learner should not infer state 3 from state 1.

## Evidence state

`draftable`

# O08 — Timekeeping / Clock-Out / Pay-Record Boundary

## Evidence finding

Local 193 explicitly connects sign-out with pay accuracy. Local 98 tells workers to complete required paperwork to get paid. Local 101 uses check-in/sign-out and written work-call records.

Federal DOL Fact Sheet #21 states that covered employers must keep accurate records of hours worked and wages; no single timekeeping method is required so long as records are complete and accurate.

Source:  
https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/21-flsa-recordkeeping

## Transferable instructional conclusion

Crew Blueprint can teach:

- know the employer/local/venue timekeeping method for the call;
- complete sign-in/sign-out/timecard/app/paperwork accurately;
- do not assume physical departure automatically records the correct end time;
- retain/verify personal work records where appropriate;
- promptly raise discrepancies through the controlling payroll/steward/employer process.

## Critical boundary

The site should not become a payroll-law calculator or claim every user is legally an employee. Live-event workers may have different employment/contractor arrangements and state/local rules. Federal FLSA educational content must be clearly scoped to covered employment relationships.

## Evidence state

`draftable with worker-classification/jurisdiction qualifier`

# Cross-source synthesis: the defensible work rhythm

Across multiple current IATSE sources, a recurring operational pattern emerges:

> **Check in → report to the assigned authority → work the assigned department/task → report/hand off → remain available for the next assigned work → follow the controlling break/standby process → continue until released → complete timekeeping/sign-out.**

This is a **cross-source industry pattern**, not one national contract clause.

The evidence does **not** support teaching either extreme:

- “A professional stagehand must look busy every second,” or
- “Once your task is done, the rest of the call is free time.”

The stronger formulation is:

> **Do available assigned work well. When the assignment ends, report back and remain appropriately available. Production work naturally includes dependencies, holds, standby, show waiting, and authorized breaks. The controlling call determines when you are actually released.**

1. Label local-union procedures as examples/local rules unless corroborated as broader patterns.
2. Do not convert local break/pay minimums into national rules.
3. Separate federal wage/hour facts from union/employer work etiquette.
4. Do not promise callbacks or promotion for good behavior.
5. Do not imply the course can authorize a learner to cross department/specialist boundaries.
6. Keep worker classification visible when discussing FLSA rights/records.
