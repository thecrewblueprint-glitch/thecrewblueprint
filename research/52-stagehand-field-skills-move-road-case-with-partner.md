# Research Package 52 — Move a Road Case With a Partner

**Research date:** 2026-08-31  
**Status:** Source pack complete; owner field review remains for explicitly flagged variable/unverified practices  
**Downstream use:** First task-first Stagehand Field Skill  
**Course boundary:** Online explanation and demonstration do not establish practical competency or employer authorization.

## Research question

What can The Crew Blueprint responsibly teach a new stagehand about moving a wheeled road case with a partner, and which parts must remain equipment-, route-, employer-, venue-, or crew-specific?

## Evidence labels

- **Authoritative** — government occupational-safety guidance.
- **Industry-primary** — a union/local, employer, or equipment manufacturer describing its own equipment or practice.
- **Industry-secondary** — an identifiable industry publication or practitioner.
- **Public — unverified** — worker discussion, anonymous account, or informal public explanation.
- **Cross-source pattern** — multiple sources support the same operating idea without establishing one universal live-event rule.
- **Crew Blueprint instructional rule** — a bounded teaching choice derived from evidence; not represented as a national standard.
- **Owner-confirmed field practice** — reserved for claims the owner later confirms.
- **Unresolved** — evidence does not support a reliable instruction yet.

## Executive finding

The course can teach a safe, transferable movement pattern:

1. inspect the case and route;
2. agree on roles, destination, pace, and one stop command;
3. move at walking speed with clear sightlines;
4. slow or stop before doors, corners, thresholds, ramps, slopes, traffic, or other changed conditions;
5. keep hands, feet, and the body out of pinch/crush paths;
6. park only where directed and secure the case using the equipment actually provided;
7. stop and ask when the case, route, load, or crew plan does not match the demonstration.

No reliable national live-event source establishes one universal pusher/spotter arrangement, one required word for stopping, one fixed person-count threshold, or one caster-end rule for every road case. The course must treat those as assigned or agreed practices rather than universal rules.

## Claim register

### 1. Push rather than pull when conditions allow

**Classification:** Authoritative general ergonomic guidance; not an absolute road-case rule.

OSHA says pushing generally requires less effort than pulling and reduces the chance of a cart running into the worker's shins or ankles. HSE likewise advises pushing wheeled cages where possible. CCOHS recommends enough aisle width for the worker to stand behind and push the cart.

**Course use:** Start from a controlled push. Pull only when the route, door, alignment, case design, or responsible lead requires it. Do not turn “push where possible” into “never pull.”

Sources:

- OSHA, [Materials Handling — Pushing, Pulling and Carrying](https://www.osha.gov/etools/electrical-contractors/materials-handling/pushing)
- HSE, [Handling roll cages, wheeled racks and other heavy units](https://www.hse.gov.uk/food/safety-hazards/roll-cages-wheeled-racks.htm)
- CCOHS, [Pushing and Pulling — Handcarts](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/ergonomics/push2.html)

### 2. Inspect the route before moving

**Classification:** Authoritative.

HSE identifies load weight/stability, sightlines, route width, corners, rough surfaces, slopes, wheel/caster condition, and brake effectiveness as push/pull risk factors. Rhino Staging instructs stagehands to check up, down, left, right, and behind before moving through a production workspace.

**Course use:** Before movement, check the full route, not just the first few feet. Look for people, moving equipment, cables, debris, liquid, stage edges, holes, door swings, low overhead clearance, narrow turns, thresholds, dock plates, ramps, and the assigned parking location.

Sources:

- HSE, [Risk factors associated with pushing and pulling loads](https://www.hse.gov.uk/msd/pushpull/risks.htm)
- HSE, [Doing a push/pull risk assessment](https://www.hse.gov.uk/msd/pushpull/assessment.htm)
- Rhino Staging, [Enhancing Situational Awareness as a Stagehand](https://www.rhinostaging.com/incidentpreventionenhancingsituationalawarenessasastagehand/)

### 3. Inspect the case before moving

**Classification:** Cross-source pattern supported by authoritative maintenance guidance, industry equipment guidance, and manufacturer specifications.

HSE says pushing/pulling equipment, including wheels, needs planned maintenance and a defect-reporting system. IATSE Local 15 warns that cable caddies can be extremely heavy, tall gondola cases can be top-heavy, and stacked cases create wheel-pocket and finger hazards. Penn Elcom documents that road-case casters vary by type, brake design, material, and load rating.

**Course use:** Check:

- casters roll and swivel as expected;
- brakes fully release before movement and hold when tested for parking;
- handles remain secure and usable;
- latches sit closed or fully stowed;
- lids, drawers, doors, and loose parts cannot open or fall;
- stacked cases sit correctly in their wheel pockets or stacking features;
- the case does not lean, rack, or carry an unstable/top-heavy load;
- labels or lead direction identify the destination.

Report a defect instead of forcing the case to move.

Sources:

- HSE, [Manual handling guidance (L23), equipment maintenance discussion](https://www.hse.gov.uk/PuBns/priced/l23.pdf)
- IATSE Local 15, [Entry Study Guide — Equipment](https://ia15.org/docs/entry-study-guide/equipment-2/)
- Penn Elcom, [Road-case casters and wheels](https://www.penn-elcom.com/us/flight-case-hardware/castors-wheels?view_all=true)
- Penn Elcom, [Example total-lock road-case caster](https://www.penn-elcom.com/us/4-total-lock-braked-heavy-duty-swivel-caster-with-rubber-blue-wheel-w9007-hd)

### 4. Use help based on control and conditions, not a universal weight number

**Classification:** Authoritative general guidance plus Crew Blueprint instructional rule.

HSE advises workers to seek help on ramps and uneven surfaces. Its simple risk filter treats a wheeled load as low risk only when the worker can move and control it very easily. OSHA presents roughly 50 pounds of required push/pull force as an ergonomic screening point for considering mechanical assistance; it is not a universal case-weight limit or a stagehand rule.

**Course use:** Use a partner or mechanical help when:

- the responsible lead assigns two or more people;
- one worker cannot start, steer, stop, or park the case smoothly;
- the case blocks the travel view;
- the route includes a ramp, slope, uneven surface, difficult threshold, or tight public/work traffic;
- the case is tall, top-heavy, stacked, unusually large, fragile, or unfamiliar;
- the consequences of losing control are serious.

Do not publish one case-weight cutoff.

Sources:

- HSE, [Handling roll cages, wheeled racks and other heavy units](https://www.hse.gov.uk/food/safety-hazards/roll-cages-wheeled-racks.htm)
- HSE, [Simple manual-handling risk filters](https://www.hse.gov.uk/msd/manual-handling-risk-filters.htm)
- OSHA, [Back-task solutions: pushing and pulling carts](https://www.osha.gov/etools/electrical-contractors/supplemental-information/solutions/tasks-back)

### 5. Move no faster than walking speed

**Classification:** Authoritative for comparable heavy wheeled units; Crew Blueprint applies it conservatively to road cases.

HSE tells workers moving wheeled cages to move no faster than walking speed. Starting, stopping, turning, poor footing, pace, and route condition increase risk.

**Course use:** Walk the case. Do not run it. Slow before every transition, traffic conflict, blind corner, or change in surface.

Sources:

- HSE, [Handling roll cages, wheeled racks and other heavy units](https://www.hse.gov.uk/food/safety-hazards/roll-cages-wheeled-racks.htm)
- CCOHS, [Pushing and Pulling — Handcarts](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/ergonomics/push2.html)

### 6. Protect hands and feet from pinch/crush paths

**Classification:** Industry-primary and industry-secondary, consistent with general material-handling risk.

IATSE Local 15 explicitly says never place fingers between a wheel and wheel pocket and warns about crush zones on specialized carts. ProSoundWeb identifies pinched hands between road cases and objects falling on feet as common stagehand injuries.

**Course use:**

- use the designed handles where they give control;
- do not place fingers between stacked cases, wheel pockets, the case and a wall/doorframe, or two cases closing together;
- do not put a foot downhill, under a caster, or in the intended travel path;
- do not try to catch a tipping or runaway heavy case with the body;
- call the transition before hands or feet enter a tight area.

Sources:

- IATSE Local 15, [Entry Study Guide — Equipment](https://ia15.org/docs/entry-study-guide/equipment-2/)
- ProSoundWeb, [Priorities in Order: A Primer on Safety for Techs and Stagehands](https://www.prosoundweb.com/priorities-in-order-a-primer-on-safety-for-techs-stagehands/)

### 7. Doors require a separate control plan

**Classification:** Authoritative general cart guidance.

CCOHS warns that pulling a door while holding or resisting a rolling cart increases injury risk.

**Course use:** Stop before the door. Decide who controls the door and who controls the case. Do not ask one person to fight the door and the rolling case at the same time. Recheck clearance and the far side before movement resumes.

Source:

- CCOHS, [Pushing and Pulling — Handcarts](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/ergonomics/push2.html)

### 8. Ramps, slopes, dock plates, and uneven surfaces change the task

**Classification:** Authoritative general guidance.

HSE says ramps, slopes, poor caster alignment, poor maintenance, and uneven/soft surfaces increase required force. It advises seeking help on ramps and uneven surfaces. CCOHS recommends powered assistance or winches for large ramps, brakes for better control, and a flat runout area.

**Course use:** Treat a ramp or slope as a new movement, not a continuation of flat-floor travel:

- stop before entering;
- confirm the route, people, roles, and stop command;
- use the number of people or mechanical aid assigned for the case and ramp;
- keep the case straight unless the site-specific plan requires otherwise;
- move slowly and maintain control through the transition;
- stop if wheels lift, bind, turn sideways, accelerate beyond control, or leave the usable surface;
- never improvise a descent when the crew cannot control the load.

Sources:

- HSE, [Doing a push/pull risk assessment](https://www.hse.gov.uk/msd/pushpull/assessment.htm)
- HSE, [Handling roll cages, wheeled racks and other heavy units](https://www.hse.gov.uk/food/safety-hazards/roll-cages-wheeled-racks.htm)
- CCOHS, [Pushing and Pulling — Handcarts](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/ergonomics/push2.html)

### 9. Caster layout changes steering behavior

**Classification:** Manufacturer-supported equipment fact plus authoritative general guidance; exact road-case handling remains equipment-specific.

Road cases can use fixed, swivel, braked-swivel, and total-lock casters. CCOHS recommends swivel casters on the handle end of a cart. Penn Elcom documents matching fixed and swivel road-case casters as well as all-swivel configurations.

**Course use:** Identify the actual caster layout before teaching which end steers. Do not claim that every road case has a fixed end or that one end always leads. If the case has mixed fixed/swivel casters, the responsible lead or equipment design controls travel orientation. Demonstrate the exact case used in the video.

Sources:

- CCOHS, [Pushing and Pulling — Handcarts](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/ergonomics/push2.html)
- Penn Elcom, [Road-case casters and wheels](https://www.penn-elcom.com/us/flight-case-hardware/castors-wheels?view_all=true)

### 10. Agree on roles and a stop command before moving

**Classification:** Cross-source safety pattern; exact live-event words are variable.

Authoritative vehicle/handling guidance supports a spotter when sightlines fail and continuous communication between the mover/operator and spotter. Public stagehand discussions report both “stop” and “hold,” plus transition warnings such as “fingers and toes.” Those worker accounts do not establish universal vocabulary.

**Crew Blueprint instructional rule:**

- one person controls the movement;
- the partner watches the obstructed side, route, traffic, clearances, and transitions;
- both people repeat the destination and agree on one stop word before moving;
- either person can call the stop word;
- the case stops immediately;
- movement resumes only after both people understand the next action.

The course should say that crews may use **stop**, **hold**, or another established call. The shared meaning matters more than pretending one word is universal.

Sources:

- CCOHS, [Forklift control guidance — spotter and communication principle](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/forklift/control.html)
- Public — unverified: [Reddit stagehand injury-prevention discussion](https://www.reddit.com/r/stagehands/comments/1kzfwgb/injury_prevention/)

These claims appear useful but lack strong direct documentation for road-case movement:

| “Hold” is the standard live-event stop command | Multiple stagehand comments, with regional disagreement | Teach an agreed stop word; do not call one word universal |
| Call “fingers and toes” before a tight transition | Stagehand worker account | Candidate communication example |

## Proposed task sequence

This is a **Crew Blueprint instructional sequence**, not an asserted national standard.

1. **Stop and inspect.** Check the case, load, casters, brakes, handles, latches, lids, stacking, balance, label, and route.
2. **Make the plan.** Confirm destination, assigned roles, path, pace, transitions, and the stop word.
3. **Set the case.** Release approved brakes, place hands on usable handles/control points, and keep feet clear.
4. **Start together.** One person calls the move. Apply force smoothly instead of jerking the case.
5. **Walk and communicate.** Maintain sightlines, watch the far side and traffic, and slow before turns or surface changes.
6. **Reset at every transition.** Stop for doors, blind corners, thresholds, dock plates, ramps, slopes, congestion, or lost communication.
7. **Park and report.** Place the case where directed, square it with the staging plan, use its approved brake/chock method, keep paths clear, and tell the lead the move is complete.

## Stop-and-ask conditions

Stop and get the lead or another qualified person when:

- you do not know the destination or route;
- the case is unmarked, unfamiliar, open, damaged, unstable, top-heavy, or stacked incorrectly;
- a wheel, swivel, brake, handle, latch, lid, drawer, or door does not work normally;
- the case will not start, steer, stop, or park smoothly;
- you cannot see the route;
- the route includes an unplanned ramp, slope, stair, lift, dock edge, public crossing, vehicle path, or overhead restriction;
- the assigned crew cannot control the case;
- the case begins to tip or run away;
- the two movers lose communication;
- the real case or route does not match the course demonstration.

## Explain-and-show course plan

Target length: **3–6 minutes before practice**.

### Watch it once

One uninterrupted movement:

- inspection;
- role agreement;
- flat-floor travel;
- corner;
- doorway;
- threshold;
- parking and handoff.

Use a separate clearly labeled demonstration for a ramp or slope. Do not splice a dangerous maneuver into appearing continuous or easy.

### See the control points

Close-ups or annotated stills:

1. caster, brake, and wheel inspection;
2. latch/lid/loose-part check;
3. hand placement on the actual case;
4. clear foot position;
5. sightline around the case;
6. partner position at a corner or door;
7. parked brake/chock method on the exact equipment.

### Show three wrong/right comparisons

1. moving before checking the route versus stopping and planning;
2. fingers between cases/doorframe versus hands on clear control points;
3. forcing a case over a bad threshold versus stopping and getting help.

### Three-question knowledge check

1. What do you do when the case blocks your view of the route?
2. What changes when you reach a door, ramp, threshold, or blind corner?
3. What do you do when a caster binds or the case begins moving faster than the crew can control?

### Observed practice check

The observer records whether the learner:

- inspected the actual case and route;
- confirmed destination and roles;
- used the agreed stop command correctly;
- started, traveled, turned, stopped, and parked under control;
- protected hands and feet;
- stopped when the conditions changed;
- asked for direction instead of guessing.

The record identifies the case, route, surface, transitions, observer, and result. Passing it does not create standing employer authorization.

## Field Skills message

> If you do not understand the task, ask. There are no stupid questions when you are actively trying to learn and work safely. It is always better to ask before a mistake than after someone gets hurt, equipment gets damaged, or the crew has to redo the work. Listen actively, ask for clarification, and request an in-field demonstration whenever you need one. You can only learn if you are willing to ask—and willing to listen.

## Research conclusion

The evidence supports a concise, visual road-case lesson without waiting for a full owner interview. The course can safely teach inspection, route planning, controlled movement, communication, changed-condition stops, pinch/crush avoidance, and parking under direction.

Owner review should concentrate on the explicitly flagged public/unverified practices and on whether the filmed role arrangement matches normal live-event work. The review does not need to reconstruct information already supported by stronger sources.
