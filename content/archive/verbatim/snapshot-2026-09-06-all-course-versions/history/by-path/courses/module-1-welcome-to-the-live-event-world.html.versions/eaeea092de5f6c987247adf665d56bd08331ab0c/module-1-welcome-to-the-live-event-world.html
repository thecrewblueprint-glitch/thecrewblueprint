Module 1

Welcome to the Live Event World

Before load-in times, call sheets, or hand signals matter, you need to understand what this job actually is, where you fit in it, and why crews keep calling back the same new hands. That's this lesson.

The Job The Phases Work Environments Who's on the Crew What Makes You Valuable

What a Stagehand Actually Does

Say the word "stagehand" and most people picture someone carrying boxes. That's part of it — but it's not the job.

On a real call you might unload trucks, push road cases, set staging, move barricade, run cable under a department's direction, sort gear by department, or help reset a venue after the event. The common thread isn't the specific task — it's that almost every other department depends on hands like yours to get their gear from the truck to the stage and back again.

That's why the job matters more than "moving boxes" suggests. A stagehand who pays attention, follows direction, protects the gear, and works safely is doing something the whole production depends on — even before they know a single piece of equipment by name.

The bar for day one

You're not expected to know the gear or the terminology yet. What matters is showing up prepared, listening, following direction, asking when you're unsure, and working safely. Confidence you haven't earned yet reads as a problem — not knowing something and saying so doesn't.

Load-In, Show Call, and Load-Out

Every call breaks into the same three phases. Knowing which one you're in changes what's expected of you.

PhaseWhenWhat It MeansLikely for Beginners?
Load-InBefore the eventGear comes in and the show gets builtVery likely
Show CallDuring the eventCrew supports the live performanceLess likely at first
Load-OutAfter the eventGear is struck, packed, and loaded outVery likely

Show call gets handed to newer hands less often because it needs more trust, timing, and familiarity with that specific production — not because it's more important. Most beginners build their reputation on load-in and load-out first, which is exactly where safety and reliability matter most anyway: gear is moving fast, the room is full of people who don't know you yet, and everyone's watching how you handle it.

For the fast-facts version of what to actually do at each phase — what to bring, when you're allowed to leave, how sign-in works — see the Load-In / Load-Out Reference in the Resource Hub. This lesson is about understanding the shape of the day; that page is about executing it.

That's the shape of one call. Stack enough calls together and you get a work week — and for local crew, that doesn't look like a typical 9-to-5. See What a Work Week Can Actually Look Like in the Resource Hub for real call-length patterns and an interactive calendar comparing a light week to a maxed-out one.

Common Work Environments

The expectations stay the same everywhere — show up ready, work safely, follow direction. The room you're in changes how that looks.

EnvironmentWhat It's LikeBeginner Focus
ArenasLarge, loud, fast-paced, heavy gear movementStay alert to moving equipment; follow direction closely
TheatersTighter backstage spaces, more precise workMove carefully; never touch fly systems or rigging unless trained and authorized
Convention CentersFreight and forklift traffic, tight room turnarounds between eventsWatch for forklifts, pallet jacks, and marked traffic paths
Outdoor ShowsWeather, uneven ground, temporary structuresHydrate, watch your footing, report weather concerns to a lead
Don't assume a task is safe because it looks simple

Each environment carries different hazards. If you're not trained, assigned, or sure how to do something safely in the room you're in — stop and ask.

Where You Fit In: Who Is on the Crew

A show has a chain of direction above you, and a call might put you alongside dozens of people or hundreds, split across departments. Knowing where you sit in that structure — and who to actually listen to — changes how you act.

Promoter / Production → Venue → Crew Chief / PC → Department Leads → You

That's the chain top to bottom. "PC" stands for production coordinator — on smaller calls the PC and the crew chief are often the same person, the one keeping the whole day on schedule. Day to day, the department leads are who actually direct your work — and a single call can have several of them, each running their own specialty:

Crew RoleWhat They DoWhat Beginners Should Know
Leads / Dept. HeadsGive direction and organize the workThis is your main point of contact
RiggersHandle overhead systems and flown equipmentStay clear unless directed — never stand under a suspended load
Audio CrewSound equipment and systemsDon't touch audio gear unless asked
Lighting CrewFixtures, truss, cable, controlFollow direction carefully around lighting gear
Video CrewScreens, cameras, projectors, LEDEquipment can be delicate and expensive
Carpenters / StagingDecks, scenic pieces, stage structurePay attention to safe lifting and placement
Truck LoadersGear movement in and out of trailersFollow truck direction — cases have a specific order

You take direction from whoever is leading your department or your section of the call — not from the show, not from the headliner, not from whoever seems busiest or sounds confident. If a lead tells you one thing and someone else tells you another, the lead giving you direction wins. When you're not sure who that is, that's the first question to ask — guessing wrong can move the wrong gear, slow the crew down, or create a safety problem.

This also means your job isn't to freelance decisions. Good new hands ask before they act when something isn't clear. It reads as competence, not weakness — the hands who get called back are the ones a lead doesn't have to worry about.

Touring crew vs. local crew

Touring crew travels with the production — they built the show and know it inside out. Local crew is hired market by market to provide labor support, which is the role most new stagehands start in. That's not a lesser spot; the touring crew's plan only works if local crew executes it well. Your job on early calls is to support that plan, not improvise your own.

What Makes a New Stagehand Valuable

Nobody expects a first-call hand to know everything. What gets you called back isn't experience — it's how you handle not having it yet. Here's what actually gets noticed on a call, grouped into three habits:

Show Up Ready
1
Punctuality Early enough to check in, find your lead, and be ready for direction — not still parking at call time.
2
Reliability Showing up prepared — right clothes, right gear, already knowing the basics of where you're going — and doing what you said you would, call after call. It's what gets your number saved for next time.
Communicate Well
1
Active listening You don't know what you don't know yet. The fastest way to learn a crew's rhythm is to watch and confirm instructions before you try to keep pace with it — not interrupt to prove you've got it.
2
Clear communication Asking questions, reporting problems, and updating your lead when a task is done — instead of disappearing, guessing, or pretending you understood.
3
Good judgment Questions are welcome, but mid-lift or mid-instruction isn't the moment — find the gap, then ask. And if something's unclear, unsafe, or outside your training, stop and ask instead of guessing.
Work the Way You're Directed
1
Following instructions Do the task exactly as directed before you try to improve on it. A lead who says "these four cases, stage left, leave them closed" wants exactly that — not your idea of a better plan.
2
Safety commitment Watching for hazards, coiling cable the way you were shown instead of the fast way, and never treating speed as more important than doing it safely. Small habits are what leads actually notice.
Good Example

A new hand isn't sure whether a cable is safe to move. They ask: "Do you want this moved, or should I leave it until the department clears it?"

Good Example

A new hand is asked to help with something they haven't been trained to do. They say: "I haven't been trained on that yet — can someone show me the safe way, or would you like me to help with something else?"

Bad Example

A new hand sees a road case and moves it without asking. It seemed helpful — but it was staged correctly and belonged to another department.

Quick Knowledge Check

Click a question to check your answer.

What is the main role of an entry-level stagehand?

To support the larger production through safe, organized, and efficient labor under the direction of crew leads and department heads.

What should you do if you're unsure whether something is safe to move?

Ask a lead or the appropriate department before acting — never guess with equipment that isn't clearly yours to move.

Why are brand-new stagehands less likely to work show call right away?

Show call requires more trust, timing, and familiarity with the specific production than load-in or load-out do.

What should a beginner do around a rigging zone?

Stay clear, never stand under a suspended load, and wait for direction from the appropriate lead.

What's one of the most valuable traits for a new stagehand?

Reliability — showing up prepared, working safely, communicating clearly, and following instructions beats acting experienced and creating confusion.

Key Takeaways

  • Stagehand work is real production work, not a spectator role — nearly every department depends on it.
  • Every call breaks into load-in, show call, and load-out. Beginners usually start with load-in and load-out.
  • The room changes the risks — arenas, theaters, convention centers, and outdoor shows each demand different awareness.
  • Know your lead and follow that chain. Guessing or taking direction from the wrong person creates problems. As local crew, your job is to support the touring crew's plan, not improvise your own.
  • What makes a new hand valuable comes down to three habits: showing up ready (punctuality, reliability), communicating well (listening, clear updates, good judgment), and working the way you're directed (following instructions, safety) — not experience you don't have yet.
