Module 1

Welcome to the Live Event World

Before load-in times, call sheets, or hand signals matter, you need to understand what this job actually is, where you fit in it, and why crews keep calling back the same new hands. That's this lesson.

What a Stagehand Actually Does

"Stagehand" covers more ground than the name suggests. On any given call you might be building truss, pushing cases, coiling cable, laying down staging deck, or hanging soft goods — whatever the show needs, in whatever order it needs to happen.

You're not a spectator brought in to watch professionals work. You're part of how the show physically comes together and comes back apart, safely and on schedule. That's true whether it's your first call or your five hundredth. The difference experience buys you is speed, judgment, and which jobs you get handed — not whether the work matters.

It's also a starting point, not a ceiling. Audio, lighting, video, rigging, and carpentry are all specialized departments that most people enter through general stagehand work first. Learning to be a solid, safe, useful hand is what makes those doors open later.

Where You Fit in the Bigger Picture

A show has a chain of direction above you, and knowing where you sit in it changes how you act on a call.

Promoter / Production → Venue → Crew Chief / PC → Department Leads → You

You take direction from whoever is leading your department or your section of the call — not from the show, not from the headliner, not from whoever seems busiest. If a lead tells you to do something and someone else tells you something different, the lead giving you direction wins. When you're not sure who that is, that's the first question to ask, not something to guess at.

This also means your job isn't to freelance decisions. Good new hands ask before they act when something isn't clear. It reads as competence, not weakness — the hands who get called back are the ones a lead doesn't have to worry about.

The Three Phases of a Show

Every call breaks down into three phases, and each one has a different pace and a different set of expectations.

Load-In

Trucks arrive, gear comes off, and the empty room turns into a show. Fast-moving, physical, and the phase where being ready and useful matters most.

Show Call

The event itself. Less lifting, more watching, holding positions, and reacting fast if something needs attention mid-show.

Load-Out

Everything in reverse, usually faster and later at night than load-in. Tired crews and last-minute urgency make this the phase where mistakes happen most.

For the fast-facts version of what to actually do at each phase — what to bring, when you're allowed to leave, how sign-in works — see the Load-In / Load-Out Reference in the Resource Hub. This lesson is about understanding the shape of the day; that page is about executing it.

What Makes a New Stagehand Valuable

Nobody expects a first-call hand to know everything. What gets you called back isn't experience — it's how you handle not having it yet.

1
Show up ready, not just on time Right clothes, right gear, already knowing the basics of where you're going. Being on time with none of that ready still creates a problem for someone else to solve.
2
Listen more than you talk, early on You don't know what you don't know yet. The fastest way to learn a crew's rhythm is to watch it before you try to keep pace with it.
3
Ask at the right moment Questions are welcome. Mid-lift, mid-cue, or mid-instruction usually isn't the moment — find the gap, then ask.
4
Do the small things right Coil cable the way you were shown, not the fast way. Watch what's overhead. Don't wander off between tasks. Small habits are what leads actually notice.
5
Be reliable before you're fast Speed comes with reps. Reliability is available to you on day one, and it's what gets your number saved for the next call.

Key Takeaways

  • Stagehand work is real production work, not a spectator role — and it's the common entry point into every specialized department.
  • Direction flows down a chain. Know who your lead is, and take direction from them specifically.
  • Every call has three phases — load-in, show call, load-out — each with its own pace and risk.
  • What makes a new hand valuable is preparation, listening, timing your questions, attention to small details, and reliability — not experience you don't have yet.
