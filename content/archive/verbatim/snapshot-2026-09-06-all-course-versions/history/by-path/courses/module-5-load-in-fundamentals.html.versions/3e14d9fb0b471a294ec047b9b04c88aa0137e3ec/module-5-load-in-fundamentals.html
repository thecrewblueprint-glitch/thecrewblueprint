Module 5

Load-In Fundamentals

Load-in can feel fast, loud, and confusing the first time. Trucks are unloading, cases are moving, departments are sorting gear, and leads are trying to keep it all on schedule. This module is the rhythm underneath all of it.

What Load-In Actually Is

Moving gear into a venue and building the show before anyone in the audience shows up.

Load-in covers unloading trucks, pushing road cases, sorting equipment by department, setting staging, running cable under direction, moving barricade, and keeping work areas clear. For a lot of new hands, it's the very first call they ever work — and it's where you actually learn how a crew operates: how gear moves, how departments talk to each other, and how much organization happens before doors open.

Load-in isn't just about speed. Working safely, following direction, and staying aware of what's happening around you matters more than how fast you personally move.

Truck Unload and Road Cases

The dock is often the first hazard zone of the day, and the cases coming off it aren't all the same.

Truck unloads are usually one of the first big tasks of a load-in. Gear tends to come off in a specific order — cases, carts, decks, truss, barricade, cable trunks, department gear — and that order matters. Don't climb into a truck, move gear, stack cases, or change the unload sequence unless a lead or truck loader tells you to. The dock area itself stacks up hazards fast: ramps, dock plates, forklifts, pallet jacks, uneven ground, and low light.

Road cases themselves can be heavy, awkward, tall, top-heavy, or just unpredictable — bad casters, locked wheels, a loose lid, a damaged latch. Move them only under direction, and pay attention to brakes, handles, lids, casters, slope, and the actual path in front of you.

Moving Cases: Push, Pull, Guide, and Pinch Points

How you move a case matters as much as whether you're allowed to move it.

Pushing is usually safer than pulling on a flat, clear path, but the right method depends on the case, the slope, the surface, and what your lead tells you to do. Pulling can work for short adjustments or tight spots, but it's riskier if the case rolls toward you or you can't see where you're going. Guiding — steering or spotting while someone else pushes — is worth doing for large cases, blind corners, ramps, doorways, and crowded areas.

Watch for pinch points

A pinch point is anywhere a hand, foot, or body part can get caught between moving gear and something else — between two cases, a case and a wall, a case and the truck, a wheel and a dock plate, or gear and a doorway. Ramps and dock plates make this worse, since a case can gain speed or a wheel can catch without warning.

Building the Room, Sorting Gear, and Cable Awareness

An empty room turns into a working event space fast, usually with several departments moving at once.

Staging, audio, lighting, video, backline, and rigging may all be setting up at the same time. You might help move items, sort cases, or clear paths — but don't touch specialized gear without direction. And don't assume something's safe to touch just because the show hasn't started yet; some systems get energized during load-in itself.

Cases also get sorted by department — audio, lighting, video, rigging, backline, wardrobe, and more. A case in the wrong spot slows the whole crew down or blocks a path. Read labels when they exist, listen to direction, confirm the location, and ask if you're unsure.

Cable deserves its own caution: it can be heavy, fragile, expensive, energized, or staged deliberately as part of a plan you can't see from the outside. Don't plug in, unplug, pull, coil, tape, move, or reroute a cable unless the department running it tells you to.

When to Move Fast, and Taking Direction Well

Load-in has real time pressure. Speed still never replaces safety.

Some moments call for moving efficiently — clearing a truck, finishing an assigned task. Others call for slowing down: ramps, dock plates, forklifts, overhead work, heavy lifts, cable paths, tight spaces, doorways, blind corners, or anything unfamiliar. Learning to read which moment you're in is its own skill, and it comes with time on calls, not all at once.

Leads are busy and won't always have time to explain something twice. Be the hand who listens fully, repeats back the key details, asks a focused question if something's unclear, completes the task as assigned, and checks back in when it's done. Taking direction well doesn't mean pretending you understood — if an instruction feels unsafe or unclear, that's still a reason to stop and ask, not push through.

On a flat, clear path, is pushing or pulling a case usually safer?

What's a pinch point?

Key Takeaways

  • Load-in is how gear gets into a venue and how the show gets built before doors open — safety and direction matter more than raw speed.
  • Truck unload order and road case handling are not yours to decide — move only under direction, and stay aware of brakes, casters, and slope.
  • Pushing beats pulling on a clear path, guiding helps on blind corners and tight spots, and pinch points are everywhere gear and bodies share space.
  • Don't touch specialized gear or cable without direction — some systems energize before the show starts, and cable is often staged for a specific plan.
  • Match the crew's pace: move efficiently when it's simple, slow down around hazards, and ask a focused question instead of guessing when a direction isn't clear.
