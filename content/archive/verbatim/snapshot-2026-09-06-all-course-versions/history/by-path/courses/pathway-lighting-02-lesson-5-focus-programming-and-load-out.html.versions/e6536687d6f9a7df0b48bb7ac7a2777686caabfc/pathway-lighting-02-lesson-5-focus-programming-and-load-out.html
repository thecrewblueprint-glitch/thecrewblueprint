Lighting Pathway · Course 2

Focus, Programming, Show Operation, and Load-Out

After installation, the lighting system must be checked, focused, programmed, rehearsed, operated, and removed. A fixture making light isn't enough — the crew has to verify full response, aim and calibrate fixtures, build repeatable console logic, operate safely during the show, and load out without losing information or damaging equipment.

What You'll Learn On a Real Call Beginner Mistakes Habit & Safety

What You'll Learn

  • Explain the difference between function check, focus, programming, and operation.
  • Recognize how scripted shows and busked shows differ.
  • Understand why some faults are not repaired during a live performance.
  • Identify load-out risks and documentation needs.

On a Real Call

How a show gets run depends on the format — but the discipline underneath stays the same.

In a theater or corporate cue-to-cue, the programmer may build precise cue stacks timed to a script. In concerts and festivals, the operator may combine stored cues, palettes, effects, and live busking. During show operation, the lighting team monitors power, data, fixtures, weather, backups, cueing, visibility, and communication with the show caller or stage management.

Focus is more than pointing a light

Focus includes purpose, position, shuttering, color quality, camera needs, scenic interaction, and documentation — not just aiming a fixture in a general direction.

Beginner Mistakes

  • Treating focus as just pointing lights generally. Focus includes purpose, position, shuttering, color quality, camera needs, scenic interaction, and documentation.
  • Ignoring backup files. Console files and show data should be saved and backed up throughout programming and before load-out.
  • Trying to repair every show fault immediately. Access risk and the possibility of causing a bigger failure matter more than fixing it fast.
  • Rushing load-out because the show is over. Fatigue, darkness, simultaneous departments, and schedule pressure make load-out high-risk, not low-risk.

Professional Habit & Safety Notes

Stay disciplined through show and load-out

Listen to the chain of command, confirm releases before moving or de-energizing systems, protect cable paths, document damage, and pack by the planned truck order.

Safety notes: Do not access overhead equipment, energized equipment, lifts, roofs, or restricted work areas during a show or load-out unless directed by authorized leads and trained for the task. Public clearance, department release, de-energization procedures, and controlled lowering of flown systems must be respected.

Why is load-out considered high risk?

Fatigue, darkness, schedule pressure, and many departments moving at once all increase hazard — the work isn't over just because the show is.

Scenario Practice

Two real situations. Think through your response before reading the expected one.

Corporate Room Access Conflict

Lighting needs a lift to focus front light, but catering is setting tables and scenic is still working downstage.

Expected response: Don't drive or move equipment through another department without direction. Ask the lighting lead where to stage and wait for coordinated access.

Load-Out Fatigue

After a long show day, multiple departments are striking at once and a cable path crosses an exit route.

Expected response: Report the blocked or unsafe path to the lead and help reroute/protect cable only as directed. Maintain awareness of public clearance and department release.

Key Takeaway

The show is not finished until the system is safely removed, documented, packed, and accounted for.

Think About It

  • When might it be safer to leave a failed fixture alone during a show?
  • What information should be saved or documented before load-out?
  • Why should load-out be planned during pre-production instead of after the final cue?
