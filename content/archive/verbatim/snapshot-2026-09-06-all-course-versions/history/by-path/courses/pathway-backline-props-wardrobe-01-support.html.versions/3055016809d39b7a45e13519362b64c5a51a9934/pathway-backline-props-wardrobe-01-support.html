Supporting Backline, Props & Wardrobe

These three departments don't share one set of rules, but they share one skill: knowing when an item is sensitive, preset, or performer-owned — and treating that status as part of the job. Moving an assigned item is labor. Changing it is department work.

What You Can Help With What Stays Off-Limits Mistakes & Communication

What You Can Help With, Under Direction

Backline, props, and wardrobe are separate crafts. A ground hand isn't any of them, but is useful to all three.

For backline, you can move road cases from truck to staging area, place cases where the backline technician asks, help move drum risers, carry or position amp cabinets and hardware when assigned, and strike/re-case equipment in the order and method the technician directs. For props, you can unload and stage prop trunks/cases, carry or roll large props into the venue, move furniture or set-dressing under direction, and help with striking and re-packing once shown the correct method. For wardrobe, you can move closed trunks and racks from truck to the assigned area, help roll empty racks or cases, and move tables, mirrors, or work lights as directed — without opening or reorganizing what's inside. General production items — tables, chairs, signage, work lights, carts — are lower-risk and common ground-hand work across all three.

What Stays Off-Limits Without Direction

Think of it as a rising scale: general production item → department-owned gear → show-critical preset → performer-owned item. The higher up that scale, the more explicit the direction needs to be before you touch it.

Moving is labor. Changing is department work.

You can move a case, trunk, rack, or prop when assigned. That doesn't authorize you to change its settings, contents, preset location, packing method, or condition — those stay with the department unless you're specifically told otherwise.

Never tune an instrument, change amp/pedal/keyboard/drum settings, or move an artist's instrument after it's been placed. Never move a prop from a preset position, substitute one prop for another, or repair/modify a prop — its exact location can be part of the show. Never open a wardrobe trunk, rearrange a costume preset, or handle performer-specific clothing, shoes, wigs, or accessories without wardrobe's direction. Stage weapons, pyrotechnic props, and other regulated items are never treated as ordinary props.

Common Mistakes and How to Ask

The novice mistake to prevent across all three departments is "cleaning up" something that was already exactly where it needed to be.

Common problems: grabbing delicate equipment instead of a designated handle, stacking cases against their labels or obvious geometry, placing drinks or loose objects on instrument cases or wardrobe trunks, crushing garment bags under other gear, and assuming two similar-looking props are interchangeable. If you don't know whether something is preset, ask before moving it.

Useful phrases: "Where do you want this staged?" · "Is this preset, or can I move it?" · "Do you want this opened or left closed?" · "I haven't handled this before — show me where to grab it." Asking first isn't passive — it's what makes you someone a department actually wants back.

Quick Knowledge Check

Click a question to check your answer.

You've been asked to move a musician's amp. Can you also adjust the settings while you're at it?

No — moving the amp is labor, changing its settings is backline's decision, not yours.

A prop is sitting alone on a table backstage and looks out of place. Should you tidy it away?

No — its exact location can be part of the show. Ask before moving anything you think is a preset.

You're moving a closed wardrobe trunk. Can you open it to check what's inside?

No — treat the closed trunk as freight and leave the contents to wardrobe unless you're told otherwise.

What's the difference between "moving" and "changing" an item?

Moving an assigned item where directed is labor. Changing its settings, contents, preset, or condition is department work.

According to the ownership-sensitivity scale, which requires the most explicit direction before you touch it?

A performer-owned or performer-specific item — the highest sensitivity level, above general production items, department gear, and show-critical presets.

Key Takeaways

  • Backline, props, and wardrobe are separate crafts — useful ground-hand support looks different in each, but the underlying discipline is the same.
  • Moving an assigned item is labor. Changing its settings, contents, preset, or condition is department work unless you're specifically told otherwise.
  • Follow the ownership-sensitivity scale: general item → department gear → show-critical preset → performer-owned item — the higher up, the more explicit direction you need.
  • When you don't know whether something is preset or personal, ask before you touch it.
