Module 10

Getting Hired, Getting Called Back, and Growing

Everything so far has been about the call itself. This last module is about what happens before it and after it — how the work actually reaches you, and how you turn one call into a career.

Where Stagehand Work Comes From

There's no single pipeline. Work reaches people through several different paths, often at the same time.

Some workers get hired through labor companies. Some are called directly by venues. Some work through unions. Some find calls through production companies, referrals, or staffing apps. Markets differ a lot — some are heavily union-based, others run mostly on labor companies and staffing agencies. No matter which path brings you the call, the basic expectations don't change: be on time, be prepared, and be someone a lead wants to call again.

Reputation matters across every one of these paths. A labor company remembers who they can count on. A venue remembers who showed up ready. A referral only happens because someone was willing to put their own name behind you.

Union and non-union paths build that reputation differently. IATSE locals run their own referral system — typically a referral list or hiring hall, often a probationary or orientation period for new hires, and in some locals a formal junior-to-journeyman structure with defined skill categories. Exact rules vary a lot by local; there's no single national entry process. Non-union work usually runs on call history instead — you build a reputation with labor companies, venues, and crew leads one call at a time, and that history is what gets you rebooked and referred to other work.

Building a Resume and Talking About Experience Honestly

You don't need a long resume to look ready. You need an honest one.

A beginner resume should be simple and relevant — it needs to show quickly that you're reliable, physically prepared for event work, safety-minded, and able to follow direction. Transferable experience counts: warehouse work, construction labor, moving companies, anything physical and structured translates.

Being new isn't a problem. Pretending you're not new is the problem — the same principle that applies to safety applies here too. Present your limited experience clearly and confidently: what you've done, what you haven't, and that you're ready to learn. Honest communication helps a lead assign you the right task and prevents the kind of unsafe guessing that comes from someone pretending to know more than they do.

Reliability and Reputation

This is the section that ties the whole course together. If you remember one thing from Stagehand Fundamentals, make it this.

Getting rebooked is rarely about advanced technical skill at the entry level. It's about reliability: showing up prepared, on time, rested enough to work safely, dressed correctly, reachable, and ready to follow direction. Reliable hands help leads plan and trust the crew — unreliable behavior can remove a beginner from future call lists fast, no matter how physically capable they are.

Reputation isn't built in one impressive moment. It's built by consistent behavior across many calls, and it can spread quickly in either direction — a good reputation brings more work, better assignments, and stronger referrals; a poor one (unsafe, unreliable, dishonest, difficult to manage) travels just as fast.

Moving From General Stagehand to a Department Specialty

General labor is the starting point for almost everyone in this industry, not a dead end.

Over time, workers often move toward audio, lighting, video, staging, carpentry, rigging, backline, or another specialty. The right way to move toward one is the same pattern this whole course has taught: show interest, ask good questions, and let a department bring you in under real training and supervision — not by touching gear you haven't been cleared for and hoping it works out.

Useful next areas to explore, once you're ready: OSHA 10, forklift awareness, lift awareness, rigging awareness, or basics in whichever department caught your interest on real calls. Awareness isn't the same as authorization or certification — specialized training still matters, and it's the next real step, not this course's job to provide.

Your First 30-Day Development Plan

Don't try to master everything at once. A simple structure beats an overwhelming one.

For your first month, focus on: showing up prepared every time, learning call etiquette on real calls, practicing clear communication, improving your case and cable awareness, learning the names of departments and leads you work with, asking for feedback when you can, and starting to notice which specialty actually interests you. That's it — not everything this course covered at once, just consistent practice on the fundamentals while you build the track record that gets you called back.

This is a Crew Blueprint development framework, not a documented industry-standard onboarding schedule — real employers and IATSE locals use their own evaluation periods, ranging from a single orientation session to 90-day, 180-day, or full-year probationary structures. Use this as a personal practice plan, not a claim about how any specific employer or local actually onboards new hands.

Quick Knowledge Check

Click a question to check your answer.

Is there one standard path stagehands use to find work?

No — labor companies, venues, unions, production companies, referrals, and staffing apps all provide real work, often at the same time, and it varies a lot by market.

You have no live-event experience. What should your resume do?

Be simple and honest, and highlight transferable experience (warehouse, construction, moving) that shows you're reliable, physically prepared, and safety-minded.

What actually gets an entry-level hand rebooked most often?

Reliability — showing up prepared, on time, and ready to follow direction — not advanced technical skill.

What's the right way to move toward a department specialty?

Show interest, ask good questions, and get brought in under real training and supervision — not by touching gear you haven't been cleared for.

What should your first 30 days actually focus on?

The fundamentals, consistently: showing up prepared, communication, case/cable awareness, learning names, and noticing what specialty interests you — not mastering everything at once.

Key Takeaways

  • Work comes from multiple paths at once — labor companies, venues, unions, referrals, staffing apps — and reputation matters across all of them.
  • An honest, simple resume beats an exaggerated one. Being new isn't the problem; pretending you're not new is.
  • Reliability, not technical skill, is what gets entry-level hands rebooked. Reputation is built call by call, in both directions.
  • Moving toward a specialty happens through real training and supervision, not by touching gear you haven't been cleared for.
  • Your first 30 days should focus on consistent fundamentals, not trying to master everything from this course at once.
