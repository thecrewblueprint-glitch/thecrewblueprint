Signal Flow, Processors, Mapping, and Redundancy

The media server renders or plays content. The LED processor adapts that content to the real cabinet raster, maps outputs, handles scaling and synchronization, and distributes LED data to receiving cards. They are not the same job, and confusing them is one of the fastest ways to misdiagnose a wall problem.

What You'll Learn On a Real Call Beginner Mistakes Habit & Safety

What You'll Learn

  • Trace a typical LED signal chain from source to physical pixels.
  • Separate media-server duties from LED-processor duties.
  • Explain mapping and receiver configuration at a beginner level.
  • Recognize common failures caused by topology or configuration mismatch.
  • Understand why redundancy must be tested.

On a Real Call

A typical chain is source or playback system to switcher or screen-management system, then router, converter, or distribution if needed, then LED processor, transport over copper or fiber, receiving cards, hub boards, driver electronics, and physical pixels.

On a concert, content may come from media servers into a router, through redundant screen-management systems, into primary and backup processors, and then to redundant LED-data paths. On a smaller corporate show, laptops and playback may feed a switcher, then a processor, then the wall. In both cases, the processor file must match the actual panel model, cabinet count, orientation, port topology, and receiver ecosystem.

A connected backup cable is not proven redundancy

Redundancy procedures should be tested during setup, not discovered during show failure. A plugged-in fiber tells you nothing about whether failover actually works.

Beginner Mistakes

  • Thinking the media server replaces the processor. The server creates the image; the processor maps and distributes it.
  • Assuming any Ethernet switch or network layout will work. Proprietary LED data isn't ordinary IP traffic unless the system documentation says so.
  • Starting the wall at the wrong cabinet. This leads to chasing a mapping problem across the entire wall.
  • Leaving backup fibers connected but never testing failover. A connected backup cable is not proven redundancy.

Professional Habit & Safety Notes

Label everything, change one variable at a time

Label home runs, ports, cabinet coordinates, fibers, and backup paths. Save known-good configurations with clear names, and change one variable at a time when troubleshooting whenever practical.

Safety notes: Do not upload unknown receiver files or unverified firmware/configuration files to live equipment unless directed by the qualified LED lead or video engineer. Do not unplug or reroute processor, fiber, or data paths without permission.

Quick Knowledge Check

Click a question to check your answer.

A media server usually does which job?

Renders or plays back content — the LED processor maps and distributes that content to the physical raster, which is a different job.

A backup fiber is plugged in. What still needs to happen?

Failover should be configured and tested — connected backup cabling isn't meaningful unless the system is configured correctly and failover is proven to work.

Scenario: Corporate General Session Resolution Mismatch

The wall is built, but the laptop won't output the expected resolution. What's your next move?

Tell the video lead. Check source format, EDID, processor input settings, raster, switcher/scaler path, and supported resolution under direction. Source compatibility can look like a wall problem — EDID and input format are part of the LED workflow.

Key Takeaway

The wall will only display correctly when the source, processor, receiver configuration, mapping, and physical topology agree.

Think About It

  • What does the LED processor do that the media server usually does not?
  • What can happen if the receiver configuration does not match the cabinet hardware?
  • Why does labeling reduce recovery time?
  • What makes redundancy real rather than theoretical?
