Pixel Pitch, Resolution, Brightness, and Equipment Choice

Pixel pitch is the center-to-center spacing between pixels, usually measured in millimeters. Smaller pitch creates more pixels in a given area and can look better up close, but it also increases pixel count, processing needs, panel count, power density, heat, cost, setup time, and sensitivity to calibration.

What You'll Learn On a Real Call Beginner Mistakes Habit & Safety

What You'll Learn

  • Define pixel pitch, native resolution, nit, refresh rate, and scan rate.
  • Explain why smaller pixel pitch is not automatically better.
  • Recognize how venue type affects equipment selection.
  • Understand why camera performance must be tested, not assumed.
  • Distinguish indoor, outdoor, fine-pitch, mesh, transparent, floor, and mobile LED categories.

On a Real Call

The right panel gets selected against the job, not against one isolated spec.

For an executive meeting, a fine-pitch wall may be needed because the front row is close and text has to be readable on camera. For an arena IMAG wall, a moderate pitch may be acceptable because the audience is farther away. For a stadium or outdoor festival, brightness, weather resistance, wind behavior, weight, service access, and long-distance viewing may matter more than fine detail.

Native resolution is not the same as wall size

Native resolution is the total physical pixel count of the assembled wall. A large, coarse-pitch wall can have fewer pixels than a smaller fine-pitch one.

Beginner Mistakes

  • Choosing the smallest pitch without checking impact. Processing, power, heat, labor, and budget all scale with pitch.
  • Confusing physical size with resolution. A large coarse wall can have fewer pixels than a smaller fine-pitch wall.
  • Running the wall at maximum brightness by default. It can overpower cameras, wash out black level, increase heat, and expose calibration differences.
  • Assuming published refresh rate guarantees camera compatibility. Scan behavior, shutter, frame rate, synchronization, PWM behavior, and camera sensor readout also matter.

Professional Habit & Safety Notes

Ask what it's for before judging the wall

Close presentation, IMAG, scenic imagery, outdoor daylight, camera backdrop, or long-distance viewing all call for different priorities. There's no single "correct" panel spec.

Safety notes: Do not promise that a pitch, brightness, IP rating, or refresh rate will be acceptable without product- and site-specific confirmation. Camera tests should be conducted with the actual camera settings where practical. Outdoor-rated product still requires qualified review of weather, support, power, and operation conditions.

Quick Knowledge Check

Click a question to check your answer.

Which statement about pixel pitch is most accurate?

Pixel pitch affects pixel density and practical viewing distance — it's one selection factor, not the automatic determinant of image quality, safety, or processing workflow.

Which symptom may appear on camera even if the wall looks good by eye?

Horizontal bands or flicker — camera interaction can cause banding, pulsing, moire, color breakup, or rolling-shutter artifacts that aren't visible to the naked eye.

Scenario: Corporate Room Camera Bands

The wall looks fine by eye, but cameras show rolling bands. What are the likely factors?

Escalate to video/LED/camera leads. Possible factors include refresh behavior, scan rate, camera shutter, frame rate, genlock, brightness, converter quality, and processor settings. Camera compatibility must be tested with the actual chain — not assumed from a spec sheet.

Key Takeaway

Select LED equipment by audience, content, environment, structure, power, camera use, and serviceability — not by one spec.

Think About It

  • Why might a 1.9 mm wall be wrong for a specific job even though it has high pixel density?
  • What is the difference between wall size and wall resolution?
  • What symptoms can show up on camera even when the wall looks fine by eye?
  • Why should maximum brightness not be the default show setting?
