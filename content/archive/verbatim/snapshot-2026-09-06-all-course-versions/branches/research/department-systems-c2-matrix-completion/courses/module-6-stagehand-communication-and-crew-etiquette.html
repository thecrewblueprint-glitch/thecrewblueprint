Module 6

Stagehand Communication and Crew Etiquette

A lot of new hands struggle not because they can't do the physical work, but because they don't know the unspoken rules of the jobsite. This module is those rules, made spoken.

Talking to Leads Signals & Radio Phone Etiquette Staying Busy Getting Asked Back

Talking to Leads, and Asking Questions the Right Way

Good communication starts with knowing who to talk to, and keeping it short.

Leads and department heads assign tasks, organize the crew, and keep the work moving safely. Identify who yours is early in the call, and check in with that person when you need direction — not with whoever happens to be standing nearby. Keep it clear, respectful, and brief; long explanations usually aren't needed on a busy site. Don't argue, interrupt, talk over instructions, or take direction from someone without confirming they're actually in your chain of command.

You're not expected to know everything, and asking beats guessing every time. Keep questions direct, specific, and tied to the actual task, and ask before you start something unclear or unfamiliar — not after. What you shouldn't do is ask questions just to avoid work or push back on a simple instruction.

Hand Signals, Radios, and Quick Responses

Jobsites get loud — trucks, forklifts, power tools, crowds — which is where hand signals matter most for a new hand.

Keep hand signals simple and visible, don't invent your own mid-task, and let one person guide the communication during equipment movement so the crew isn't getting mixed signals. If a signal is unclear, stop if it's safe to and ask.

For the actual hand signals used on real calls, see Field Tools for Real Calls in the Resource Hub.

Radio use isn't something you need on day one — it's worth knowing for down the road, once you're trusted with more. Keep transmissions short, clear, and professional, don't talk over anyone, and listen before you key up. Terms can vary by employer, venue, or department, so confirm local usage instead of assuming.

Quick responses worth knowing

Copy — heard and understood. Heard — message received. Standing by — waiting, ready. Say again — repeat that. Don't say "copy" if you didn't actually understand the instruction — that causes more problems than admitting you need it repeated.

Phone Etiquette on a Call

Your phone can create real distraction and real safety risk, even when it feels harmless.

Using your phone during active work makes you look unreliable or unavailable, even if you're just checking the time. It's fine for call details, maps, an emergency, or work-related texts — it shouldn't get in the way of the job itself. Photos and video may be flat-out restricted by the venue, artist, client, or production, and never post backstage, credential, schedule, or private event content without permission. That one isn't a judgment call.

Staying Busy Without Overstepping, and Handling Correction

Being proactive is good. Overstepping is risky. They're not the same thing.

A good hand doesn't vanish once a task is finished — check back in with your lead. Overstepping means starting tasks, moving gear, opening cases, coiling cable, changing a layout, entering a restricted area, or helping another department, all without being told to. Safer ways to stay useful: check in, keep your assigned area clear, stage yourself where you were told to, and wait ready.

Getting corrected is part of the job, not proof you're failing at it. Don't argue, make excuses, or take it personally — listen, acknowledge it, adjust. If the correction is about safety, take it seriously and change the behavior immediately, not eventually.

Respecting Everyone on Site, and What Gets You Asked Back

Live events run on cooperation between groups who don't always report to the same people.

Touring crew travels with the production and knows the show inside out. Local crew provides labor and support at the venue — the role most new hands start in. Venue staff know the building, the policies, and the emergency procedures. Treat all three professionally. Don't talk down to people, gossip, complain loudly, or create friction between groups.

Getting asked back isn't about being the strongest or most technical hand on the call. Leads remember who's on time, prepared, safe, respectful, and easy to direct — that's what actually reduces stress for the whole crew. Bad habits can cost you future calls even if you're fully capable physically. Reputation gets built one call at a time.

Quick Knowledge Check

Click a question to check your answer.

Who should you check in with when you need direction?

Your identified lead or department head — not whoever happens to be standing nearby.

Should you say "copy" if you didn't actually understand the instruction?

No — saying "copy" without understanding causes more problems than asking for it to be repeated.

Is it fine to quickly check your phone during active work?

No — it creates real distraction and safety risk, and makes you look unreliable even if it's brief.

What does "overstepping" mean on a call, and why is it risky?

Starting tasks, moving gear, or entering areas without being told to — it's risky because it creates safety and coordination problems the crew isn't expecting.

Does getting corrected by a lead mean you're failing?

No — it's a normal part of the job. Listen, acknowledge it, and adjust, especially fast if it's about safety.

Key Takeaways

  • Identify your lead early, keep communication brief and respectful, and ask questions before starting something unclear — not after.
  • Keep hand signals simple and let one person guide equipment movement — that's what you'll actually use early on. Radio etiquette (short, work-only traffic) is worth knowing for later. Know what "copy," "heard," "standing by," and "say again" actually mean either way.
  • Phones create real distraction and safety risk on active work — and never post backstage or credentialed content without permission.
  • Stay busy by checking in and staying ready, not by taking on tasks nobody assigned you. Take correction as normal, not personal — especially on safety.
  • Respect touring crew, local crew, and venue staff equally. Getting asked back comes down to being on time, prepared, safe, respectful, and easy to direct — one call at a time.
