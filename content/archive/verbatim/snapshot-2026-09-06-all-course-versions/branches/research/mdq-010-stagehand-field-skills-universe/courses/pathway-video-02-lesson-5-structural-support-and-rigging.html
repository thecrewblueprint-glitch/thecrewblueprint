Structural Support, Rigging, and Load-Path Awareness

LED walls can be ground-supported, flown from approved points, roof-supported, mounted to temporary structures, built on mobile trailers, or installed in custom engineered systems. The structural concern is never just panel weight — dead load includes bumpers, rigging, cables, distribution boxes, covers, and secondary components.

What You'll Learn On a Real Call Beginner Mistakes Habit & Safety

What You'll Learn

  • Recognize the difference between ground-supported and flown LED walls.
  • Describe a basic load path without calculating it.
  • Understand why panel weight alone is not enough for structural decisions.
  • Identify dynamic effects such as hoisting, wind, movement, and impact.
  • Know role boundaries for rigging and structure.

On a Real Call

The same wall looks different depending on how it's supported — but the structural discipline underneath stays the same.

In an arena, a flown wall may hang from approved rigging points through hoists, truss, bumpers, and cabinet locks. At an outdoor festival, a wall may be part of a roof, tower, or ground-support system with ballast and wind planning. In a convention center, even an indoor ground-support wall still needs review for floor loading, stage decks, risers, slope, access, and egress.

Dynamic load can exceed static weight

Hoisting, stopping, wind, rotation, impact, and movement all add dynamic load on top of the wall's dead weight — the published panel weight alone never tells the full story.

Beginner Mistakes

  • Making structural decisions from the published panel weight alone. Dead load includes far more than the panels themselves.
  • Assuming a clean rectangular image means the wall is mechanically safe. Image quality and structural integrity are unrelated.
  • Removing braces, ballast, pins, locks, or outriggers because they're in the way. They belong to the approved system, not to convenience.
  • Standing under or behind a wall without understanding the exclusion zone. Established work plans exist for a reason.

Professional Habit & Safety Notes

Respect the load path and the exclusion zone

If something looks loose, missing, overloaded, unsupported, out of plumb, or different from the plan, stop and report it through the chain of command — don't fix it yourself and don't wait to see if it's fine.

Safety notes: Do not calculate, approve, or modify rigging, ballast, pick points, hoists, truss, roof systems, or ground support unless qualified and authorized. Follow the approved rigging or ground-support plan and the direction of the qualified lead. Do not let cables become suspension members or uncontrolled strain points. Do not work under suspended loads or within restricted areas unless the site plan and qualified supervision permit it.

Quick Knowledge Check

Click a question to check your answer.

A clean image on the LED wall proves what?

That the signal path is producing an image — nothing about structural approval, electrical code compliance, or the wind plan.

Who should approve rigging-point loads or ballast decisions?

Qualified and authorized rigging/structural personnel under the site plan — not any stagehand who helped build the wall, no matter how experienced they feel.

Scenario: Arena Flown Wall, Blocking Brace

A flown wall is physically complete, and a stagehand wants to remove a brace that's blocking access. What do you do?

Do not remove it. Stop and ask the rigging/LED lead. Any support component belongs to the approved system until qualified personnel say otherwise — structural components are not convenience items.

Key Takeaway

Structural safety belongs to qualified planning and supervision; a beginner's job is to recognize boundaries and report concerns.

Think About It

  • What is included in system dead load besides the panels?
  • Why is the load path more important than the image on the wall?
  • Who should decide whether a ground-supported wall has enough ballast?
  • What should you do if a support component appears missing or moved?
