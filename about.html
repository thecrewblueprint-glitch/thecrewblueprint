I’m building the guide I wish more new stagehands had before the first call.

I’m Aaron Bowman. I work in live-event production and run Deadhang Labor LLC. The Crew Blueprint grew from a simple idea: a new worker should be able to learn some of the language, expectations, work rhythm, and boundaries before they are standing in a loud venue trying to learn all of it at once.

Why I’m building this

Live-event work has a steep learning curve. A lot of useful context is still passed from person to person on the job: what a call looks like, how departments interact, what crew leads expect, what language people use, and when the right move is simply to stop and ask.

That kind of learning is valuable, but the first time someone hears it does not have to be while a truck is being unloaded or a crew is already under time pressure. The Crew Blueprint is my attempt to move some of that orientation earlier, in plain language, so a learner can arrive with better context and better questions.

What I’m trying to do well

This project is not about making someone look more qualified than they are. It is about giving people a stronger starting point and making the line between general knowledge and real work authority easier to see.

Make the work less mysterious

Explain common production language, call phases, crew expectations, department roles, and everyday work patterns before the learner is under pressure.

Teach the boundary with the task

Knowing what belongs to the lead, technician, employer, venue, or another qualified person is part of learning the job—not an afterthought.

Check the field against the source

Where possible, I compare field practice with stronger sources such as official guidance, manufacturer information, employer or union material, and practitioner review.

Where my own limits matter

I’m a working member of the live-event industry, but I’m not the final authority on every department, venue, piece of equipment, employer policy, local practice, or specialized role. I don’t want this site to pretend otherwise.

That limitation is part of the design. If the controlling answer belongs to an employer, venue, manufacturer, union, qualified technician, or another specialist, the material should say so clearly. If a practice varies from crew to crew, it should be presented as variable. If something still needs practitioner review, it should stay in review rather than being marketed as finished.

What this site can do

Build vocabulary, context, safety awareness, job-readiness habits, department awareness, and a clearer understanding of where a learner’s responsibility starts and stops.

What still happens on the job

Employer training, site rules, equipment-specific instruction, supervised practice, real-time crew direction, and any qualification or authorization required for the work.

What The Crew Blueprint is today

It is a growing training library. Stagehand Fundamentals is the foundation, Field Skills covers practical early-career topics, and the public department catalog currently has starter courses for Lighting, Audio, Video, Staging & Carpentry, and Backline / Props / Wardrobe. There is deeper research and draft material behind the scenes, but I would rather keep that material in review than promise a release before it is ready.

Built in public, reviewed carefully

The project will keep changing as the research improves, field feedback comes in, and course material earns a stronger review state.

Useful before complete

The goal is not to pretend the entire industry has been solved. It is to make the material that is ready genuinely useful and be honest about the rest.
