Course 01 · Free foundation

Crew Ready

How to become the person a crew can trust with an assignment: understand the plan, communicate clearly, protect equipment, respect boundaries, and hand the work off cleanly.

Lesson 1

Understand the shape of the day

A live event is not one task. It is a sequence of handoffs. Equipment arrives, work areas open, departments build, systems are checked, the show runs, and then everything has to come apart without losing control of people, equipment, or information.

What makes you useful

  • Know which phase the crew is in before you start acting.
  • Identify who is directing your assignment and where the completed task hands off.
  • Keep access paths, cases, tools, and work areas organized enough that the next person can work.
  • When priorities change, confirm the new priority instead of silently abandoning the old one.

What keeps you safer

Work conditions change fast. A route that was clear ten minutes ago may now contain moving equipment, temporary structures, vehicles, cable, cases, or another department’s work. Treat the environment as active, not fixed.

Takeaway: before moving, lifting, opening, connecting, disassembling, or relocating something, know whose task it is, what the expected result is, and what conditions could make the plan unsafe.
Lesson 2

Receive an assignment correctly

Reliable workers do not need every instruction repeated, but they also do not pretend they understood something they did not. A good assignment has four parts: the task, the result, the boundary, and the handoff.

  • Task: what you are being asked to do.
  • Result: what “done” should look like.
  • Boundary: what you should not change, operate, open, disconnect, or decide on your own.
  • Handoff: who needs to know when the task is complete or blocked.

If one of those is missing and it matters, ask a short question before committing to the work. That is not weakness. It is how crews prevent small misunderstandings from becoming damaged equipment, lost time, or injuries.

Useful habit: repeat back the part of an unfamiliar assignment that could be misunderstood: location, quantity, orientation, destination, or person receiving the handoff.
Lesson 3

Communicate like the work matters

Live production is noisy, crowded, time-sensitive, and distributed across departments. Good communication is brief, specific, and useful to the person receiving it.

Strong crew communication

  • Use names, positions, locations, or clear identifiers when ambiguity matters.
  • Report a problem with enough context to act on it: what, where, and whether work has stopped.
  • Do not bury a safety concern inside a long story. Lead with the concern.
  • When you are asked for status, answer the status first and the explanation second.
  • Do not use “done” if the task still needs inspection, testing, approval, or another department’s work.

A crew lead should not have to guess whether you completed a task, found a problem, left the area, or changed the plan.

Example: “Stage-right case line is clear. Two cases are held because their labels do not match the pack list. I stopped there and need a lead to confirm them.”
Lesson 4

Treat equipment as someone else’s responsibility too

Cases, cables, fixtures, speakers, screens, scenic pieces, tools, stands, hardware, controls, and accessories all belong to a larger system. Your job is not simply to move the object in front of you. It is to preserve its identity, condition, accessories, labeling, and ability to be used by the next person.

  • Read labels before assuming two similar-looking items are interchangeable.
  • Keep protective parts, caps, covers, cases, and accessories with the equipment they belong to.
  • Do not force connectors, latches, fasteners, moving parts, or cases that do not behave as expected.
  • If something appears damaged, mismatched, wet, overheated, altered, or otherwise abnormal, stop and route it to the responsible person.
  • Do not “fix” unfamiliar equipment because you want to be helpful.
What employers value: people who reduce preventable damage, preserve organization, and report problems early are more useful than people who move fast while creating rework.
Lesson 5

Know the difference between helping and taking authority

Many live-event tasks require specific training, supervision, employer authorization, qualified personnel, venue rules, or manufacturer procedures. Being near the task, seeing someone else do it, or understanding the concept does not give you that authority.

For a new worker, a strong response to unfamiliar or controlled work is simple: identify the responsible person, ask what support role is appropriate, and stay inside that role. This matters especially around electrical systems, rigging, work at height, powered equipment, moving machinery, structural systems, special effects, and other safety-critical work.

Boundary: this course does not teach you to perform controlled technical work. It teaches you to recognize when the work has moved beyond general assignment execution and into a task that needs the appropriate qualified or authorized person.
Lesson 6

Close the loop

Reliable workers finish more than the physical task. They finish the communication and organization around it.

  • Return tools, unused materials, packaging, and protective parts to the expected location.
  • Leave access paths and work areas usable unless directed otherwise.
  • Report missing, damaged, mismatched, or unresolved items.
  • Tell the person who assigned the task whether it is complete, blocked, or waiting for another step.
  • When leaving a work area, do not leave another person to discover your unfinished problem by accident.
The standard: the next person should be able to understand the state of the work without reconstructing what you did.
