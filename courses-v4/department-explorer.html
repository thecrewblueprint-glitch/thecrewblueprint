Department Explorer

Now that you have the foundation, compare what the major production departments actually do so you can choose what to learn and support next—without treating one title or management ladder as the only way forward.

1. Departments share habits, not identical jobs

Communication, equipment handling, documentation, troubleshooting, logistics, and safety boundaries transfer across production. The deeper technical work does not. Your best next step is the lane whose problems and responsibilities you want to understand more deeply.

2. Lighting

Lighting work combines fixture handling, position/orientation, control and data concepts, documentation, troubleshooting, show-file workflows, and interfaces with production power. As responsibility grows, technicians move from assigned support toward system reasoning, programming, documentation, and department coordination.

Safety boundary: production-power work and other electrical tasks remain subject to employer rules, applicable electrical requirements, and qualified-person/authorization boundaries.

3. Audio

Audio work centers on signal paths, sources and destinations, consoles, loudspeaker systems, networking/control, communication systems, troubleshooting, and careful listening. Experienced audio workers take increasing ownership of system state, documentation, coordination, and problem isolation.

4. Video, LED, camera & playback

Video work can include LED/display systems, signal transport, switching, cameras, playback/media, projection, networking, timing, and commissioning. The common thread is preserving the correct content/signal path and knowing what system state is expected.

5. Staging, scenic, shop & logistics

Staging/scenic work connects physical build systems, deck/platform elements, drawings, movement, materials, shop preparation, and show-change workflow. Shop/logistics roles form their own persistent lane around receiving, inventory, QC, truck flow, custody, and equipment lifecycle—not merely “work before the show.”

Boundary: structural decisions, machinery, specialized tools, lifting systems, and other controlled tasks require the appropriate employer procedures, training, supervision, and qualified personnel.

6. Leadership, stage management & production management

As responsibility increases, the work shifts from doing more physical tasks to owning more decisions and handoffs. Crew leads coordinate people and task execution. Stage management coordinates cues and run-of-show execution. Production managers integrate advance work, labor, vendors, schedules, documentation, changes, and stakeholders.

There is no requirement that every worker wants or should move toward management. Deep specialist work and lateral movement are valid growth paths.

Choose your next lane: ask which problems you want to become responsible for solving—not which title sounds highest.
