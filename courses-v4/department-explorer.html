Course 04 · Free foundation

Department Explorer

Walk through each department the way you will meet it on a call: what that department is responsible for, what the support hand actually does there, what stays with the department, the mistakes that cost the crew time, and the questions that keep you useful. Then use what you learned to pick the lane you want to learn next.

Lesson 1

How departments split the work

On a load-in you will hear a department head call for hands: “I need four for lighting,” “two with me on the PA,” “everybody else on decks.” You get sent to a department and you work for that department until the lead releases you. So you need to know more than which cases are theirs. You need to know which problems that department owns.

Every department owns a set of problems. Lighting owns how the stage is lit and the gear, power and data that make that happen. Audio owns the signal path and what the audience and performers hear. Video owns what shows up on the screens. Staging owns the structure people stand and perform on. Backline, props and wardrobe own things that belong to the show and the performers. The hand’s job is to help the department solve its problems without creating new ones.

The rule that carries across every department

Moving an assigned item is labor. Changing its setup, settings, contents, preset position or configuration is department work, unless the department lead gives you that task.

A hand who carries a case where it was asked to go is helping. A hand who opens that case and “organizes” it, straightens a fixture that looks crooked, or unplugs a cable that looks unused is changing something the department set on purpose.

Not every object carries the same risk

  • General production items: tables, chairs, empty carts, work lights, generic support cases.
  • Department gear: fixture cases, speaker cabinets, LED cabinets, deck components, cable trunks.
  • Show-critical presets: anything placed or set for a cue or a performance: a prop on a table in the wing, a set fixture focus, a set microphone position.
  • Performer-owned or performer-specific items: instruments, pedalboards, fitted costumes, personal gear.

The further down that list an item sits, the more clearly you need to be told before you touch it, move it, open it or change it.

How this course works: each department lesson covers what the department owns, what support looks like, what stays with the department, common mistakes, and the questions to ask. Exact procedures always come from the lead, the employer and the manufacturer for the gear in front of you.
Lesson 2

Lighting

What lighting owns: fixtures and where they hang, truss or electrics, power and data distribution to the fixtures, the lighting console and control network, and the focus: where each light points and how it looks.

What support looks like

  • Opening fixture cases and staging fixtures in the order the plot or the lead calls for.
  • Carrying fixtures by their handles, not by the yoke, lens barrel or cable.
  • Keeping clamps, safety cables, accessories and case lids with the right fixture.
  • Assisting with truss assembly under the lighting or rigging lead’s direction and keeping the hardware organized.
  • Running and dressing power and data cable along the route the department picked, then coiling it the way that department wants.
  • Keeping the area under truss clear when it moves, and watching for snags you can report.

What stays with the lighting department

Focus and optics (pan, tilt, shutters, lens, color), fixture addressing and modes, patching, console and network settings, feeder and power distribution, deciding where truss hangs, and running motors or a fly system. Once truss is overhead it is part of a safety-critical rigged system, and qualified people inspect and approve it.

Mistakes that cost the crew time

  • “Fixing” a fixture that looks crooked. It may be focused that way on purpose. If you did not set it, do not correct it. Ask the lighting lead.
  • Grabbing a fixture by the lens, yoke or shutters. That can knock out the focus or damage parts.
  • Touching lamps in older lamp-based fixtures with bare skin, which can damage the lamp.
  • Mixing cable types or lengths, or coiling against the department’s method.
Safety boundary: when truss or electrics move, one designated person calls the move. Stay clear of the moving load, and if communication breaks down, stop. Feeder power is dangerous and is connected by qualified personnel only. Do not energize, reconfigure or troubleshoot temporary power.

Ask: “Which case goes where?” · “Do you want this plugged in or just placed?” · “Is this cable clear to strike?”

Lesson 3

Audio

What audio owns: the signal path from microphones and instruments through consoles, processing and amplifiers to the loudspeakers and monitors, plus wireless frequencies, system tuning and how the show sounds. On many crews the person mixing is the A1 and the assist-level audio tech is the A2. The hands work alongside both.

What support looks like

  • Moving speaker cabinets, subs, wedges, amp racks and cable trunks, using the cabinet handles and team carries for heavy boxes.
  • Staging gear where the audio lead wants it and in the orientation they ask for.
  • Feeding cabinets into a line-array build: rolling them in, steadying them and passing hardware while the audio tech makes the connection.
  • Pulling cable along the department’s route and keeping connectors off wet or dirty floors.
  • Striking cable only after the audio lead says it is cleared, then coiling or packing it the way they want.

What stays with the audio department

Console settings of any kind, microphone and stand positions once set, patching, amplifier and processor settings, system tuning, wireless frequencies, and array angles and rigging hardware. Line-array hardware is specific to each manufacturer and model. Never improvise it.

Mistakes that cost the crew time

  • Pulling a cable because it “looks done.” It may still be patched, carrying signal or needed for a test.
  • Pulling on the cable instead of the connector body, or forcing a connector that will not seat.
  • Moving a microphone, stand or DI that the audio team already placed.
  • Using grilles, pins or connectors as handles on a cabinet.
Safety boundary: stay out from under a suspended or partly built array unless your assigned task requires it and the site procedure allows it. Sound checks and shows can be loud enough that OSHA’s noise standard (29 CFR 1910.95) requires employer hearing-conservation measures. Wear hearing protection when the site requires it, even though you are not the audio tech.

Ask: “Do you want this connected or just placed?” · “Which side faces the stage?” · “Is this line cleared to strike?”

Lesson 4

Video, LED and playback

What video owns: LED walls and projection, the processors that drive them, video signal routing and switching, cameras, media playback and what finally appears on the screens.

What support looks like

  • Moving cases, carts and dollies to the build line and unloading LED cabinets from them.
  • Carrying cabinets by the handles or grip points the LED lead shows you, and keeping hands and tools off the LED face.
  • Assisting with repetitive mechanical assembly after the lead demonstrates it on that exact product: bringing cabinets together under control, engaging locks the way they were shown.
  • Running power and data jumpers to the ports the lead identifies, fully seated, with the cable dressed so connectors are not carrying weight.
  • Striking cabinets in the lead’s sequence and putting each one into the case slot, cart or orientation built for that product.

What stays with the video department

Processor settings, pixel mapping, calibration, receiving cards and other internal components, configuration files, signal troubleshooting, power planning, the structure of a flown or ground-stacked wall, and figuring out why a panel is dark or the wrong color.

Mistakes that cost the crew time

  • Bumping or pressing the LED face or edges. Pixels break easily.
  • Letting magnet-assisted cabinets snap together, or forcing a lock before the cabinets are aligned.
  • Letting go of a cabinet during strike before someone else has its weight.
  • Stacking panels in an improvised pile. Some products must travel upright and others flat, so follow the case system for that product.
Safety boundary: LED walls are heavy, have pinch points between cabinets, and are often flown. Follow the exclusion zones and the lead’s calls. Do not memorize one wall and assume the next one works the same way. Learn to approach any wall carefully, then follow the procedure for the wall in front of you.

Ask: “Show me where to grab this one.” · “Which case slot does this go in?” · “Do you want me making the locks, or just holding?”

Lesson 5

Staging and scenic carpentry

What staging owns: decks, risers, stairs, rails, legs and bracing, scenic units, and the build sequence that turns a pile of parts into a structure people stand, walk and perform on.

What support looks like

  • Moving and staging deck, legs, braces and stair units where the lead wants them, using team carries and calling your moves.
  • Assembling only the system you have been shown and assigned, in the lead’s layout and sequence.
  • Protecting finished and painted scenic surfaces while you carry and set.
  • Watching fingers and feet at pinch and crush points between decks, legs and carts.
  • Noticing what looks wrong (a missing brace, a missing rail, an unlevel section, a connection that did not lock) and reporting it.

What stays with the staging department

The stage layout, load capacity, whether bracing or rails can be left off, hardware substitutions, cutting, drilling or modifying structural parts, leveling workarounds, and approving the finished stage for use. Flying scenery or stage elements takes separate training and authorization.

Mistakes that cost the crew time

  • Assuming one staging system goes together like another. Leg systems, locks and brace geometry differ between products.
  • Swapping in hardware that “fits” because the right part is not handy.
  • Skipping a brace because the deck “feels solid.”
  • Dragging scenic pieces across the floor and damaging the finish.
Safety boundary: a stage that looks finished can still be missing a leg, brace or rail. If the build does not match what you were shown, stop and tell the staging lead. Structural decisions belong to the people responsible for the structure.

Ask: “Is this the same system we built yesterday?” · “Where does this brace go?” · “This leg will not lock. Do you want to look at it?”

Lesson 6

Backline, props and wardrobe

What these departments own: the things that belong to the show and the performers. Backline techs look after instruments, amps, pedalboards, keyboards and drums, often set up to an artist’s exact preferences. Props run every object a performer handles, and a prop’s spot in the wing can be part of a cue. Wardrobe handles costumes, fit, repairs, laundry and quick changes.

What support looks like

  • Moving closed backline cases, rolling risers, prop trunks and wardrobe racks where the department lead directs.
  • Carrying the case rather than the item inside it, unless you are told otherwise.
  • Placing props on the correct side of the stage and in the wing the props lead names.
  • Keeping drinks, tools, tape and dirty gloves off instrument cases, prop tables and wardrobe racks.

What stays with these departments

Tuning and amp, pedal, keyboard or drum settings. Moving an instrument once it is placed. Changing a prop preset. Opening wardrobe trunks or garment bags. Cleaning, steaming, repairing or rearranging costumes. Handling a performer’s personal items without permission.

Mistakes that cost the crew time

  • “Cleaning up” a prop table or a wing. An item sitting alone may be exactly where it needs to be for the next cue.
  • Assuming two similar props or two similar costume pieces are interchangeable.
  • Putting costumes on the floor, or crushing garment bags under cases.
  • Bumping a tuned instrument or a set pedalboard while moving past it.
Permission before intervention: on these departments, being quick to ask and move is valuable. Deciding, rearranging or “improving” things on your own is not.

Ask: “Is this preset, or can I move it?” · “Do you want the case moved, or the item itself?” · “I have not handled this before. Show me where to grab it.”

Lesson 7

Who runs the call

You will take direction from different people during one day. Knowing who owns what tells you where to bring a question or a problem.

  • Crew chief or crew lead: assigns the hands, keeps people moving and is usually your first stop for “where do you want me?”
  • Department head or department lead (head electrician, audio crew chief, LED lead, head carpenter, backline tech, props head, wardrobe supervisor): owns that department’s work and tells you how it gets done.
  • Stage manager: runs rehearsals and performances, calls the cues and keeps the show record. Stage management is its own career, separate from the technical departments. It works alongside them, not above them.
  • Production manager: ties the whole production together: schedule, labor, vendors, the advance, budget and changes. People reach this role from stage management, technical departments, touring and venue work.
  • Shop and warehouse leads: own prep, QC, inventory and returns. Course 3 covered this lane. It is a career in its own right, not just the work that happens before the show.

Two ways to grow

Some people go deeper in one craft: hand, then technician, then department lead. Leading a department usually takes deep skill in that craft. Others build broad production and communication skill and move into coordination and production management across departments. Both are real paths. Nobody has to move toward management.

On the call: a safety concern goes to whoever is closest and responsible right now. A question about how the work gets done goes to the department lead you were assigned to.
Lesson 8

Choosing what to learn next

Pick a department by the problems you want to solve, not by the job title that sounds most advanced.

  • If you notice how a stage looks and like working with fixtures, cable, power and control, look at lighting.
  • If you care about how things sound and like following a signal from the source to the speaker, look at audio.
  • If you like screens, signal routing, cameras and careful physical builds, look at video and LED.
  • If you like building things with your hands and reading drawings, look at staging and scenic carpentry.
  • If you like show-specific care, performers and running a show from backstage, look at backline, props or wardrobe.
  • If you like order, inventory and getting the right gear to the right truck, look at shop and logistics.
  • If you are the person who keeps track of everything and everyone, look at stage management or production coordination.

What to do on your next calls

  • When the crew chief asks for hands, volunteer for the department you want to learn.
  • Watch what the department lead checks before calling something done.
  • Ask one good question per call, at a moment when the lead is not under pressure.
  • Keep notes on the gear, words and procedures you saw. Every venue and every product teaches you something.
What this course does and does not give you: passing it means you understand what departments do and where support work stops, well enough to choose what to learn next. It is not field experience, a qualification, a certification or authorization to do department work. Those come from training, supervised practice and your employer.
